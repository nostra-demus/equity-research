# Moat — MGM

## 1. Named Competitors

(Inherited from `08_competitive-map.md`.)

- **Competitor A — Caesars Entertainment, Inc. (NasdaqGS: CZR):** MGM's most direct Las Vegas Strip rival by property count and convention footprint; also competes in regional US markets and online gaming. Highly leveraged (~7.0x EBITDA); loss-making at the net income line in LTM.
- **Competitor B — Las Vegas Sands Corp. (NYSE: LVS):** MGM's primary Macau competitor (one of six concessionaires); no longer operates on the Las Vegas Strip. Lower leverage (3.2x EBITDA); highest consolidated EBITDA margins in the comp set (35.2% LTM); owns its real estate in Macau and Singapore.
- **Competitor C — Wynn Resorts, Limited (NasdaqGS: WYNN):** MGM's closest luxury peer on the Las Vegas Strip and also a fellow Macau concessionaire. Two Las Vegas properties targeting ultra-premium gaming customers; concentrated portfolio; 6.7x EBITDA leverage; 24.1% EBITDA margin LTM.

## 2. Moat Sources

|---|---|---|---:|
| Brand | Y — partial | Bellagio and Aria are genuine premium brands commanding ADR of $249 on the Las Vegas Strip and driving customer preference among premium-mass table players. MGM Rewards (multi-million member base) creates repeat-visit incentives. However, below those two flagship properties the MGM portfolio (Luxor, Excalibur, New York-New York) is mass-market with weak brand differentiation vs. Caesars' comparable-tier properties. LeoVegas and BetMGM are recognized but not dominant digital brands. Brand moat is real but narrow and concentrated in two properties. MGM's EBITDA margin (13.8% consolidated; 33.9% Strip segment EBITDAR) is broadly comparable to Wynn's Strip-level margins and below LVS's 35.2% consolidated. The $2.3B annual triple-net rent to VICI is a fixed cost that competitors who own their real estate (LVS, Wynn for some properties) do not carry. No disclosed procurement scale advantage. [FY2025 10-K, Item 7 MD&A, p.39; Capital IQ Comparable Analysis, Operating Statistics, data as of 2026-07-03] | 10 |
| Distribution | Y — partial | MGM operates 16 domestic casino properties across 9 states plus two Macau properties, giving geographic breadth that smaller luxury operators (Wynn) lack. [FY2025 10-K, Item 2, Properties, p.30; Item 7 MD&A, p.37 — convention calendar as a structural mid-week revenue driver] | 38 |
| Scale | Y — partial | MGM is the largest single operator on the Las Vegas Strip by room count (37,000 rooms) and casino square footage (1,054,000 sq ft), operating more Strip rooms than any individual competitor. This scale creates capacity advantages in booking major entertainment events, conventions, and high-end gaming tournaments. In Macau, MGM China is mid-tier by scale (2,013 rooms vs. Sands China's substantially larger footprint). Scale on the Strip is real; in Macau it is not a differentiator. [FY2025 10-K, Item 2, Properties, p.30 — property table] | 40 |
| Technology / IP | N | No patent portfolio or proprietary technology moat identified in the filings. MGM's digital operations (LeoVegas, BetMGM) use licensed or jointly-developed technology. The 10-K risk factors explicitly warn that competitors could replicate technology with lower development effort. MGM Rewards is a data-rich loyalty platform but is not defensible intellectual property — Caesars has an equivalent (Caesars Rewards) and tracks similar customer data. [FY2025 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors, p.20 — IP risk language; FY2025 10-K, Item 1, p.3 — BetMGM joint venture with Entain technology] | 8 |
| Licenses / regulation | Y — meaningful | Gaming concessions and licenses create high barriers to entry. In Macau, the government capped the market at exactly six concessionaires (January 2023 renewal); no new operator can legally enter. In Nevada and other US states, gaming licenses require multi-year regulatory background investigations and financial suitability review, making rapid new entry impossible. The Las Vegas Strip's finite physical geography (4.2-mile stretch of land) is effectively fully occupied — a new casino requires either buying an existing property or acquiring one of the few remaining undeveloped parcels. These regulatory and physical barriers prevent new competitive threats from materializing quickly. The barrier protects the entire incumbent set, not MGM specifically vs. Caesars — but they do defend MGM's profits against outside entry. [FY2025 10-K, Item 1, pp.7–9 "Regulation and Licensing"; FY2025 10-K, Item 1, p.3 — Macau six-concessionaire structure; 08_competitive-map.md, Section 4] | 62 |
| Network effects | N | No evidence of network effects in any meaningful sense. A larger casino does not become more valuable to a patron because more other patrons are present. The loyalty program (MGM Rewards) has tier-credit accumulation benefits but these do not scale with the size of the member base — they are loyalty incentives, not true network effects. Online gaming (BetMGM) has some liquidity effects in poker but this is a minor sub-segment. [Not proven from available data] | 5 |
| Switching costs | Y — very narrow | High-limit table players with established credit lines (markers), dedicated casino hosts, and long-standing relationships with Bellagio or Aria have meaningful switching costs: a $500k–$2M credit relationship requires rebuilding trust, credit history, and host relationships at a new property. The 10-K notes that a "significant portion of operating income is generated from high-end gaming customers" and that losing key customers could "materially adversely affect" results. However, for the majority of MGM customers — mass-market slot players, occasional visitors, regional casino patrons — switching costs are near zero. Switching costs are material for a very small high-value segment only. [FY2025 10-K, Item 1A, p.17 — high-end customer dependency; Item 7 MD&A, p.34 — high-end customer variability] | 28 |
| Natural resource access | N | MGM does not control any natural resources. Physical land on the Las Vegas Strip is a scarce input but MGM leases rather than owns most of its Strip real estate (VICI Properties owns the land and buildings). MGM China holds gaming rights but these are concession-based (licenses), addressed in the Licenses row. [Not proven from available data] | 0 |
| Location advantage | Y — meaningful | The Las Vegas Strip concentration gives MGM real location-based pricing power within the Strip micro-market. Bellagio and Aria sit at the premium end of the Strip's geographic hierarchy (the "center Strip" area between the Flamingo and the Wynn corridors), adjacent to the highest-traffic retail and entertainment nodes. In regional markets, several MGM properties operate under state-limited license structures (e.g., MGM National Harbor in Maryland — one of only a few commercial casinos in the Washington D.C. suburban market), giving a near-monopoly on drive-in gaming catchment. [FY2025 10-K, Item 2, Properties, p.30; competitive-map Section 4 — regional markets described as "local monopoly conditions"] | 52 |

**Summary:** Two genuine, evidence-supported moat sources — Licenses/regulation (defending the franchise against new entry) and Location advantage (Las Vegas Strip center positioning and regional near-monopoly licenses). Brand (Bellagio/Aria) and Scale (Strip room count) provide secondary support. No technology, natural resource, network effect, or meaningful cost moat exists.

**Notes on MGM ROIC computation.** EBIT of $1,002M (FY2025, from 10-K Item 7 MD&A, p.36). Normalized tax rate of 20%: the FY2025 effective rate computes to ~20.2% (pretax income ~$653M, implied provision ~$132M), which matches the normalized structural rate applied here — no unusual one-off non-deductible distortions were identified that would require stripping. NOPAT = $1,002M × (1 − 20%) = $801M. Invested capital (ex-lease ROU basis): PP&E net $6,306M + Goodwill $4,902M + Intangibles net $1,357M + Net Working Capital $820M − Cash $2,063M = $11,322M. ROIC FY2025 = $801M / $11,322M = **7.1%**. Through-cycle (FY2023–FY2025 average EBIT $1,448M, NOPAT $1,158M, same IC base): **10.2%**. Gross-capital basis (including full lease obligations as capital deployed — total debt + lease liabilities + equity − cash = $32.4B): ROIC drops to 2.5% (FY2025) / 3.6% (through-cycle), showing how the entire leaseback structure is capital-consuming when economic substance is considered.

**The economic moat test (required).** A moat must produce a return on capital above the cost of capital. Peer-superiority alone is not a moat.

> Return on capital **above** cost of capital on the ex-lease IC basis: **10.2% through-cycle ROIC** (FY2023–FY2025 average, ex-lease invested capital) vs **~9–10% estimated WACC** (CAPM inference: risk-free rate 4.3% + beta 1.3 × ERP 5.5% = 11.5% cost of equity; after-tax cost of debt 4.6% at 5.7% avg fixed rate × 80%; blended at ~22% equity weight / ~78% debt+lease weight ≈ 6.1% — Inference, not from filings) — gap is narrow and **regime-dependent**: on the gross capital basis (lease obligations included in invested capital), through-cycle ROIC of 3.6% is materially below even the lowest WACC estimate. The economic moat test passes marginally on a post-leaseback ex-lease IC basis but fails if the economic substance of the $25B VICI lease obligation is treated as invested capital.

**Important qualifications:**

1. **Ex-lease vs. gross-capital divergence.** MGM sold its Las Vegas Strip real estate to VICI Properties in 2022 and now pays ~$2.3B/year in triple-net rent. Stripping the lease liabilities from invested capital makes the denominator appear smaller and ROIC higher. On a gross-capital basis that recognizes the $25B lease obligation as economically equivalent to debt secured by those properties, through-cycle ROIC is 3.6% — materially below the cost of capital. Both views are presented; the ex-lease view is what management implicitly uses; the gross-capital view is more conservative and reflects the economic substance of the arrangement.

2. **FY2025 as a weak year.** Business quality (`07`) scored cyclicality at 32/100 (high cyclicality). FY2025 Strip revenues fell 4% on 8% visitor volume decline. The through-cycle (FY2023–2025) average ROIC of 10.2% is used in preference to the single-year FY2025 figure of 7.1%.

3. **Management does not disclose ROIC or ROCE.** The above is a computed figure, not a management-disclosed one. No reconciliation is required against a management headline return because none exists.

## 4. Where The Company Sits

**Relative to peers:** Company sits **at the median to bottom of named peers on EBIT margin** — MGM's FY2025 EBIT margin of 5.7% (consolidated GAAP) trails LVS (24.5% LTM) and Wynn (15.7% LTM) materially, and is below Caesars (18.1% LTM). This reversal is entirely explained by MGM's $2.3B annual rent charge sitting in "Corporate and other" (reducing consolidated EBITDA by ~$2.3B before arriving at EBIT), which does not affect Caesars (also a leaseholder to VICI) proportionally in the same way, and does not affect LVS or Wynn (property owners). On a segment EBITDAR basis (before rent), MGM Strip is 33.9% — comparable to or slightly below Wynn's Strip-level performance and below LVS's integrated resort margins.

**Absolute (the economic moat test):** On the ex-lease post-leaseback capital base, the company earns a return on capital **marginally above** its cost of capital (10.2% through-cycle ROIC vs. ~9–10% estimated WACC — Inference, not from filings), but on the gross-capital basis that includes lease obligations as invested capital, the company earns a return **below** its cost of capital (3.6% vs. ~6–10% cost). The answer to the economic moat test is therefore **regime-dependent and narrow**; the moat does not survive scrutiny under the more economically honest gross-capital treatment.

## 5. Moat Verdict

**Narrow moat** — some advantage exists, but it is limited in scope, regime-dependent in economics, and under medium-term pressure.

The strongest moat sources are the oligopolistic market structure of the Las Vegas Strip (finite physical land, high-cost entry barrier, government licensing requirements) and the Macau government-administered six-concessionaire oligopoly, which together make new competitive threats from outside the current incumbent set effectively impossible over any normal investment horizon. Within the incumbent set — where the real competition actually occurs — MGM's advantages are real but narrow: the Bellagio and Aria brands command genuine pricing power for premium-mass table gaming customers, and the convention capacity at center-Strip properties creates structural group-booking advantages. These translate into through-cycle ROIC marginally above cost of capital on a post-leaseback capital basis, but the advantage evaporates when lease obligations are treated as invested capital.

The durability test this moat must pass over the next 5 years: (1) the Macau gaming concession (25% of revenue) expires December 2032 — renewal on unfavorable terms or with new local-content obligations would directly compress returns; (2) the VICI master lease requires ~$2.3B/year in rent regardless of revenue, and a sustained consumer spending downturn would expose the operating leverage this creates; (3) iGaming and online sports betting must not structurally erode the regional casino volume base faster than BetMGM grows to replace it. If any of these three tests fail simultaneously, the narrow moat disappears.

**CLAUSE.md §24, Filter 5 check (fast-changing industry):** `07_business-quality` scored industry rate-of-change at 60/100 — moderate. The physical Strip business is slow-changing; the digital and Macau components are faster-changing. This does not trigger the automatic cap (score ≤40), but does require discounting the durability of the digital moat component in line with the module rules.

**Moat trajectory — eroding.** The moat's direction over the last 3–5 years is eroding on three measures: (a) return on capital is declining — FY2023 EBIT of $1,891M > FY2024 EBIT $1,450M > FY2025 EBIT $1,002M on rising revenue, indicating cost structure deterioration under fixed rent and rising D&A; (b) Las Vegas Strip ADR fell from $260 (FY2024) to $249 (FY2025), a 4.2% decline, suggesting weakening pricing power even at the premium end; (c) the Macau concession clock is ticking — with expiry in December 2032, every passing year reduces the certainty-weighted value of the China segment and creates market-discount pressure. The entry barrier (licenses/location) has not changed, but the return above cost of capital is compressing year by year. An eroding moat at this trajectory feeds the permanent-impairment / declining-perpetuity risk trigger in downstream valuation modules (CLAUDE.md §24, Filter 5).
