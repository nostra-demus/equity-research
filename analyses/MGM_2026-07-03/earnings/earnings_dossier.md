# Earnings Module — MGM (Synthesis)

## Abstract

MGM's dominant earnings trend is decelerating: Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA (the profit measure before interest, taxes, and depreciation) has fallen year-over-year in four of the last five comparable quarters, with the LTM figure 1.7% below FY2024 despite revenue growing 2.8%. The biggest driver of this pressure is the Las Vegas Strip's fixed-cost trap — approximately $1.87 billion in annual rent owed to VICI Properties escalates regardless of revenue, so the 6.5% RevPAR (revenue per available room) decline in FY2025 translated directly into an 8% segment profit decline. The consensus bar has been cut 33% from 18 months ago (FY2026 normalized EPS from $2.90 to $1.95) and sits at "fair" rather than depressed, meaning a beat requires operational improvement rather than a low hurdle. The single biggest near-term risk is that the apparent Las Vegas Strip gaming recovery in April-May 2026 was driven by statistical hold variance rather than genuine visitor volume growth, while Macau June 2026 GGR fell 12.1% year-over-year, removing the segment that has been offsetting Strip weakness all year. The verdict is mixed earnings setup: partial signs of stabilization are real, but the structural cost trajectory and critical red flags prevent any confident inflection call.

## 5. Earnings Setup Summary

### Revenue Setup

The 2022–2023 post-COVID revenue surge is finished. MGM's revenue grew 1.7% in FY2025 and 4.2% in Q1 2026 year-over-year — the latter entirely driven by Macau volume recovery and MGM Digital growth, not the Las Vegas Strip. The current revenue trajectory is not sustainable as a growth narrative: it depends on Macau's post-COVID recovery continuing at roughly the same pace while the Strip stabilizes, but Macau June 2026 GGR already fell 12.1% year-over-year with World Cup demand diversion as the stated cause. The single factor that would flip revenue direction upward is a sustained Las Vegas leisure recovery — specifically occupancy returning above 93% from its current 92% — driven by either a reversal of the Canadian visitor decline (geopolitically driven and outside management's control) or a US consumer re-engagement with discretionary travel. On the downside, the reported and organic revenue stories are nearly identical: there are no material M&A effects in FY2025 (Northfield Park sale closed April 2026 and removes approximately $400M from Regional from Q2 2026 onwards) and no FX effects (MOP/HKD pegged to USD). The gap between headline and underlying is therefore small, but the Northfield divestiture will create a structural regional revenue gap in FY2026 comparisons that consensus may not fully reflect.

### Margin Setup

The current Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA margin of 13.8% (FY2025) is not a cyclical peak — it has already compressed 20 basis points from FY2024 and 60 basis points from FY2023. But it is also not a trough, because the structural rent escalation (up to 2% per year) means that even if revenue flatlines, EBITDA will compress annually. The segment that would inflict the largest single bite on consolidated margins if it moves adversely by 10–20% is Las Vegas Strip, where revenue declined 4% in FY2025 and EBITDAR fell 8% — an approximately 85% incremental flow-through rate. Management has no contractual pass-through mechanism on costs: rent escalates on a fixed schedule, union contract wages cannot be reduced, and gaming taxes are a fixed percentage of win. The company is a price-taker on its largest fixed costs. The only protection is operating leverage on the upside — every incremental Strip revenue dollar converts at very high rates — but that lever requires revenue growth, which has been absent.

### Quality Check

The single largest gap between reported and economic earnings is the $1.87 billion in annual cash rent paid to VICI that does not appear in Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA. This is fully disclosed and is not a manipulation — but it means the $2,426M FY2025 Adj EBITDA is approximately 77% pre-committed to landlords before capex, debt service, or equity returns. A normalized "EBITDA after rent" figure for FY2025 is approximately $556M, not $2,426M. The FY2025 GAAP net income of $206M is also unrepeatable — a -85.5% effective tax rate driven by a non-recurring valuation allowance release, IRS refund, and Macau tax exemption will not recur in FY2026. The property transaction add-back has appeared every year for three consecutive years (a gain in FY2023, charges in FY2024 and FY2025), so calling it non-recurring systematically overstates Adj EBITDA. For modeling normalized earnings next year, the correct starting point is Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA (not GAAP) because GAAP is too distorted by the FX loss, D&A step-up, and goodwill impairment — but the analyst must then deduct the full annual rent of approximately $1.87–2.3B to arrive at an economically meaningful figure.

### Consensus Bar

For the consensus bar to be beaten materially, Las Vegas Strip gaming revenues would need to sustain the April-May 2026 momentum (Strip win up 6.6% and 13.2% year-over-year), the Q1 2026 self-insurance charges would need to not recur, and Macau volume growth would need to absorb the brand-fee headwind. The bar is most likely set incorrectly on the upside for FY2026 EPS — not because the revenue bar is too high (revenue estimates have actually drifted up $162M over six months) but because the cost trajectory is worse than the consensus embeds: gross margin has fallen 490 basis points since FY2022 on a cost base growing faster than revenue. The clearest anchoring error in current consensus is the assumption that the FY2025 GAAP tax benefit (-85.5%) will normalize without a large reset in FY2026 GAAP EPS. Approximately 50–60% of current FY2026 consensus is anchored to the convention calendar recovery (a near-term event) and the Macau visitor-arrivals tailwind (whose momentum has visibly slowed in June 2026).

## 6. Key Trend Summary

Revenue growth has clearly decelerated: from +38.9% in FY2022 (Macau reopening + post-COVID Las Vegas surge) to +23.1% in FY2023 to +6.7% in FY2024 and +1.7% in FY2025. Revenue growth has effectively stalled as the post-COVID catch-up faded, with the most recent quarter (Q1 2026) showing +4.2% YoY — a mild improvement but still well below the FY2022–FY2023 pace.

Margins are compressing at both the gross and EBITDA level. Gross margin has declined from 49.3% in FY2022 to 44.4% in FY2025, a 490-basis-point drop over three years. Company Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA margin has narrowed from 14.4% in FY2023 to 13.8% in FY2025 and 13.4% on an LTM basis. The quarterly trend is more alarming: EBITDA margin has fallen in every year-over-year comparison from Q4 2024 through Q1 2026 except Q4 2025 (which bounced partly due to strong Las Vegas holiday demand). The compression reflects fixed-cost growth (triple-net lease rent, labor) against slower revenue.

There is mild seasonality. Q4 is the highest-revenue quarter (averaging 26.2% of annual revenue), and Q1 is the highest-margin quarter. Q3 is the weakest quarter on margins, and this weakness has intensified in FY2025. Seasonal patterns are not extreme enough to materially complicate period comparisons.

Two inflection points stand out. First, FY2022 is an accounting inflection rather than an operating one: the VICI sale-leaseback created a massive non-cash D&A charge (approximately $2.7B) and an enormous operating lease liability (~$25B), turning reported EBIT deeply negative and making strict net debt look low (~$2.8B) — but replacing financial debt with approximately $2.3B in annual fixed rent obligations. Second, Q3 2025 is a genuine earnings inflection: the $278.9M Empire City goodwill impairment and simultaneous LV Strip RevPAR decline of 7% YoY caused EBITDA margin to hit a three-year low of 11.3%. Q4 2025 partially recovered, and Q1 2026 sequential improvement is encouraging, but the underlying EBITDA trend (Q1 2026 company adj EBITDA -8.9% YoY) has not yet turned.

## 7. The Single Biggest Revenue Driver

The single biggest driver of MGM revenue is **Las Vegas Strip RevPAR** — the product of occupancy and average daily rate across the company's 9 Las Vegas Strip properties. The LV Strip is 48.1% of consolidated revenue and 54.7% of positive-segment EBITDAR. A 10% change in LV Strip RevPAR (i.e., $23 per room from the current $238 level) flows directly to approximately $840M in revenue — roughly 4.8% of total consolidated revenue — before any gaming or F&B effects.

The current direction is deteriorating. RevPAR was $245 in FY2024 and fell to $229 in FY2025 (-6.5%); Q1 2026 RevPAR was $238 vs $242 a year earlier (-1.7%). The decline is entirely occupancy-driven (92% now vs 94% a year ago) rather than ADR compression ($257 flat), which means room pricing power is holding but visitor volumes are down. The risk: a US consumer spending contraction or a further softening in leisure and convention demand could push occupancy below 90% — a level not seen since post-COVID recovery — while ADR pricing power would likely follow if occupancy falls materially. The 30–40% decline in Canadian visitor volumes already represents a real demand headwind visible in Q1 2026 table drop data. If LV Strip gaming volumes (table drop currently -3.4% YoY) also contract alongside hotel occupancy, the revenue impact to the segment would be compounded beyond what RevPAR alone captures.

**Pass-through lag:** MGM does not pass through cost increases via contractual revenue escalators. Labor wage increases from union renewals must be absorbed or offset by operating efficiency. Energy cost increases hit the P&L directly. The VICI lease rent escalates regardless of revenue. There is no pass-through mechanism on the cost side — cost increases either compress margins or are offset by volume/price-driven revenue growth. Given the fixed-cost nature of the VICI rent ($1.87B/year), the most effective offset to any cost increase is revenue growth at existing fixed cost base (operating leverage), which has been absent at Las Vegas Strip since FY2024.

## 4. Margin Walk — Which Margin Level Matters Most?

For MGM, **Segment Adjusted EBITDAR margin** at the property level and **Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA margin** at the company level are the most decision-relevant metrics. Here is why.

MGM does not own its US real estate — VICI does. The triple-net lease converts a capital asset into a fixed annual rent obligation of ~$1.87B. Gross margin captures the property-level operational performance before this structural obligation and before D&A (which is economically non-representative because the lease ROU amortization is an accounting consequence of a sale-leaseback transaction, not capital consumption). EBIT and net income are further distorted by: (1) the $278.9M goodwill impairment in FY2025; (2) large FX swings from USD-denominated MGM China debt; (3) income-tax benefits from deferred tax assets. None of these reflects the underlying cash-generating power of the operating properties.

Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA of $2,426M in FY2025 (13.8% margin) is the figure that, after netting capital expenditure ($1,069M), produces free cash flow and determines debt capacity. It is also what the company itself guides toward and what sell-side consensus tracks.

EBIT and gross margin remain useful as directional indicators of the fixed-cost absorption story: if COGS rises faster than revenue (which it has every year since FY2022), gross margin compression tells you the operating cost trend before rent and D&A.

## 8. The Single Biggest Margin Driver

**Las Vegas Strip operating leverage under fixed rent.**

The Triple-net lease rent to VICI ($1.87B/year, rising annually) is the structural constraint on MGM's margin recovery. But it is not itself a "driver" — it is fixed. What makes it the central margin variable is the operating leverage it creates: every dollar of Las Vegas Strip revenue growth above the zero line converts at an extremely high rate to EBITDA (roughly 80–85% flow-through on incremental Strip revenue, based on the FY2024–2025 reverse), while every dollar of revenue decline below flat destroys EBITDA at the same rate.

The Las Vegas Strip ($8.4B revenue, 48% of total) is currently in a softening cycle — visitor volume fell 8% in 2025, RevPAR fell 7%, and revenue fell 4% despite a flat overall hospitality market. The business has partially stabilized in Q1 2026 (LV revenue grew YoY for the first time in six quarters, driven by convention calendar and the return of MGM Grand room inventory). But the midweek value-customer segment remains soft, Canadian visitors are down 30–40%, and international first-time visitors are at record lows.

If Las Vegas Strip revenue decelerates again or contracts from here — due to a US consumer slowdown, further decline in Canadian/international inbound, or convention cancellations — the fixed rent structure means EBITDA will compress sharply. Conversely, if the convention calendar holds and leisure stabilizes, the operating leverage means a modest 3–4% Strip revenue recovery could deliver outsized EBITDA uplift.

The current direction is a tentative partial recovery, but it is fragile: the short booking cycle (management's own word) means forward visibility is limited, and any macro shock (rising gasoline prices reducing drive-to-Vegas traffic, a recession, geopolitical disruption to international travel) could reverse the Q1 2026 momentum quickly.

## 6. Seasonality Read

Q2 (April–June) averages 24.8% of MGM annual revenue — the second-strongest quarter by revenue, roughly equal to Q1. Q2 EBITDA margins have historically been 14.1%–15.4% on the CIQ basis, the second-best margin quarter behind Q1 and clearly above Q3 (which averages 11.3%–14.2%). This means Q2 is a seasonally reasonable quarter for margins — not peak (that is Q1) and not the weakest (that is Q3). The convention calendar is the key Q2 driver for Las Vegas: CEO commentary from Q1 2026 call cited "Google, Cisco, and other groups booked for summer" with convention room-night mix expected at 20% for Q2, up 2 percentage points year-over-year. This is a mild seasonal tailwind. For Macau, Q2 does not include Chinese New Year (Q1 benefit) and June is typically softer due to the absence of Chinese holiday-driven tourism — and in 2026, World Cup-related demand diversion compounds this June softness. On balance, seasonality helps more than it hurts for the Q2 LV setup but is a headwind for the MGM China Q2 exit rate (June data already confirmed -12.1% YoY GGR).

---

## 7. Historical Pattern

MGM has beaten EPS normalized estimates in 3 of the last 5 reported quarters (FQ1 2025, FQ2 2025, FQ4 2025 — all large beats) and missed in 2 (FQ3 2025 on hold, FQ1 2026 on self-insurance). The beat pattern is systematically inflated by: (1) table hold favorable variance, which can add $0.10–$0.20/share per quarter; and (2) a conservatively-set EPS bar (consensus has been revised down 33% from 18 months ago, to $1.95 for FY2026 vs $2.90 prior). Revenue beats have been more consistent — 3 of 4 quarters where actuals are available. The synthesizer should weight the historical pattern modestly: the beat tendency is real at the revenue level, but EPS beats are hold-driven noise roughly half the time, and the FQ1 2026 miss shows that unexpected cost items (self-insurance) can override underlying operational improvement. Given that the bar has been reset lower following large cuts, the set-up for a revenue beat is present, but EPS is inherently random due to hold.

---

## 8. Setup Verdict

**Setup is balanced** — the weight of evidence neither clearly favors a beat nor a miss.

The single most important factor in the verdict is the Las Vegas Strip gaming win trend: May 2026 Strip GGR was up 13.2% year-over-year (NGCB data, unverified web source) and April was +6.6%, against a weak Q2 2025 comparative that included a negative hold quarter. If those gains reflect genuine volume recovery — convention fill driving mid-week demand, MGM Grand rooms restored — Q2 revenue beats are achievable. However, the biggest risk that could flip this to a miss is Macau June GGR weakness (-12.1% YoY), which captures the last month of Q2 for MGM China and could offset the LV Strip tailwind, particularly if combined with another hold-negative outcome at the LV table games level.

---

## 9. Second-Quarter Look-Ahead

FQ3 2026 (July–September) presents a materially harder setup than FQ2. Q3 is structurally MGM's weakest margin quarter (11.3%–14.2% EBITDA margins historically), and 2025's Q3 already included the $278.9M goodwill impairment. The year-over-year EBITDA comparison will be easier on that specific impairment, but Macau GGR entered July under World Cup pressure (June -12.1% YoY), US leisure demand at Las Vegas in the summer months is the segment most exposed to high-income consumer caution, and Q3 2025 Strip margins were already compressing. If BetMGM (50% JV) is on track toward $300–350M full-year EBITDA, Q3 should see incremental affiliate income, but this is small relative to core property economics. The Q3 2026 setup looks weaker than Q2, primarily because Macau momentum entering July is negative and summer LV leisure demand visibility is low with a short booking cycle.

---

## 10. Pre-Mortem

If this earnings setup fails — meaning Q2 is a clear operational miss rather than a balanced outcome — the most likely reason is that the Las Vegas Strip gaming win improvement in April–May was predominantly hold-driven rather than volume-driven, meaning the underlying occupancy and gaming traffic trends never actually inflected. Combined with a larger-than-expected Macau revenue impact from World Cup demand diversion in June, and without the one-time self-insurance tailwind proving as clean as management suggested, the operating improvement story from Q1 call commentary would have been based on two quarters of noise (favorable hold, convention timing) rather than a genuine demand inflection in the Strip leisure business.

## 2. Cash Conversion Assessment

CFO has tracked Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA closely over the three years where both measures are available on a comparable basis. CFO/EBITDA has been 98–115% in FY2023–FY2025, well above the 70% threshold for a healthy conversion ratio. This is partly a function of the EBITDA definition itself: the company's Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA already strips out property transactions, impairments, and preopening costs, so the bridge to CFO is unusually clean. The elevated ratios (above 100%) in FY2023 and FY2025 reflect working capital releases and, in FY2025, the one-off tax refund.

The key structural caveat: CFO includes roughly $509M (FY2025) of non-cash operating lease amortization that passes through the operating section under ASC 842, while the actual cash rent of approximately $1.87 billion per year flows through financing activities. This understates the true cash cost of the business. Viewed on an economic basis — where rent paid is a real operating cost — the CFO-to-EBITDA conversion overstates the proportion of EBITDA that converts to cash available to equity holders.

The trajectory on ordinary FCF (CFO minus capex) is stable at $1.2–1.5 billion per year, though it is being pinched from both directions: the VICI rent escalator and rising capex for the Osaka development.

## 10. The Single Biggest Quality Concern

The biggest risk that reported earnings overstate economic reality is the structural exclusion of approximately $1.87 billion in annual cash rent payments from the Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA metric, combined with the sharp D&A growth that is expanding the gap between the metric and cash available to equity holders.

Here is the mechanics: MGM sold its real estate to VICI Properties and the Bellagio REIT in 2022, in exchange for lease-back agreements under which it pays roughly $1.87 billion per year in cash rent (per the FY2025 10-K lease disclosures, p. 45). This rent flows through the financing section of the cash flow statement as operating lease payments and through the income statement as occupancy expense — it is a genuine cash outflow that reduces the cash available to service financial debt and fund equity returns. The Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA adds back D&A on the right-of-use assets ($509M non-cash lease amortization in FY2025) but does NOT deduct the cash rent. The company's own segment metric, Adjusted EBITDAR (Earnings before Rent), makes this explicit — by design it sits above the rent line. On an after-rent basis, the FY2025 Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA of $2,426M falls to approximately $556M before capex (EBITDA minus rent of ~$1,870M). This is not a hidden distortion — the company is transparent about it — but investors comparing MGM's Adjusted EBITDA multiples to peers who own their own real estate are making a structurally invalid comparison. The reported EBITDA is not freely available to equity holders; the vast majority of it is pre-committed to landlords. This structural reality, not an accounting manipulation, is the single most important lens for understanding MGM's real earnings power.

**Evidence:** FY2025 10-K (Feb-11-2026), Item 7 Liquidity and Capital Resources, p. 45 ("required to make annual contractual cash rent payments of $1.8 billion to our landlords over the next twelve months under triple net lease agreements"); Segment Adjusted EBITDAR definition, p. 41; Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA reconciliation, p. 42 (footnote 1: "Includes triple net lease rent expense of $2.3 billion in each of 2025, 2024, and 2023").

1. **Tax rate:** FY2025 effective rate of −85.5% is non-recurring; normalized rate closer to 5–11% range (per FY2023–FY2024 actuals). Beware any model using FY2025 GAAP net income as a base.
2. **Rent economics:** On-lease Adjusted EBITDA must be compared only to on-lease peers; all Adjusted EBITDA multiples should be cross-checked against EBITDAR (before rent), as MGM's rent obligation of ~$1.87–$2.3B/year is a fixed, contractual, senior-priority cash drain.
3. **MGM Digital goodwill impairment risk:** $341M goodwill at only 7% fair-value headroom; Deloitte flagged as Critical Audit Matter. Any revenue shortfall at LeoVegas could trigger further impairment in FY2026.

## 5. Interaction Effects

Las Vegas Strip RevPAR and US consumer spending (the largest external dependency) are mechanically linked — weaker consumer confidence drives lower visitor volumes and lower occupancy, which is exactly how RevPAR has been declining (occupancy from 94% to 92%, not ADR). ## 6. Non-Linear Or Asymmetric Risks

Three non-linear or asymmetric effects are material for MGM:

**Operating deleverage on the fixed rent.** The $1.87B triple-net lease rent to VICI escalates regardless of revenue. When revenue grows, the fixed rent base creates operating leverage: each additional dollar of Las Vegas Strip revenue converts at roughly 80–85% to segment EBITDAR (historical flow-through rate, FY2024–2025 reverse implied). When revenue falls, the same mechanism works in reverse with equal severity — a 4% revenue decline in FY2025 produced an 8% EBITDAR decline for the LV Strip segment. There is no variable cost offsets to the rent, which means small revenue declines below break-even (approximately the rate of revenue growth needed to absorb the annual rent escalation) generate disproportionately large EBITDA compression.

**Hold-percentage non-linearity.** Table games hold (the house win-rate) is statistically random around a normalized mean, but it is mechanically amplified by the drop base. If US or Macau gaming volumes fall (table drop contracts) and hold percentage also moves unfavorably in the same quarter — a plausible negative correlation since soft consumer periods can affect player behavior — the combined revenue impact is the product of two negative moves, not the sum. The Q2 2025 quarter (-$20M estimated hold impact) and Q1 2026 China February hold weakness illustrate that both can occur simultaneously.

**MGM Digital Brazil investment overshoot.** Management explicitly flagged on the Q1 2026 call that Brazil investment may exceed original FY2026 guidance. The Digital segment has a narrow cost base ($182.7M Q1 2026 revenue), meaning a $20–30M Brazil cost overshoot would represent a proportionally large hit to segment losses and could push the full-year loss toward the bear case of -$130M rather than the targeted -$45M. This is a small tail risk in absolute terms but non-linear in the sense that management guidance has already been flagged as uncertain.

## 5. Red-Flag Severity Verdict

**Critical concerns** — critical flags present; earnings setup is unreliable or should be downgraded.

Three critical flags are triggered. First, the triple-net lease rent ($1.87B) is structurally excluded from Consolidated Adjusted EBITDA — the company's and the market's primary metric — making MGM's headline earnings meaningfully overstate the cash available to equity holders; an EBITDA multiple applied to this metric is economically misleading without rent adjustment. Second, the FY2025 GAAP effective tax rate of -85.5% is non-recurring, meaning FY2025 GAAP net income and EPS are unrepeatable baselines; any forward model calibrated to FY2025 GAAP is wrong. Third, the single highest-sensitivity variable (LV Strip RevPAR, ±$200M EBITDA per 7% move) is still below its FY2024 level on an LTM basis and the recovery narrative rests on one quarter of convention-driven improvement against a background of sustained US leisure softness and a 30–40% Canadian visitor decline. The most dangerous single red flag is the rent-exclusion from Adj EBITDA combined with EBITDA declining on a YoY basis in four of the last five quarters: at $1.87B fixed rent on ~$2.43B Adj EBITDA, the margin for error is structurally narrow, and the trend is going the wrong way. This would be resolved by evidence of sustained LV Strip leisure recovery (three or more consecutive quarters of RevPAR above FY2024 levels) and/or a formal company sensitivity disclosure breaking down EBITDA by rent-adjusted vs rent-excluded basis.
