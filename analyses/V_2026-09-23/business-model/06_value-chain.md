## 2. Input Cost Pass-Through

| Axis | What it asks | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Contractual pass-through** | Is there an escalator, indexed-pricing, or cost-plus clause that moves price automatically? | **Not proven from available data.** The reviewed filings do not disclose an automatic cost escalator for Visa's network, technology, vendor or personnel costs. Visa's core network-and-processing expense is maintenance, equipment rental and other data-processing services, rather than a disclosed commodity input. | [FY25 Form 10-K, p.46] |
| **Realised recovery** | Of a given input-cost increase, how much did the company actually recover—through price, mix, hedging, re-sourcing, or a cost programme? | **Not computable from the pool.** FY25 network-and-processing expense was $894 million, up 15%, but Visa did not disclose a gross pre-mitigation input-cost shock or the resulting margin impact attributable to that shock. | [FY25 Form 10-K, p.46] |

`Realised recovery = 1 − (observed margin impact ÷ disclosed gross pre-mitigation input impact)`. Neither term is disclosed for a defined Visa input-cost event, so treating pricing as 100% recovery, or as zero recovery, would be unsupported. Visa has demonstrated **customer price and mix management**, not a measured input-cost pass-through: FY25 service, data-processing and other revenue each benefited from “select pricing modifications,” while client incentives rose to $15.751 billion, up 14%. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.45–46] In Q1 FY26, management said service revenue grew 13% against 9% payments-volume growth partly from pricing and card benefits, and data-processing revenue grew 17% against 9% transaction growth partly from pricing, value-added services and cross-border mix; that supports pricing capability but does not isolate price, volume or an input-cost recovery rate. [Visa Q1 FY26 earnings call, pp.7–8] The filings disclose no hedging, sourcing or cost-programme result that can be converted into a recovery percentage. Cost absorption is therefore not measured separately from price, mix and operating investment.

**Supplier / input concentration.** Visa does not disclose the share of purchases, cost of revenue or network cost paid to its largest supplier or top three suppliers. Its $894 million FY25 network-and-processing expense is an operating-expense category, not a vendor-spend schedule, so no concentration percentage is calculated. [FY25 Form 10-K, p.46] The CIQ supplier view names 22 recent relationships, but it supplies neither contract terms nor spend share; the filing also says Visa depends on vendors and suppliers to process transactions and provide services. [CIQ Suppliers export (V), retrieved 2026-09-23 — vendor export; FY25 Form 10-K, p.29] A material single-source input is **not proven from available data**.

## 3. Customer Pricing Power

Visa can change its own network pricing, but the evidence supports negotiated pricing rather than an ability to raise price without consequence. In FY25, “select pricing modifications” contributed to service, data-processing and other revenue; in Q1 FY26 management said new-pricing benefits were expected to be similar in magnitude to FY25, mainly in the second half. [FY25 Form 10-K, p.45; Visa Q1 FY26 earnings call, p.8] The reported volume reaction cannot be isolated: Q1 FY26 service revenue grew 13% while the relevant payments volume grew 9%, and data-processing revenue grew 17% while processed transactions grew 9%, but management also cited card benefits, value-added services and mix. [Visa Q1 FY26 earnings call, pp.7–8] So a price increase with no volume loss is **not proven from available data**.

The counterweight is visible in both contracts and results. Visa says some large financial-institution clients can issue non-Visa products or terminate with relatively short notice, and it faces pricing pressure from RTP networks, other facilitators and government involvement. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.28–29] Client incentives—up-front cash, discounts, rebates and performance payments—were $4.680 billion in Q3 FY26, up 18% year on year, while net revenue rose 14%; these are concessions to financial institutions, sellers, acquirers and other partners, not a raw-material cost pass-through. [Visa Q3 FY26 Earnings Release, pp.2, 4] Visa therefore has monetisation tools and a valuable network, but must share part of that value to retain routing and acceptance.

## 4. Economic Control Verdict

**Mixed.** Visa controls the processing platform and can make pricing modifications, supported by a global installed network and value-added services. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.4–6, 45] But it is not insulated from the bargaining power of issuers, acquirers and sellers: large clients retain product and termination flexibility, Visa must use incentives and discounts, and regulation or alternative routing can shift volume. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.28–29] Supplier concentration is not quantified, so the upstream side cannot support a stronger conclusion.

## 5. The Single Biggest Bargaining Risk

Large issuing banks, acquirers and payment facilitators steering transactions to RTP, domestic networks or alternative products could force higher incentives and lower Visa fees before a visible volume loss. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.28–29]
