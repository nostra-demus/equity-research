# Business Model Reality Check — V (Synthesis)

## Abstract

Visa runs a global payments network that authorizes, clears and settles transactions without lending. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.4–6] Its sole Payment Services segment earns volume-, processing- and cross-border-linked fees, less client incentives. [FY25 Form 10-K, Notes 1 and 14] Scale is the advantage: about 12 billion cards, bank accounts and wallets connect with over 175 million merchant locations. [FY25 Form 10-K, p.4] The weakness is defending volume: Q3 FY26 incentives grew 18%, versus 14% net revenue and 10% transactions, while regulation can change routing and fees. [Q3 FY26 Earnings Release, pp.2, 4; FY25 Form 10-K, pp.23–25] No disqualifier or rejector filter tripped; this is a high-quality business worth deeper work, with regulation and incentives central to the next stage.

## 3. Reconciliation

- **GAAP operating income versus the disqualifier expense denominator:** The audited FY25 filing reports $23.994bn of operating income and a 60.0% margin, including a $2.562bn litigation provision. The disqualifier scan's $26.808bn implied operating income and $13.192bn expense denominator omit that provision and reflect a different vendor-style basis. The filing basis wins for profitability. The related-party verdict remains N because the reported numerator was zero, so the denominator difference does not change the threshold result. [FY25 Form 10-K, p.60; FY26 DEF 14A, p.42; CIQ Financials→Income Statement, FY25 — vendor export]
- **Vendor-defined gross margin versus GAAP operating margin:** The 97.8% figure is Capital IQ's gross-margin classification after the $894m network-and-processing cost line; Visa does not present a separate GAAP gross-profit subtotal. It does not conflict with the 60.0% GAAP operating margin, which includes personnel, marketing, depreciation, administration and litigation. This synthesis labels each basis and uses neither as a substitute for per-transaction contribution margin. [CIQ Financials→Income Statement, FY25 — vendor export; FY25 Form 10-K, pp.45–47, 60]
- **Customer concentration:** Customer-geography identifies one client at 11%, while the disqualifier scan calls the >40% test not assessable. These are compatible: 11% is the only quantified client, but the filing does not prove the largest-client or top-three aggregate or disclose the named client's contract terms. The synthesis carries 11% as disclosed and treats the broader concentration test as unproven. [FY25 Form 10-K, Note 14, p.87; Item 1A, pp.27–28]
- **Network-scale definitions:** Business identity reports nearly 5 billion Visa payment credentials, while the moat report uses a broader figure of roughly 12 billion cards, bank accounts and wallets connected through the Visa-as-a-Service foundation. These are different scopes, not conflicting counts; this synthesis uses “payment credentials” only for the 5 billion figure and preserves the broader label for 12 billion. [FY25 Form 10-K, p.4]
- **Strong moat versus mixed economic control and only moderate business quality:** These are different questions. Visa's scale, distribution and return spread support a strong existing moat, while client bargaining power, regulation and new rails limit how much value Visa keeps and whether the moat widens. The stable-not-widening verdict retains both the improving gross margin/EBITDA margin/return series and the contradicting 559-basis-point GAAP operating-margin and 940-basis-point cash-conversion declines. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.4, 17, 23–29, 60; CIQ Financials→Income Statement/Cash Flow/Ratios, FY21–FY25 — vendor export]

## 4. Note To The Final Synthesizer

- **Strongest business-model positive:** Visa connects roughly 12 billion cards, bank accounts and wallets with more than 175 million merchant locations, and CY24 payment volume of $13.433tn exceeded Mastercard's $8.014tn and American Express's $1.750tn in Visa's named-network chart. The chart is one snapshot, not a share trend. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.4, 17]
- **Strongest business-model negative:** Defending routing is getting more expensive: Q3 FY26 client incentives increased 18%, versus 14% net revenue and 10% processed transactions, while some large clients can use competing products or leave on relatively short notice. [Q3 FY26 Earnings Release, pp.2, 4; FY25 Form 10-K, pp.27–28]
- **Most important segment:** Payment Services is the only reportable segment and accounts for all $40.0bn of FY25 net revenue; revenue categories and geographies are not separate profit pools. [FY25 Form 10-K, Note 14, pp.87–88]
- **Cleanest unit-economics read:** Net revenue per processed transaction increased 1.1% to $0.155 in FY25, but this mixes transaction-, volume-, cross-border- and service-based revenue. Contribution after variable costs, retention and payback cannot be derived. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.45–46; FY25 Annual Report, p.2]
- **Versus named peers:** Visa's FY25 GAAP operating margin was 60.0% versus Mastercard's 57.6%; American Express's 15.0% net margin and 33.9% return on equity include lending and are not comparable. Visa's standardized five-year return on capital was 22.4%, but clean peer ROIC data are missing. [FY25 Form 10-K, p.60; Mastercard FY25 Form 10-K, p.68; American Express FY25 Form 10-K, p.42; CIQ Financials→Ratios, FY21–FY25 — vendor export]
- **Main external dependency:** Consumer spending, especially travel-linked cross-border commerce, drives payment volume; regulation can separately change routing, interchange-linked economics, domestic processing and market access. No filing sensitivity quantifies a 20% demand or regulatory shock. [Q3 FY26 Form 10-Q, p.32; FY25 Form 10-K, pp.23–25, 30]
- **Most important capital-allocation or governance signal:** Cash returns are supported by low leverage, but legal cash demands and settlement guarantees are the constraint: Visa paid $3.142bn for legal matters in the first nine months of FY26, retained a $1.274bn accrual and $0.888bn escrow, and cannot determine the future settlement-guarantee amount. [Q3 FY26 Form 10-Q, Notes 9 and 16, pp.19, 24–25; Liquidity and Capital Resources, p.39]
- **Required red flag:** Do not extend the current tax rate mechanically. The Singapore operating-hub incentive reduced FY25 tax by $453m and expires under disclosed terms in September 2028, while nine-month FY26 GAAP net income includes a separate $351m one-time non-cash deferred-tax benefit. Renewal or post-2028 treatment is not established. [FY25 Form 10-K, Note 19, p.98; Q3 FY26 Form 10-Q, Note 15; Q3 FY26 Earnings Release, pp.10–11]
- **Automatic disqualifier:** None triggered. The absence of separate FY23/FY24 auditor reports and undisclosed largest-customer contract terms remain limitations, not positive proof. [FY25 Form 10-K, p.56; FY25 Form 10-K, Note 14, p.87]
- **Rejector filters:** Crooks/integrity, serial acquirer and fast-changing-industry filters did not trip; no score cap was applied. No `RF-DISQ-001`, `RF-RFS-001` or `RF-BQ-005` tag was emitted upstream.
- **Biggest missing data point:** Cohort-level incremental contribution after client incentives, with retention, contract duration and payback, is needed to prove that each added client or transaction creates value rather than merely adding volume. [FY25 Form 10-K, Note 1, p.69]
- **Deeper-work decision:** Yes. Move toward low-quality if adverse routing or fee rules cause meaningful volume loss, incentives continue to outrun revenue and transactions, or FY26 GAAP operating margin remains below FY25's 60.0% while cash conversion deteriorates further. A recovery to at least FY24's 65.7% margin, without weaker cash conversion, would instead strengthen the moat trajectory. [FY25 Form 10-K, p.60; CIQ Financials→Cash Flow, FY24–FY25 — vendor export]

## 5. Simple Summary

- Visa runs the network that moves payment instructions between banks and sellers; it does not lend the purchase money. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.4–6]
- It earns service, processing, cross-border and other fees, then subtracts client incentives. [FY25 Form 10-K, Note 1, p.69]
- Each new unit's value is not proven: revenue per transaction improved, but contribution margin, retention and payback are missing. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.45–46]
- Payment Services is the whole reported business; there is no disclosed second segment to offset it. [FY25 Form 10-K, Note 14, pp.87–88]
- The moat is strong against Mastercard and American Express because Visa has greater disclosed network scale, but the evidence supports stable, not widening. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.4, 17]
- Consumer spending, travel, FX, regulation and alternative payment rails matter most outside management's control. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.23–25, 30; Item 7A]
- Capital allocation is broadly sound, but acquisition returns are unproven and litigation and settlement guarantees can consume cash. [FY25 Form 10-K, Note 2, p.68; Q3 FY26 Form 10-Q, Notes 2, 9 and 16]
- It deserves deeper work. The next test is whether Visa can hold margins and cash conversion without incentives rising faster than volume and revenue. [Q3 FY26 Earnings Release, pp.2, 4; FY25 Form 10-K, p.60]

# Business Identity — V

## 1. What The Company Actually Does

Visa is a U.S. GAAP company reporting in U.S. dollars that runs a global payments technology network. [FY25 Form 10-K, Cover; Item 8] In its four-party model, the network authorizes, clears and settles a payment between the person or business paying, the card issuer, the merchant’s acquirer and the seller; Visa sends the authorization request between acquirer and issuer rather than funding the purchase itself. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.4–5] Its direct customers are primarily issuing and acquiring financial institutions, while sellers, consumers, payment processors, fintechs and governments participate in the wider network. [FY25 Form 10-K, Note 1, p.69] These customers buy network access and related processing, issuer, acceptance, risk-and-security, and advisory services so they can issue payment credentials, accept payments and move money without building a global network themselves. [FY25 Form 10-K, p.4; Note 1, p.69] Visa does not issue cards, lend money, set cardholder rates or earn interchange reimbursement fees or merchant discount rates. [FY25 Form 10-K, p.6] In FY25, Visa processed 258 billion of 329 billion Visa-branded payments and cash transactions; it had nearly 5 billion payment credentials available at more than 175 million merchant locations in over 200 countries and territories. [FY25 Form 10-K, p.4] For financial reporting, Visa has one reportable segment: Payment Services. [FY25 Form 10-K, Note 14, p.87]

## 2. How The Company Makes Money

`Net revenue = (payments volume × service-fee rate) + (processed transactions × data-processing/network-access fee) + (cross-border volume × cross-border processing and currency-conversion fee) + (value-added-service, licence, certification and account-holder fees) − client incentives.` Service revenue is mainly calculated by applying current-quarter pricing to prior-quarter payments volume; the other activity-based revenue lines are recognized in the current period. [FY25 Form 10-K, Note 1, p.69]

These are Visa’s network and service fees, not the interchange reimbursement fees paid by acquirers to issuers or the merchant discount rate charged by acquirers to sellers. [FY25 Form 10-K, p.6] Usage drives the first three terms: in Q3 FY26, payments volume grew 10%, processed transactions grew 10% to 71.7 billion, and cross-border volume excluding intra-Europe grew 12%, all on a constant-dollar basis. [Visa Q3 FY26 Earnings Release, 2026-07-28, p.2] Price and mix matter because cross-border revenue includes currency conversion, while value-added services can carry fixed or transaction-based fees. [FY25 Form 10-K, Note 1, p.69] Operating margin—the portion of revenue left after operating costs—also depends on client incentives and costs: in Q3 FY26, client incentives rose 18% and GAAP operating expenses rose 19%, versus net-revenue growth of 14%. [Visa Q3 FY26 Earnings Release, 2026-07-28, pp.2, 4]

## 3. Business Type Classification

Global payments-network and transaction processor: payments volume, transaction count and cross-border commerce drive fees, while value-added services add fixed and transaction-based revenue. [FY25 Form 10-K, Note 1, p.69]

## 3a. Sector Overlay & Required-KPI Checklist

No sector overlay for global payments-network and transaction processor — generic read. The generic usage read has payment volume, processed transactions and cross-border volume present in the pool; a single reported network take-rate KPI is not proven from available data. [Visa Q3 FY26 Earnings Release, 2026-07-28, p.2; FY25 Form 10-K, Note 1, p.69]

## 4. What Drives Variance

Revenue moves first with payments volume and transaction count, while cross-border activity adds a separate fee stream that includes currency conversion. [FY25 Form 10-K, Note 1, p.69] Timing matters: Q3 FY26 service revenue used the payments volume of the quarter ended March 31, 2026, whereas the other revenue categories reflected current-quarter activity. [Visa Q3 FY26 Earnings Release, 2026-07-28, p.2] In that quarter, service revenue grew 14%, data-processing revenue 17%, international-transaction revenue 6%, other revenue 45% and client incentives 18%; this shows that revenue mix and client-contract economics can move differently from aggregate payment volume. [Visa Q3 FY26 Earnings Release, 2026-07-28, pp.2, 4]

**Unit economics: Unclear.** The FY25 revenue-minus-network-processing spread proxy was about $0.152 per processed transaction, but it does not prove incremental value creation because Visa does not split fixed from variable costs and its revenue is earned from several bases, not only transaction count. [FY25 Form 10-K, Item 7, pp. 45–47] The single most valuable missing disclosure is cohort-level incremental contribution after client incentives, together with contract retention and payback; without it, the test of contribution margin × unit lifetime exceeding acquisition cost cannot be completed.

## 4. Sensitivity

Client incentives are the disclosed input most likely to change this read. Incentives grew from $12.297bn in FY23 to $15.751bn in FY25; per processed transaction they rose from $0.058 to $0.061 (5.8%), while net revenue per processed transaction increased only 1.1% over the same two years. [FY25 Form 10-K, Item 7, pp. 45–46; FY25 Annual Report, p. 2] The latest quarter points the same way: Q3 FY26 client incentives rose 18%, compared with 14% net-revenue growth and 10% processed-transaction growth. [Q3 FY26 earnings release, Financial Highlights and Operational Performance Data] A 20% increase in FY25 incentives is a **zero-mitigation bound, not a forecast**: $15.751bn × 20% = $3.150bn, or $0.0122 per FY25 processed transaction; holding pre-incentive revenue and transaction count fixed would reduce net revenue from $40.000bn to $36.850bn (−7.9%) and revenue per transaction from $0.1553 to $0.1431. Pricing, volume, mix, and client-contract responses could offset part of that result, but the filing does not quantify the offset.

## 3. Customer Pricing Power

Visa can change its own network pricing, but the evidence supports negotiated pricing rather than an ability to raise price without consequence. In FY25, “select pricing modifications” contributed to service, data-processing and other revenue; in Q1 FY26 management said new-pricing benefits were expected to be similar in magnitude to FY25, mainly in the second half. [FY25 Form 10-K, p.45; Visa Q1 FY26 earnings call, p.8] The reported volume reaction cannot be isolated: Q1 FY26 service revenue grew 13% while the relevant payments volume grew 9%, and data-processing revenue grew 17% while processed transactions grew 9%, but management also cited card benefits, value-added services and mix. [Visa Q1 FY26 earnings call, pp.7–8] So a price increase with no volume loss is **not proven from available data**.

The counterweight is visible in both contracts and results. Visa says some large financial-institution clients can issue non-Visa products or terminate with relatively short notice, and it faces pricing pressure from RTP networks, other facilitators and government involvement. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.28–29] Client incentives—up-front cash, discounts, rebates and performance payments—were $4.680 billion in Q3 FY26, up 18% year on year, while net revenue rose 14%; these are concessions to financial institutions, sellers, acquirers and other partners, not a raw-material cost pass-through. [Visa Q3 FY26 Earnings Release, pp.2, 4] Visa therefore has monetisation tools and a valuable network, but must share part of that value to retain routing and acceptance.

## 4. Economic Control Verdict

**Mixed.** Visa controls the processing platform and can make pricing modifications, supported by a global installed network and value-added services. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.4–6, 45] But it is not insulated from the bargaining power of issuers, acquirers and sellers: large clients retain product and termination flexibility, Visa must use incentives and discounts, and regulation or alternative routing can shift volume. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.28–29] Supplier concentration is not quantified, so the upstream side cannot support a stronger conclusion.

## 5. The Single Biggest Bargaining Risk

Large issuing banks, acquirers and payment facilitators steering transactions to RTP, domestic networks or alternative products could force higher incentives and lower Visa fees before a visible volume loss. [FY25 Form 10-K, pp.28–29]

# Competitive Map — V

## 1. Dominant Segment

Payment Services is Visa’s sole reportable segment and generated all $40.0 billion of FY25 net revenue. [FY25 Form 10-K, Note 14, p. 87]

## 2. Named Competitors

Visa names both companies below as global or multi-regional payment-network competitors. Peer figures use each company’s own FY25 calendar year ended December 31, 2025; Visa’s FY25 ended September 30, 2025, so they are not a same-date comparison. [FY25 Form 10-K, Cover; Competition, p. 17; Mastercard FY25 Form 10-K, Cover; American Express FY25 Form 10-K, Cover]

### Competitor A — Mastercard Incorporated

- **Ticker / listing:** MA / NYSE. [Mastercard FY25 Form 10-K, Cover]
- **Where they compete:** A global, multi-regional branded card network, competing for consumers, financial institutions and sellers; its network authorizes, clears and settles transactions. [FY25 Form 10-K, Competition, p. 17; Mastercard FY25 Form 10-K, Item 1, Business]
- **Scale:** FY25 net revenue was $32.791 billion. Visa’s comparable-network chart reports Mastercard CY24 payment volume of $8.014 trillion. [Mastercard FY25 Form 10-K, Consolidated Statements of Operations, p. 68; FY25 Form 10-K, Competition, p. 17]
- **Profitability / return on capital:** FY25 GAAP operating margin was 57.6% ($18.897 billion operating income ÷ $32.791 billion net revenue). Return on equity (ROE, annual earnings relative to average book equity) was 210.5%: $14.968 billion net income ÷ [($7.737 billion FY25 equity + $6.485 billion FY24 equity) ÷ 2]. This book-equity return is elevated by a small equity denominator after share repurchases and is not a like-for-like operating ROIC comparison. [Mastercard FY25 Form 10-K, Consolidated Statements of Operations, p. 68; Consolidated Balance Sheets, p. 70]
- **Source named in:** Visa’s Competition section names Mastercard among global or multi-regional networks. [FY25 Form 10-K, Competition, p. 17]
- **One-line read:** *Inference, based on the disclosed business models:* the closest direct rival, using the same issuer–acquirer network model and also selling payment-related services beyond switching. [Mastercard FY25 Form 10-K, Item 1, Business]

### Competitor B — American Express Company

- **Ticker / listing:** AXP / NYSE. [American Express FY25 Form 10-K, Cover]
- **Where they compete:** A global or multi-regional branded card network competing for cardholders, issuing partners and merchant acceptance. [FY25 Form 10-K, Competition, p. 17]
- **Scale:** FY25 total revenues net of interest expense were $72.229 billion; Visa’s comparable-network chart reports American Express CY24 payment volume of $1.750 trillion. Revenue is not directly comparable with Visa because American Express also earns lending-related income. [American Express FY25 Form 10-K, Table 1: Summary of Financial Performance, p. 42; FY25 Form 10-K, Competition, p. 17]
- **Profitability / return on capital:** FY25 GAAP net margin was 15.0% ($10.833 billion net income ÷ $72.229 billion total revenues net of interest expense), and reported return on average equity was 33.9%. These are financial-company metrics: the same filing includes provisions for credit losses and $224.791 billion of loans and Card Member receivables, unlike Visa’s network-only model. [American Express FY25 Form 10-K, Table 1: Summary of Financial Performance, p. 42]
- **Source named in:** Visa’s Competition section names American Express among global or multi-regional networks. [FY25 Form 10-K, Competition, p. 17]
- **One-line read:** *Inference, based on its lending balance sheet and income statement:* a material branded-network rival, but its integrated issuer-and-lender economics make its margin and ROE a poor direct proxy for a stand-alone payment-network return. [American Express FY25 Form 10-K, Table 1: Summary of Financial Performance, p. 42]

## 3. Competitive Position

**Position vs peers: not disclosed.** Visa’s filing provides a single CY24 network snapshot: Visa payment volume was $13.433 trillion versus Mastercard’s $8.014 trillion and American Express’s $1.750 trillion; Visa therefore represented 56.5% of the five-network chart total of $23.769 trillion ($13.433 trillion ÷ [$13.433 trillion + $8.014 trillion + $1.750 trillion + $0.253 trillion + $0.319 trillion]). [FY25 Form 10-K, Competition, p. 17]

That snapshot does not show a share change, and Visa does not disclose a two-year comparable share series across the named networks. It cannot establish whether Visa is gaining, holding or losing share; payment-volume growth alone would not resolve that question without matching peer growth on the same basis.

## 4. Competitive Shape

The global branded-card-network subset is an **oligopoly**, led by Visa and Mastercard: their CY24 payment volumes sum to $21.447 trillion ($13.433 trillion + $8.014 trillion), or 90.2% of the five-network chart’s $23.769 trillion total. [FY25 Form 10-K, Competition, p. 17] This is not a claim about all payments: Visa separately identifies UnionPay, local and regional networks, real-time-payment systems, digital wallets and alternative-payment providers, which the chart does not measure as one market. [FY25 Form 10-K, Competition, pp. 17–18]

## 5. Caveat

*Inference, based on Visa’s competitor chart:* Mastercard and American Express are the two most credible direct rivals at global scale and are named in Visa’s own filing. [FY25 Form 10-K, Competition, p. 17] Discover/Diners Club, JCB and UnionPay are also named, but the supplied evidence does not give the same current, stand-alone financial and return data for a comparable third profile. A two- or more-year, like-for-like payment-volume or transaction-share series covering Visa, Mastercard, American Express, UnionPay, domestic networks and real-time payments would resolve the position-versus-peers gap.

## 3. Most Severe New Flag

Tax-rate normalisation and incentive-expiry exposure is the most material incremental flag. The $351m FY26 benefit is explicitly one-time and non-cash, while the $453m FY25 Singapore incentive is conditional and ends under the disclosed terms in September 2028. Neither fact establishes that Visa will lose the Singapore incentive, but both mean reported tax rates and after-tax earnings should not be extended mechanically. [FY25 Form 10-K, Note 19—Income Taxes, p.98; Visa Q3 FY26 Earnings Release, 2026-07-28, pp.10–11]

## 4. Cross-Cutting Patterns

Near-term GAAP earnings contain disclosed items moving in opposite directions: the $351m deferred-tax benefit lifts nine-month net income, while $563m of severance and $1.255bn of litigation provision reduce it. They are separately disclosed and should be retained as separate normalisation items rather than netted into a single view of operating momentum. This is not an aggressive-accounting allegation: the company identifies the tax benefit as one-time and the severance cost as non-representative in its official reconciliation. [Visa Q3 FY26 Earnings Release, 2026-07-28, pp.10–11]
