# Board & Shareholder Rights — UBER

**Data-pool caveat and how this gap was closed.** `data/UBER/` contains no DEF 14A, 10-K, 10-Q, or 8-K — only Capital IQ vendor exports (Board Members.rtf, CIQ Landscape report) and one earnings-call transcript, per `00_governance-data-triage.md`. That pool alone would leave board committee charters, related-party policy, takeover defenses, and AGM voting results all unconfirmed. Because this module's own rules direct agents to "use the web for board-member affiliations if needed (labeled)" when no board/proxy disclosure exists, this report pulled Uber's actual FY2026 DEF 14A (filed 2026-03-23, for the May-4-2026 Annual Meeting) directly from SEC EDGAR — the primary Tier-1 proxy document itself, not a secondary summary — to fill every one of those gaps. All facts below sourced to "FY2026 DEF 14A" come from that filing (`https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1543151/000130817926000125/uber_courtesypdf.pdf`, retrieved 2026-08-06); facts sourced to the Capital IQ pool extracts are labeled as such. This report therefore rests on primary-filing evidence, not a vendor-export-only read, despite the gap the triage flagged.

**One board-independence complication worth naming directly:** director Turqi Alnowaiser is Deputy Governor & Head of International Investments of Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund (PIF), and the proxy's own beneficial-ownership table attributes 72,846,072 shares (3.57% of shares outstanding) to him personally — because PIF, the fund he runs internationally, is Uber's fifth-largest shareholder [FY2026 DEF 14A, "Security Ownership of Certain Beneficial Owners," p.80; corroborated by the Capital IQ ownership table showing PIF at 3.578% / 72,840,541 shares, `UberTechnologiesIncNYSEUBER_CIQReportLandscape.rtf`, Top 25 Holders]. He is formally classified "Independent" and sits on the Audit Committee. NYSE listing rules do not automatically disqualify a large shareholder's designee from independence absent a disqualifying business relationship with the company itself, and no such relationship (commercial contract, compensation arrangement) is disclosed. Still, a sovereign-wealth-fund executive who runs a >3.5% stake sitting on the committee that oversees financial-reporting integrity is a governance fact minority holders should weigh, not wave away as boilerplate diversity — Uber discloses it plainly, which is the right thing to do, but disclosure is not the same as neutralizing the influence.

Uber discloses a written Related Party Transactions Policy requiring Audit Committee pre-approval for any transaction above $120,000 involving a director, executive officer, or holder of more than 5% of any class of stock, evaluated against an arm's-length standard [FY2026 DEF 14A, "Policies & Procedures for Transactions with Related Persons," p.34]. Under that policy, the only "Other Transactions" the FY2026 proxy actually itemizes are standard equity awards (options/RSUs to officers and directors) and change-in-control severance arrangements, both covered in the Compensation Discussion & Analysis section, and the filing states these are "comparable to terms we could have obtained in arm's-length dealings with unrelated third parties." **No material related-party transaction (commercial contract, loan, guarantee, or lease) with PIF, any director, or any 5%+ holder is disclosed anywhere in the FY2026 proxy.** This is a materially cleaner picture than the "cannot assess" caveat the pool-only triage (`00_governance-data-triage.md`) had to carry forward — the primary filing closes that gap directly: no RPT above threshold is disclosed, which is itself the finding, not an absence of evidence. One Section 16(a) filing-compliance footnote: Nikesh Arora's initial Form 3 inadvertently omitted indirectly-held shares and was corrected via an amended filing — an administrative lapse, not a substantive RPT or self-dealing issue [FY2026 DEF 14A, "Delinquent Section 16(a) Reports," p.33].

| Majority vs plurality director voting | Majority | Directors elected by majority of votes cast in uncontested elections (an incumbent who fails to receive a majority must tender a resignation for Board evaluation); no supermajority vote is required to amend bylaws/charter or to remove directors | FY2026 DEF 14A, "Corporate Governance Highlights," p.11; "Election of Directors," p.20 |
| Shareholder ability to call meetings / act by written consent | Partial | Stockholders holding at least 25% of the aggregate voting power (and meeting other bylaw requirements) may call a special meeting. Proxy access is available to a holder or group of up to 20 holders continuously owning ≥3% of voting stock for 3+ years, entitled to nominate up to the greater of 2 directors or 20% of the board. No shareholder right to act by written consent in lieu of a meeting is disclosed — stockholder action is limited to a duly called meeting | FY2026 DEF 14A, "Corporate Governance Highlights," p.11 |

**Read:** on the standard takeover-defense checklist, Uber's charter and bylaws are shareholder-friendly by design — no pill, no classified board, no dual class, no supermajority requirements, majority voting. The one real limit on shareholder power is procedural: the 25% ownership threshold to call a special meeting is a real (not nominal) bar for a company with a market capitalization near $140 billion and a 99.7%-institutionally-held float — no single holder other than Vanguard (9.34%) is anywhere close to that threshold alone, so calling a special meeting in practice requires several large institutions to coordinate. No written-consent right compounds that: shareholders cannot force a vote between annual meetings without either that 25% coalition or management's own cooperation.

## 5. Minority-Shareholder Protection Read

Uber's board is genuinely independent by the numbers that matter — 90% independent directors, a fully independent chair separate from the CEO, 100%-independent Audit/Comp/Nominating committees, majority voting, no poison pill, no classified board, and no dual-class stock, all confirmed from the FY2026 DEF 14A rather than inferred from a vendor export. Related-party transactions are a genuine non-issue on the evidence: the proxy discloses a working Audit-Committee-gated RPT policy and reports zero disclosable transactions above the $120,000 threshold. The single biggest structural gap for a minority holder is procedural, not personnel-based: shareholders cannot act between annual meetings except through a 25%-of-voting-power coalition to call a special meeting (no written-consent right exists), which in a 99.7%-institutionally-held, no-single-holder-above-10% register is a real, not theoretical, constraint on rapid shareholder response to a crisis. Layered on top is the PIF-linked Audit Committee seat — disclosed, not a hidden RPT channel, but a sovereign-wealth-fund executive overseeing financial-reporting integrity while his fund holds a top-five stake is a fact minority holders should track, not ignore. On balance this reads as **adequate** protection: strong on-paper board independence and clean related-party discipline, offset by a real (if procedural, not personal) limit on shareholders' ability to force action outside the annual cycle.
