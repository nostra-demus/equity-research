## 2. Input Cost Pass-Through

Hydro does not "pass through" its two biggest input costs in the retail sense — it buys and sells the same commodity, so cost and revenue move together almost mechanically, but on different clocks. Alumina bought or sold externally is priced "with reference to alumina spot price indexes" with a stated one-month delay, so a spike or drop in the alumina index shows up in Hydro's books roughly a month later on both sides of the ledger [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, Note 8.2, p.186-187]. Energy for smelters is mostly locked in on long-term contracts (up to 20 years, "mainly at fixed prices"), which insulates Hydro from short-term power-price swings on the input side but also means it cannot renegotiate down quickly if power prices fall, and — as the Tomago (Australia) full impairment shows — cannot always renew on comparable terms when a contract nears expiry ("the full write-down reflects the significant uncertainty regarding future power prices beyond the current power price agreements expiring in 2028") [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, impairment discussion / p.15]. About 25% of Aluminium Metal's alumina consumption is fixed 2-3 years ahead under Hydro's "Integrated hedge program," and since 2025 that fixed-price slice is also linked to the caustic-soda price — a contractual escalator of sorts, but on the input side, not something charged to a customer [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, Note 1.4, p.186]. On the output side there is no true "pass-through clause" to customers for base metal price — customers simply pay the LME price plus a premium at the time of sale; the only discretionary lever is the size of the premium Hydro can command for value-add/low-carbon product, and management states there are "limited possibilities for hedging future premiums" [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, Note 8.2, p.187]. Extrusions, the one segment with genuinely negotiated (non-index) pricing, has not been able to use that negotiating room to defend margin: its reported EBIT margin fell from roughly 4% in 2020-2023 to -2.2% in 2025 even as management cut costs and proposed closing five European plants [`03_segment-map.md`, §1].

**Supplier / input concentration (quantify).** The pool does not disclose a single-supplier or top-3-supplier percentage of COGS or purchases — Hydro reports only that its supplier base spans "approximately 39,000" vendors globally, with no spend concentration by vendor stated [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, "Workers in the value chain," p.126]. Where the report does quantify concentration, it is by country/region of input, not by vendor: caustic soda for Alunorte's refining is sourced from the USA (a single-country dependency); coal for Alunorte is sourced from mines in Colombia (a single-country dependency); anodes are sourced from Europe, Norway and China; extrusion ingot is sourced across the Americas, Asia, Europe and the Middle East [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, "Workers in the value chain," p.119]. On bauxite — the one input Hydro is largely self-sufficient in — "close to 85 percent" of total (direct plus indirect) bauxite sourcing traces back to mines in Brazil and Australia, with "the third largest indirect bauxite source, at approximately 6 percent," coming from two mines in Guinea, a jurisdiction the report says Hydro "monitors... continuously" given "the challenging situation in Guinea" [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, p.119-120]. None of these is disclosed as a percentage of COGS or purchase spend, so the size of the dependency in cost terms is **not proven from available data** — but the single-country concentration in caustic soda (USA) and coal (Colombia) for the Alunorte refinery, and the Guinea bauxite exposure, are the closest things to a material single-source dependency this pool surfaces, and they carry into §5 below.

## 3. Customer Pricing Power

Hydro cannot raise the base price of aluminium or alumina — that price is set on the LME and by public alumina indexes, not by Hydro — so "pricing power" for this company means the size of the premium it can add on top, and the evidence is genuinely mixed. On value-add/standard-ingot premiums, Hydro is a price-taker on the market-wide level (US Section 232 tariffs lifted the Midwest premium Hydro realizes "from just over $2,000 per tonne at the end of '25 to north of $2,500" by end-Q1 2026, and European duty-paid premiums moved from $335 to $587 in the same quarter) — these are policy- and supply-shock-driven, not company-specific pricing actions [Q1 2026 transcript, Apr-29-2026, prepared remarks, p.4-5]. Where Hydro does show an actual pricing action with a volume result is its low-carbon product line: sales of Hydro CIRCAL (recycled) and Hydro REDUXA (low-carbon primary) reached 58 thousand tonnes and 470 thousand tonnes respectively in FY2025 (roughly a quarter of FY2025 primary-aluminium production of 2,065 kmt combined), and the company states this delivered "a more than 50 percent increase in greener products earnings in 2025 versus 2024" — evidence of real, incremental pricing power tied to a differentiated, certified product rather than the commodity itself [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, p.106-107 ("Progressing on low-carbon aluminium volumes")]. Outside that green-premium niche, Extrusions — the one segment that actually negotiates prices with individual customers — could not defend margin through 2024-2025 (EBIT margin -2.2% in 2025), the clearest evidence that customer pricing power is weak once you leave the commodity-price mechanism [`03_segment-map.md`, §1].

## 4. Economic Control Verdict

**Mixed.** On the input side Hydro is structurally advantaged — it owns its own bauxite mine supplying close to 100% of two major refinery/smelter chains, generates roughly half its own hydropower and locks in most of the rest on contracts running up to 25 years, and this combination places its smelting on the "16th percentile of the global cost curve" (cheaper than 84% of global competitors) [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, p.14, 119-120]. But on the output side, across every stage that touches an external customer — LME-priced primary metal, index-priced alumina, near-zero-margin Metal Markets, and now negative-margin Extrusions — Hydro is a price-taker with only a narrow, growing lever (low-carbon product premiums) to push back against a market it does not set.

## 5. The Single Biggest Bargaining Risk

The dependency on external power-supply contracts for smelters outside Hydro's own captive Nordic hydropower base: the Tomago (Australia) smelter was already fully written down because of "significant uncertainty regarding future power prices beyond the current power price agreements expiring in 2028," showing how fast losing a favorable power-supplier relationship can destroy asset value once Hydro is not the one generating the electricity.
