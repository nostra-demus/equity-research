# Business Model Reality Check — NHY (Synthesis)

## Abstract

Norsk Hydro mines bauxite, refines it into alumina, smelts it into aluminium, and shapes some into extruded products, backed by captive Nordic and Brazilian hydropower. Profit is dominated by Hydro Aluminium Metal, 39.5% of FY2025's NOK 28,889 million adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, amortization) despite only 7.1% of revenue. Its strongest positive: a 16th-percentile global cost-curve position from captive bauxite and hydropower gives it the best EBITDA margin (23.7%) among three named peers. Its strongest negative: near-total price-taking drove a business-quality score of 34/100, with group ROE swinging from 24.9% (2022) to 2.6% (2023). No disqualifier triggered; verdict is cyclical, worth deeper work only with a timing edge.

## 5. Simple Summary

- **What it does:** Mines bauxite, refines it to alumina, smelts it into aluminium, and turns some into extruded products (window frames, car parts, cooling tubes) — plus runs a captive hydropower business that powers its own smelters [`02_business-identity.md`].
- **How it makes money:** Sells aluminium and alumina at prices set by the London Metal Exchange and public alumina indexes, plus a regional/product premium it partly controls; margin comes from being cheap to produce (16th-percentile global cost curve), not from pricing power [`02_business-identity.md`; `06_value-chain.md`].
- **Whether each new unit creates value:** Yes, at current pricing — Aluminium Metal earns a 14.4% after-tax return on capital (ARoaCE) against a 10% group hurdle, but the margin cushion (~USD 532/mt) is smaller than a single 20% price swing, so the "creates value" answer is cycle-dependent, not guaranteed [`04_unit-economics.md`].
- **Which segment matters most:** Hydro Aluminium Metal, 39.5% of FY2025 profit despite only 7.1% of revenue — but this rotates with the alumina cycle and was Bauxite & Alumina's turn in FY2024 [`03_segment-map.md`].
- **Whether it has a moat, and against whom:** A narrow moat against RUSAL, Chalco, and Hindalco — built on captive bauxite and hydropower giving the best margin of the three — but it is eroding at the whole-company level as Hydro Extrusions' margin turns negative and five European plants face closure [`09_moat.md`].
- **What external variables it depends on:** The LME aluminium price, the USD/NOK exchange rate, EU carbon policy (CBAM/ETS), and geopolitical supply shocks — all outside management's control, cushioned only partly by hedging and a low-cost position [`10_external-dependency.md`].
- **Whether capital is allocated well:** Reasonably — one bolt-on deal in five years plus active divestment of non-core bets, falling share count, and low leverage (net debt/EBITDA 0.24x) — but an unquantified Brazilian tax/Alunorte contingency and a thin impairment cushion on the one recent acquisition (Alumetal) are live, unresolved risks [`11_capital-allocation-governance.md`; `12_red-flags-sweep.md`].
- **Whether it deserves deeper work:** Only with a strong, evidenced view on where the aluminium/alumina price cycle is headed — the business model itself is a cyclical, price-taking commodity producer, not a durable compounder.

# Business Identity — NHY

## 1. What The Company Actually Does

Norsk Hydro digs up bauxite ore in Brazil, turns it into alumina powder, then uses electricity to turn that alumina into liquid aluminium metal, and finally shapes some of that metal into finished products such as window frames, car parts and cooling tubes — while also running a Nordic and Brazilian hydropower business that supplies much of the electricity the smelters need. It owns "one of the largest bauxite mines in the world" (Paragominas, Brazil) and "the largest alumina refinery outside China" (Alunorte, Brazil), five wholly-owned primary-aluminium smelters in Norway plus stakes in five more abroad (Qatar, Brazil, Canada, Australia, Slovakia), a network of 12 metal recyclers, and 69 extrusion plants in 20 countries [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, "About Hydro" / Business areas, p.10-18]. Its buyers are other industrial companies, not consumers: automakers, construction and building-systems firms, packaging and beverage-can makers, and electrical/renewable-energy manufacturers, reached through roughly 30,000 customers across more than 140 locations in 40 countries [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, "Our presence", p.12]. Customers pay Hydro to solve two problems: getting a steady, contracted supply of a metal that is priced daily on a public commodity exchange (the London Metal Exchange, LME), and — increasingly — getting that metal with a certified low carbon footprint, for which some customers pay a premium [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, p.16-17]. FY2025 total revenue was NOK 207,971 million and adjusted EBITDA — earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization, a company-defined measure that strips out one-off items — was NOK 28,889 million [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, Consolidated income statement, p.135; Key figures, p.36].

## 2. How The Company Makes Money

Hydro is really five linked commodity businesses sharing one value chain, each with its own formula:

- **Hydro Bauxite & Alumina:** `Revenue = alumina tonnes sold × realized alumina price (USD/mt)`. FY2025: alumina production 6,086 thousand tonnes (kmt), realized alumina price USD 432/mt [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, Key figures, p.36].
- **Hydro Aluminium Metal (primary smelting):** `Revenue = primary aluminium tonnes produced × (LME aluminium price + product premium)`. FY2025: primary aluminium production 2,065 kmt, realized aluminium price (LME) USD 2,573/mt [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, Key figures, p.36].
- **Hydro Metal Markets (recycling + trading):** `Revenue = recycled/traded tonnes sold × (LME price + premium)`, plus trading and hedging margin [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, Business areas — Metal Markets, p.16-17].
- **Hydro Extrusions:** `Revenue = extrusion tonnes sold × price per tonne`, where price is negotiated per customer/application rather than set purely by LME [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, Business areas — Extrusions, p.18]. FY2025 external sales volumes were 978 kmt [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, Key figures, p.36].
- **Hydro Energy:** `Revenue = power volume sold × power price`, from ~18 TWh/year of owned and contracted Nordic/Brazilian hydropower, most of which is used internally to feed the smelters rather than sold externally [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, Business areas — Energy, p.15].

Volume is set by smelter/refinery capacity and utilization (Hydro's total primary-aluminium capacity is about 2.1 million tonnes/year) [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, p.16]. Price is set almost entirely outside the company's control — by the LME 3-month aluminium price (FY2025 average around USD 2,573/mt realized) and by product premiums that shift with regional supply/demand [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, p.36; Q1 2026 report/deck]. Margin is driven by where Hydro sits on the industry cost curve: management states Hydro's primary-aluminium smelting placed at the 16th percentile of the global cost curve in 2025 (lower is cheaper, better), with alumina, power and carbon anodes together making up 80-85% of smelting cash cost, and Alunorte's refining costs dominated by bauxite, energy and caustic soda (about 85% of cash cost) [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, p.14, p.16].

## 3. Business Type Classification

Vertically-integrated commodity aluminium producer — bauxite mine to alumina refinery to primary smelter to recycler/extruder — with a captive hydropower business that funds and de-risks its own energy input costs.

## 4. What Drives Variance

The two biggest swing factors are the LME 3-month aluminium price and regional product premiums — both set externally, not by Hydro — since realized aluminium price on its own moved from USD 2,218/mt (2023) to USD 2,374/mt (2024) to USD 2,573/mt (2025) [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, p.36]. The next biggest factor is input cost, especially the price of alumina, electricity/carbon anodes for smelting (together 80-85% of smelting cash cost), and energy/caustic soda for alumina refining (about 85% of refining cash cost) [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, p.14, p.16]. Currency also matters directly — Hydro reports in NOK but sells largely at USD-linked prices, so the realized USD/NOK exchange rate (10.39 in 2025 vs 10.75 in 2024) changes reported revenue and margin even if underlying USD prices and volumes are flat [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, p.36]. Volume/utilization (smelter curtailments or ramp-ups) and mix (how much output goes to higher-value casthouse/extrusion products versus standard ingot) are secondary but still material drivers, particularly for Hydro Extrusions and Metal Markets [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, p.16-17].

## 2. Dominant Segment

This dominance is not stable across time: in FY2024, Hydro Bauxite & Alumina was actually the largest Adjusted-EBITDA contributor (41.0% vs Aluminium Metal's 36.7%), driven by a spike in alumina prices [FY2025 Integrated Annual Report, Alternative Performance Measures, p.232-233]. First, segment reporting covers revenue (external and internal), EBIT, EBITDA, Adjusted EBITDA (via the Alternative Performance Measures note), depreciation & amortization, capital expenditure and total assets for every segment — a materially fuller picture than revenue-only disclosure [FY2025 Integrated Annual Report, Note 1.4, p.149-151]. The "Other and Eliminations" bucket is immaterial for revenue (~0.0%) and profit (0.5%) but houses NOK 17,254m of assets in FY2025 — 8.3% of the group's NOK 208,295m total assets — a large unallocated-asset pool investors cannot attribute to an operating segment; this falls short of the >10% "Other" flag threshold but is close enough to note. Most importantly for forward comparability: Hydro has told investors it will **stop presenting EBIT and EBITDA as segment performance measures** once IFRS 18 takes effect (annual periods beginning on or after 1 Jan 2027), replacing them with a new metric, "Adjusted OPDAI" (Operating Profit before Depreciation, Amortization and Impairment, adjusted), plus the equity-accounted share of profit/loss [FY2025 Integrated Annual Report, Note 10.5, p.206]. Downstream agents (`unit-economics`, `competitive-map`) should expect a discontinuity in segment profit metrics from FY2027 reporting onward and should not silently splice Adjusted OPDAI onto the historical EBITDA series without reconciliation.

# Unit Economics — NHY

## 1. Natural Unit

The natural economic unit for Hydro Aluminium Metal (the dominant segment by profit — 39.5% of FY2025 group Adjusted EBITDA, per `03_segment-map.md`) is **one tonne of primary aluminium produced and sold** (metric tonne, "mt"). This is a manufacturer, so the unit is a physical unit of output, not a customer or account — the closest parallel to "manufacturer, per unit produced or ton" in the natural-unit reference list.

Hydro is a genuinely integrated value chain (no segment exceeds 85% of profit), so four secondary unit types matter and are not built out below: bauxite/alumina per tonne (Hydro Bauxite & Alumina), extruded product per tonne (Hydro Extrusions, whose EBIT margin turned negative in FY2025 per the segment map), recycled casthouse product per tonne (Hydro Metal Markets recyclers), and NOK/MWh of power sold (Hydro Energy).

## 3. Value Creation Read

**Creates value at current pricing, with a plausible (but not fully proven) through-cycle case.** The segment's own return metric — Adjusted return on average capital employed (ARoaCE), "the profit it earns on the capital tied up in the business, after tax" — was **14.4% for Hydro Aluminium Metal in FY2025**, clearing the group's stated hurdle of >10% ARoaCE "over the course of a business cycle" [FY2025 Integrated Annual Report, Adjusted RoaCE per business area chart, p.33; Profitability roadmaps, p.36]. Cash margin per tonne is positive and rising (≈USD 532/mt FY2025, rising to ≈USD 980/mt by Q1 2026 per the quarterly deck), and the segment sits in the cheap 16th percentile of the global cost curve, meaning even at lower prices it should stay profitable relative to higher-cost competitors [FY2025 Integrated Annual Report, p.15]. That said, the segment's *own* differentiated cost-of-capital hurdle (the report states hurdles are "differentiated for each business area" but does not give the Aluminium Metal-specific number) is not disclosed — so the margin above hurdle cannot be pinned exactly, only inferred against the group-wide 10% floor. The single most valuable missing disclosure to firm this up is the segment-specific cost-of-capital / ARoaCE hurdle rate.

## 4. Sensitivity

The realized LME aluminium price is the input most likely to swing the value-creation read if it moves 20%: a 20% move on the FY2025 realized price (USD 2,573/mt) is roughly USD 515/mt — bigger than the entire FY2025 cash margin per tonne (≈USD 532/mt) and close to double the FY2024 margin (≈USD 441/mt), meaning a 20% price fall could push the unit close to break-even at the cash-margin level even before capex and other costs. This is also the historically most volatile input: the segment's reported EBIT margin ranged from 3.6% to 31.0% over FY2020-FY2025 (per `03_segment-map.md`), and the realized LME price itself moved from USD 2,552/mt to USD 2,996/mt within calendar 2025 alone, a swing the annual report attributes partly to "an increased share of speculative trading" and a "bullish copper market" rather than purely fundamental supply/demand [FY2025 Integrated Annual Report, Market development and outlook, p.27]. By contrast, the implied all-in cash cost per tonne moved a smaller ≈11% (USD 2,225-2,500/mt) across the three most recent quarters disclosed — cost is the more stable side of the unit-economics equation, price is the volatile one.

## 2. Input Cost Pass-Through

Hydro does not "pass through" its two biggest input costs in the retail sense — it buys and sells the same commodity, so cost and revenue move together almost mechanically, but on different clocks. Alumina bought or sold externally is priced "with reference to alumina spot price indexes" with a stated one-month delay, so a spike or drop in the alumina index shows up in Hydro's books roughly a month later on both sides of the ledger [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, Note 8.2, p.186-187]. Energy for smelters is mostly locked in on long-term contracts (up to 20 years, "mainly at fixed prices"), which insulates Hydro from short-term power-price swings on the input side but also means it cannot renegotiate down quickly if power prices fall, and — as the Tomago (Australia) full impairment shows — cannot always renew on comparable terms when a contract nears expiry ("the full write-down reflects the significant uncertainty regarding future power prices beyond the current power price agreements expiring in 2028") [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, impairment discussion / p.15]. About 25% of Aluminium Metal's alumina consumption is fixed 2-3 years ahead under Hydro's "Integrated hedge program," and since 2025 that fixed-price slice is also linked to the caustic-soda price — a contractual escalator of sorts, but on the input side, not something charged to a customer [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, Note 1.4, p.186]. On the output side there is no true "pass-through clause" to customers for base metal price — customers simply pay the LME price plus a premium at the time of sale; the only discretionary lever is the size of the premium Hydro can command for value-add/low-carbon product, and management states there are "limited possibilities for hedging future premiums" [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, Note 8.2, p.187]. Extrusions, the one segment with genuinely negotiated (non-index) pricing, has not been able to use that negotiating room to defend margin: its reported EBIT margin fell from roughly 4% in 2020-2023 to -2.2% in 2025 even as management cut costs and proposed closing five European plants [`03_segment-map.md`, §1].

**Supplier / input concentration (quantify).** The pool does not disclose a single-supplier or top-3-supplier percentage of COGS or purchases — Hydro reports only that its supplier base spans "approximately 39,000" vendors globally, with no spend concentration by vendor stated [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, "Workers in the value chain," p.126]. Where the report does quantify concentration, it is by country/region of input, not by vendor: caustic soda for Alunorte's refining is sourced from the USA (a single-country dependency); coal for Alunorte is sourced from mines in Colombia (a single-country dependency); anodes are sourced from Europe, Norway and China; extrusion ingot is sourced across the Americas, Asia, Europe and the Middle East [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, "Workers in the value chain," p.119]. On bauxite — the one input Hydro is largely self-sufficient in — "close to 85 percent" of total (direct plus indirect) bauxite sourcing traces back to mines in Brazil and Australia, with "the third largest indirect bauxite source, at approximately 6 percent," coming from two mines in Guinea, a jurisdiction the report says Hydro "monitors... continuously" given "the challenging situation in Guinea" [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, p.119-120]. None of these is disclosed as a percentage of COGS or purchase spend, so the size of the dependency in cost terms is **not proven from available data** — but the single-country concentration in caustic soda (USA) and coal (Colombia) for the Alunorte refinery, and the Guinea bauxite exposure, are the closest things to a material single-source dependency this pool surfaces, and they carry into §5 below.

## 3. Customer Pricing Power

Hydro cannot raise the base price of aluminium or alumina — that price is set on the LME and by public alumina indexes, not by Hydro — so "pricing power" for this company means the size of the premium it can add on top, and the evidence is genuinely mixed. On value-add/standard-ingot premiums, Hydro is a price-taker on the market-wide level (US Section 232 tariffs lifted the Midwest premium Hydro realizes "from just over $2,000 per tonne at the end of '25 to north of $2,500" by end-Q1 2026, and European duty-paid premiums moved from $335 to $587 in the same quarter) — these are policy- and supply-shock-driven, not company-specific pricing actions [Q1 2026 transcript, Apr-29-2026, prepared remarks, p.4-5]. Where Hydro does show an actual pricing action with a volume result is its low-carbon product line: sales of Hydro CIRCAL (recycled) and Hydro REDUXA (low-carbon primary) reached 58 thousand tonnes and 470 thousand tonnes respectively in FY2025 (roughly a quarter of FY2025 primary-aluminium production of 2,065 kmt combined), and the company states this delivered "a more than 50 percent increase in greener products earnings in 2025 versus 2024" — evidence of real, incremental pricing power tied to a differentiated, certified product rather than the commodity itself [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, p.106-107 ("Progressing on low-carbon aluminium volumes")]. Outside that green-premium niche, Extrusions — the one segment that actually negotiates prices with individual customers — could not defend margin through 2024-2025 (EBIT margin -2.2% in 2025), the clearest evidence that customer pricing power is weak once you leave the commodity-price mechanism [`03_segment-map.md`, §1].

## 4. Economic Control Verdict

**Mixed.** On the input side Hydro is structurally advantaged — it owns its own bauxite mine supplying close to 100% of two major refinery/smelter chains, generates roughly half its own hydropower and locks in most of the rest on contracts running up to 25 years, and this combination places its smelting on the "16th percentile of the global cost curve" (cheaper than 84% of global competitors) [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, p.14, 119-120]. But on the output side, across every stage that touches an external customer — LME-priced primary metal, index-priced alumina, near-zero-margin Metal Markets, and now negative-margin Extrusions — Hydro is a price-taker with only a narrow, growing lever (low-carbon product premiums) to push back against a market it does not set.

## 5. The Single Biggest Bargaining Risk

The dependency on external power-supply contracts for smelters outside Hydro's own captive Nordic hydropower base: the Tomago (Australia) smelter was already fully written down because of "significant uncertainty regarding future power prices beyond the current power price agreements expiring in 2028," showing how fast losing a favorable power-supplier relationship can destroy asset value once Hydro is not the one generating the electricity.

## 4. Read

Norsk Hydro is a vertically-integrated commodity aluminium producer, not a durable compounder: nearly every dollar of revenue and most of its cost base are set by the LME aluminium price, the alumina index price, and the USD/NOK exchange rate — none of which Hydro controls [02_business-identity.md §2, §4] — and that price-taking shows up directly in the numbers, with group ROE collapsing from 24.9% (2022) to 2.6% (2023) and back to 7.7% (2025) [Norsk Hydro ASA OB NHY Financials.xls, Ratios tab]. FY2025's reported returns (ROE 7.7%, EBITDA margin 24.7%) sit roughly mid-cycle relative to this five-year band — well below the 2022 price-spike peak (ROE 24.9%, EBITDA margin 28.5%) and well above the 2023 trough (ROE 2.6%) — so the headline FY2025 figures should be read as a mid-cycle point, not a run-rate, and a buyer should not extrapolate either the 2022 peak or the 2023 trough forward. The industry rate-of-change row scored 68 (above the ≤40 threshold), so this is not a technology-cycle bet; it is a cyclical, price-taking commodity business whose main quality risk is the interaction of thin pricing power (score 18) with heavy fixed assets (capital intensity score 28) — a combination that turns commodity-price swings directly into large ROE and segment-margin swings, as already seen in the FY2023 trough and the Q1 2026 alumina-vs-aluminium rotation. The single factor a buyer should watch over the next 24 months is Cyclicality (score 15): whether the LME aluminium price and the alumina-vs-aluminium spread stay near FY2025's mid-cycle level, revert toward the 2023 trough, or extend the current Aluminium Metal-led upswing, given Q1 2026 already showed an 85% quarter-on-quarter Adjusted EBITDA collapse in Bauxite & Alumina against a near-doubling in Aluminium Metal [03_segment-map.md, segment table].

## 3. Competitive Position

Position vs peers: not disclosed as a clean market-share trend, but two proxies exist. First, Hydro states it "produces 30 percent of all primary aluminium in Europe" [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, p.645-646] and holds "14 percent [share] in Europe and 18 percent" for Hydro Extrusions in an unspecified second geography [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, p.732] — both are point-in-time shares with no prior-year comparison given, so a gaining/losing read cannot be made from them alone. Second, Hydro Extrusions itself is contracting, not growing: five European plants were proposed for closure in FY2025 with impairment/restructuring charges booked, and the segment's EBIT margin fell from roughly 4% (2020-2023) to -2.2% (2025) [`03_segment-map.md`, Hydro Extrusions row] — a proxy for share loss or capacity rationalization in the downstream business, even without a stated share number. Net read: Position vs peers in primary aluminium — not disclosed as a share trend; margin data supports a cost-position edge, and Extrusions shows proxy evidence of contraction, not gain.

## 4. Competitive Shape

The global primary aluminium and alumina industry is oligopoly-leaning at the top but has a long fragmented tail: Hydro's own comparable-company set spans only 10 credible aluminium-focused or aluminium-adjacent public peers worldwide [Company Comparable Analysis Norsk Hydro ASA.xls, Business Description tab], and the largest of them (Chalco, Shandong Hongqiao, RUSAL, Hindalco) are each state-linked, closely-held, or geographically concentrated rather than globally diversified challengers to Hydro's specific European/Brazilian footprint. Within Europe specifically, Hydro's disclosed 30% share of regional primary-aluminium production [integrated-annual-report-2025.pdf, p.645-646] indicates a concentrated regional structure (Hydro plus a small number of others make up the bulk of European supply), even though globally the industry (led by Chinese producers such as Chalco and Hongqiao, which together already show combined LTM revenue of roughly USD 59.3bn against Hydro's USD 20.9bn [Company Comparable Analysis Norsk Hydro ASA.xls, Financial Data tab]) is better described as several large national-scale producers competing without a single dominant global leader — no HHI or top-N global share figure is available in this pool to size the global structure precisely.

## 5. Caveat

Global primary-aluminium production shares (Hydro's absolute global tonnage as a percentage of world primary aluminium output) are not disclosed anywhere in this pool — only the regional (Europe, 30%) figure is given. A global HHI or top-5-producer share figure would sharpen the "competitive shape" read in §4. Peer return-on-capital figures (ROE/ROIC/ROCE) for RUSAL, Chalco and Hindalco are sourced from third-party web aggregators, not each company's own primary annual filing (which is outside this data pool) — these are dated 2026-07-19 and labelled unverified; the `moat` agent should treat them as directional, not audited, and prefer the CIQ-sourced margin and leverage figures above them where precision matters.

## 5. Moat Verdict

**Narrow moat.** The strongest, best-evidenced advantage is **cost advantage / natural resource access**, combined score reflecting captive bauxite (Paragominas, ~100% of two smelter/refinery chains) and captive/long-contract hydropower, which place Hydro's primary smelting at the 16th percentile of the global cost curve — genuinely hard for RUSAL, Chalco or Hindalco to replicate without owning equivalent captive resources. But the hard rule applies here: the group's return on capital sits only modestly above its cost-of-capital proxy through the cycle (13.3% vs 10%, +330bps) and is at or below it on the latest year depending on which return figure (Adjusted vs Reported) is used — this is not the clear, durable margin the "Strong moat" bar requires, and it is entirely carried by the upstream/Energy segments while Metal Markets and Extrusions sit below cost of capital with no evidenced moat source of their own. The durability test over the next 5 years: can the 16th-percentile cost position and captive-resource base be defended as Chinese and Russian-linked producers (Chalco, Hongqiao, RUSAL) expand or as Norwegian hydropower concessions approach their ~2050 reversion point, while Extrusions either stabilizes or continues shedding capacity. Industry rate-of-change scored 68/100 in `07_business-quality.md` (above the ≤40 fast-changing threshold), so no additional disruption-based durability discount applies — this is a slow-moving, capital-cycle industry, not a technology-cycle bet.

**Moat trajectory: eroding.** At the whole-company level the trajectory points down, not sideways: Hydro Extrusions' EBIT margin fell from ~4% (2020-2023) to -2.2% (2025), triggering five proposed European plant closures [`03_segment-map.md`, Extrusions row] — a segment losing, not gaining, competitive ground. The upstream cost-curve position (16th percentile) has no disclosed multi-year trend in this pool to confirm it is widening, and `07_business-quality.md`'s competitive-intensity row (score 45/100) flags that "a mid-cost-curve competitor a few years from now is a live possibility, not a tail risk" given global overcapacity dynamics — a stable-to-slightly-eroding read for the segment that actually carries the moat. Combined, the group-level trajectory is judged eroding, driven by the downstream segment's real, evidenced decline and a caution flag (not yet a trend) on the upstream cost position.

## 3. Classification

**Mostly externally driven.** Hydro's earnings move first and most on the LME aluminium price, the alumina price/index it is contractually tied to, the USD/NOK exchange rate, power prices, EU carbon policy, and — as Q1 2026 showed in real time — geopolitical supply shocks and US tariff policy. Management's levers are real but narrow: forward hedging on the LME (up to about three years), NOK-denominated debt to partly match the cost base, a lower-cost position on the industry cost curve, a higher mix of value-add/recycled product versus standard ingot, and operational flexibility (curtailment) in a downturn. None of these levers changes the direction of the LME price, the carbon price, or a currency move — they only cushion the size of the swing.

## 4. External Dependency Risk Score

**74/100** (this score is INVERTED — higher = worse / more dangerous external dependence).

Rationale: the FX sensitivity alone (NOK 4,900m per NOK 1.00 move, ~17% of FY2025 Adjusted EBITDA) and the commodity/geopolitics evidence (a single quarter's LME move of over USD 400/mt driven by a Middle East supply shock) show that quarter-to-quarter results are dominated by variables management does not set. The score sits in the 61-80 "mostly externally driven, limited management levers" band rather than the 81-100 "pure pass-through" band because Hydro does have a genuine, evidenced cost-curve advantage (competitive, partly self-generated hydropower) and an active hedging program that measurably dampens — though does not eliminate — the swings.

## 5. The Single Biggest Lever

The USD/NOK exchange rate: Hydro's revenue is set in US dollars (LME-linked) while its dominant cost base is in Norwegian kroner, and the company's own disclosed sensitivity — NOK 4,900 million of Adjusted EBITDA per NOK 1.00 move in USD/NOK — implies that a 20% adverse move (a materially stronger NOK, roughly NOK 2.00 off the FY2025 realized rate of 10.39) would swing Adjusted EBITDA by close to NOK 9.8 billion, more than a third of FY2025 Adjusted EBITDA of NOK 28,889m, on a factor Hydro cannot control and only partially hedges through the currency of its debt [FY2025 Annual Report, p.38, "Key financial exposures"].
