# Business Model Reality Check — DHER (Synthesis)

## Abstract

Delivery Hero runs local food-delivery and quick-commerce apps (talabat, Baemin, foodpanda, Glovo, PedidosYa) in roughly 70 countries, taking a commission on restaurant orders while increasingly buying and reselling groceries itself through its own warehouses. Its Middle East business, run mostly through the 80%-owned talabat, produces most of the group's profit — 68.3% of FY2024 Adjusted EBITDA (€472.9m of €692.5m) at a 13.4% segment margin [FY24 Annual Report, "Key Figures," p.4] — while group-wide return on capital still sits far below the company's own 10.7%+ disclosed cost of capital (roughly 0.8%–1.6% in the best year, FY2025, and about −6.1% averaged over the last five years). The controlling negative: KPMG's audit report carries a going-concern-level "material uncertainty" note tied to the Glovo Spain subsidiary and up to €770m of rider-classification liability [FY24 Annual Report, Auditor's Report, p.222–223], which locks this review at avoid deeper work regardless of the Middle East segment's relative strength.

## 5. Simple Summary

- **What it does:** Runs local food-delivery and quick-commerce apps (talabat, Baemin, foodpanda, Glovo, PedidosYa) in about 70 countries, plus its own small warehouses (Dmarts) that it stocks and sells from directly.
- **How it makes money:** A cut of restaurant/shop orders (commission) as an agent in the marketplace business, and close to the full sale price as the seller of record in quick commerce, plus a fast-growing advertising layer (~€1.5bn revenue run rate) sold to restaurants and brands.
- **Whether each new unit creates value:** Cannot be proven from filings — DHER discloses no order count, average order value, or per-order acquisition cost for any segment; the best available proxy (MENA Adjusted EBITDA/GMV) points toward value creation in MENA specifically, not group-wide.
- **Which segment matters most:** MENA (via talabat), which generates 68.3% of Group Adjusted EBITDA at the group's best and improving margin, even though the larger Asia segment brings in more revenue.
- **Whether it has a moat, and against whom:** No group-level moat proven — return on capital sits below the company's own cost of capital every year for the last five years. A real, but narrow and segment-confined, cost/scale edge exists in MENA against Uber's own Middle East business and against smaller rival Jahez.
- **What external variables it depends on:** Currency swings and hyperinflation accounting across roughly 85% of non-euro revenue, and rider-employment regulation across multiple jurisdictions — the same exposure behind the auditor's going-concern note — plus, right now, whether merger-control regulators approve the announced Uber acquisition.
- **Whether capital is allocated well:** No — a serial-acquirer pattern has produced ~€2.05bn of cumulative goodwill write-downs since 2021, shares outstanding rose ~50% over five years with no offsetting buyback, and a large unrecognized contingent liability (up to €770m) sits off the balance sheet.
- **Whether it deserves deeper work:** No — the auditor's going-concern note on the Glovo Spain subsidiary locks this at "avoid deeper work" regardless of the Middle East segment's relative strength.

# Business Identity — DHER

## 1. What The Company Actually Does

Delivery Hero runs local smartphone-app marketplaces that connect end customers, restaurants/shops, and delivery riders, and separately runs its own small warehouses (called Dmarts) that it stocks and delivers from directly. In the marketplace part of the business, a customer opens an app under a local brand — talabat in the Middle East, Baemin in South Korea, foodpanda across Asia, PedidosYa in Latin America, Glovo/foodora/efood in Europe — orders from a restaurant or shop listed on that app, and a rider (self-employed, or employed by a third-party logistics firm, the platform itself, or the restaurant, depending on the market) delivers it [FY24 Annual Report, p.100]. The company takes a cut of each order rather than owning the food or goods being sold — it calls itself an "agent" for accounting purposes [FY24 Annual Report, p.100; Delivery_Hero_SE-Annual_Report(Apr-25-2025).pdf, Note on revenue recognition]. In the second, faster-growing part of the business — quick commerce, run through the Dmarts network in 48 countries — Delivery Hero buys and holds the groceries, electronics, and household items itself and sells them directly to the customer, so here it is the seller of record ("principal"), not just the middleman [FY24 Annual Report, p.100]. The company operates in around 70 countries across Asia, the Middle East and Africa (MENA), Europe, and Latin America, and is headquartered in Berlin, Germany [FY24 Annual Report, p.100]. The customer pays to solve a convenience problem — getting a meal, groceries, or an everyday item delivered to their door within roughly 20–60 minutes without leaving home — and restaurants and shops pay Delivery Hero for order volume, a ready-made delivery fleet, and increasingly for advertising space inside the app [FY24 Annual Report, p.100; FY2025 Earnings Call, Mar 26, 2026, prepared remarks].

## 2. How The Company Makes Money

Delivery Hero has two different revenue formulas sitting side by side, because it plays two different roles in its own marketplace:

- **Marketplace / agent model (restaurant and shop delivery):** `Revenue = order volume × commission rate + delivery fees + advertising & subscription fees`. The commission is "a contractually specified percentage of the order amount," and the percentage varies by country, restaurant type, and services bundled in (point-of-sale system, last-mile delivery, marketing support) [FY24 Annual Report, p.100].
- **Quick commerce / principal model (Dmarts):** `Revenue = Gross Merchandise Value (GMV) of goods sold, net of VAT`, i.e. Delivery Hero recognizes close to the full retail sale price because it owns the inventory being sold, not just a commission on it [FY24 Annual Report, p.100].
- **Advertising (AdTech), layered across both models:** a growing, separate revenue stream sold to restaurants and consumer-goods brands for placement inside the apps, described by management as approaching a "EUR 1.5 billion revenue run rate" and explicitly framed as reducing "reliance on commission alone" [FY2025 Earnings Call, Mar 26, 2026, prepared remarks].

The company's own internal scoreboard is Gross Merchandise Value (GMV) — "the total value paid by customers (including VAT, delivery fees, and service fees less other subsidies)" — which management uses "to assess performance, compare different business models, and manage the Group as a whole," because it strips out the difference between being an agent (commission only) and a principal (full sale price) [FY24 Annual Report, p.100 & p.101, footnote 3]. Group GMV was €48.8 billion in FY2024, split roughly 48% Asia, 26% MENA, 18% Europe, 8% Americas [FY24 Annual Report, p.4 (Group overview infographic)]. Total revenue was €14,059.8m in FY2025 versus €12,294.8m in FY2024 [Delivery Hero SE XTRA DHER Financials.xls, Segments tab]. Volume is driven by number of orders and average basket size; price/take-rate is driven by the mix shift toward the company's own delivery fleet ("own delivery" reached 78% of orders at group level in FY2025, up from 67% in FY2024, which raises revenue per order because Delivery Hero now captures the delivery fee itself rather than leaving it with a third party) and by the share of GMV coming from the higher-take-rate quick-commerce/Dmarts business, which alone did more than €7.5 billion in GMV in FY2025, up over 30% year-on-year [FY2025 Earnings Call, Mar 26, 2026, prepared remarks]. Margin is driven by rider/logistics cost per order, discount and voucher spend, and the mix of low-margin core delivery versus higher-margin advertising revenue.

## 3. Business Type Classification

A two-sided, app-based local-delivery marketplace that is converting itself into an owned-inventory quick-commerce retailer, with a fast-growing advertising layer bolted on top of both.

## 4. What Drives Variance

Revenue moves mainly with order volume (orders × basket size = GMV) and with the mix shift between commission-only delivery, owned-delivery (which adds the delivery fee to revenue), and quick-commerce/Dmarts (which adds the full sale price to revenue) — management states "revenue growth is expected to continue to exceed growth in GMV" specifically because of this mix shift, not because of price increases [FY2025 Earnings Call, Mar 26, 2026, prepared remarks]. Margin moves with rider/logistics cost per order, voucher and discount spend, and the growing weight of high-margin advertising revenue (nearing a €1.5bn run rate) against low-margin delivery. FX is a stated headwind: management flagged "USD and Korean won devaluation" as a drag on FY2026 revenue-versus-GMV comparisons, given the company reports in euros but earns heavily in Asia and MENA currencies [FY2025 Earnings Call, Mar 26, 2026, prepared remarks; FY24 Annual Report, risk section on functional-currency exposure]. Separately, and outside normal operating variance: Delivery Hero is the subject of an announced Uber Technologies acquisition offer (M&A call held Jul 16, 2026, expected close "second half of 2027," Prosus having "irrevocably committed to tender its stake") — this is a live corporate-action overlay on the standalone business and should not be read as a steady-state company at present [Uber Technologies, Inc., Delivery Hero SE - M&A Call, Jul 16, 2026].

## 2. Dominant Segment

**MENA is the dominant segment on profit — the metric that matters — even though Asia is larger on revenue.** MENA generated €472.9m of Adjusted EBITDA in FY2024, 68.3% of the Group's €692.5m total and the single largest segment contributor, at the best margin of any segment (13.4% Adj. EBITDA/segment revenue, up from 11.3% in 2023) [FY24 AR, "Key Figures" p.4]. Asia is larger by revenue (31.8% vs. MENA's 27.6% of Total Segment Revenue) but contributes less profit (55.6% vs. 68.3%) at a lower and flatter margin (9.5%, roughly unchanged from 10.3% in 2023) because of the competitive pressure described above [FY24 AR, p.4, p.105]. Per this module's rule, when revenue and profit shares disagree, the dominant segment is named by profit: **MENA**.

MENA does not clear the 85% threshold for "effectively single-segment" (it is 68.3% of profit and 27.6% of revenue), so Delivery Hero remains a genuinely multi-segment business — the other four segments together still generate 31.7% of Group Adj. EBITDA (and Asia alone is 55.6%, a large second contributor), and two segments (Europe, Integrated Verticals) are net drags on Group profit.

**FY2025 directional cross-check (unaudited, Tier 5/6 — not the primary basis for this finding):** per a Capital IQ workbook export tied to the Mar-26-2026 FY2025 earnings call, MENA's dominance appears to have widened in FY2025 — segment Adj. EBITDA of €546.0m (60.5% of the €902.8m segment-level sum) vs. Asia's €333.1m (36.9%), with Asia's Adj. EBITDA actually declining year-over-year [Delivery Hero SE XTRA DHER Financials.xls, Segments tab, Dec-31-2025 column]. This is consistent with, but not proof beyond, the FY2024 audited finding above; no audited FY2025 segment note is present in this pool to confirm it at Tier-1 quality.

## 3. Segment Disclosure Quality

Segment definitions are broadly consistent year-over-year — the same five segments (Asia, MENA, Europe, Americas, Integrated Verticals) appear across FY2020–FY2024 in both the Annual Report and the Capital IQ financials export — but the underlying historical figures have been restated or reclassified repeatedly: the Capital IQ segment history is labeled "Restated" for FY2020–FY2022 and "Reclassified" for FY2023, meaning the segment revenue/Adj. EBITDA figures reported for a given historical year have changed across successive annual filings [Delivery Hero SE XTRA DHER Financials.xls, Segments tab, column headers]. There is no discrete "Other" or "Corporate" segment row in the reportable-segment table itself, but a large centralized reconciling bucket sits between segment profit and Group statutory profit: "Unallocated Management Adjustments" (−€511.9m in FY2024) plus "Unallocated Expenses for Share-Based Compensation" (−€171.1m in FY2024) total −€683.0m — almost exactly offsetting the €692.5m of segment-level Adjusted EBITDA — which is why the Group's audited IFRS operating result was actually a loss of −€341.3m and net result a loss of −€881.7m in FY2024, despite segment Adjusted EBITDA showing a €692.5m profit [Delivery Hero SE XTRA DHER Financials.xls, Segments tab; FY24 AR, p.107, "Consolidated Statement of Profit or Loss"]. This is the single biggest disclosure-quality issue: **profit is disclosed at segment level only on a heavily adjusted, non-IFRS basis** (Adjusted EBITDA is defined by management to exclude share-based compensation, legal/antitrust and courier-reclassification expenses, restructuring costs, corporate-transaction costs, and IFRS 16 right-of-use depreciation [FY24 AR, p.101, footnote 5]), and the bridge back to audited IFRS profit is a large, unallocated, Group-level item that the segment table gives no way to attribute to any one segment. A second disclosure gap specific to MENA: the segment consolidates 100% of talabat's results even though DH holds only an 80% economic interest following talabat's December 2024 Dubai listing, so the segment table alone cannot be used to size DHER shareholders' actual claim on MENA profit without a further minority-interest adjustment [FY24 AR, p.106]. No FY2025 audited annual report or segment note is present in this pool; FY2025 segment figures used above are sourced only from a Capital IQ workbook export tied to a Mar-26-2026 earnings-call transcript (Tier 5/6), not an audited filing.

# Unit Economics — DHER

Basis: Delivery Hero SE (XTRA:DHER), Prime Standard / Frankfurt Stock Exchange, IFRS as adopted by the EU, EUR, fiscal year ending Dec-31. Figures below are FY2024 (year ended Dec-31-2024) from the audited FY2024 Annual Report (published Apr 25, 2025) unless stated otherwise. FY2025 figures are explicitly labeled Capital IQ workbook / earnings-transcript sourced (Tier 5/6, per `analyses/DHER_2026-08-12/business-model/00_data-triage.md`) — no audited FY2025 annual report or `ciq_facts.json` sidecar exists in this pool.

Upstream input used: `03_segment-map.md` (present) identifies **MENA** as the dominant segment on profit — 68.3% of Group Adjusted EBITDA (€472.9m of €692.5m) at the best segment margin (13.4% Adj. EBITDA/Segment Revenue, up from 11.3%), even though Asia is larger by revenue [FY24 Annual Report, "Key Figures" p.4]. This report builds the unit-economics table for MENA.

## 1. Natural Unit

The natural economic unit for MENA is **the order** — a single food-delivery or quick-commerce transaction placed through a MENA platform brand (talabat, Hungerstation, InstaShop, or Yemeksepeti) [FY24 Annual Report, Combined Management Report, A.3 "Segments", p.100–101]. Delivery Hero itself frames its economics this way: "GMV is influenced by the number of orders, as well as average basket size per order" [FY24 Annual Report, p.5081-region text].

A second, structurally different unit exists in the Integrated Verticals segment: the **Dmart (quick-commerce) order**, where Delivery Hero acts as principal — buying and reselling inventory through its own warehouses rather than earning a commission — giving that unit a merchandise-margin economic structure instead of MENA's commission-based one [FY24 Annual Report, p.101].

## 2. Unit Economics Table

**Caveat before the table: Delivery Hero does not disclose an absolute order count, an average order value in currency, or a segment-level customer-acquisition cost anywhere in the pool — for MENA or for the Group.** The only segment-level per-volume metric it discloses is Adjusted EBITDA as a percentage of GMV (a monetary-basket proxy, not a literal per-order figure). Most rows below are therefore "Not disclosed" at the true unit level; where a Group-level or GMV-normalized proxy exists, it is shown and labeled as a proxy, not as the per-order number the row asks for.

## 3. Value Creation Read

**Unclear at the true per-order level — but the one disclosed proxy points toward value creation, not destruction.** MENA's Adjusted EBITDA / GMV margin rose from 3.1% to 3.7% in FY2024 while segment GMV grew +28.8% [FY24 Annual Report, "Key Figures" p.4] — the only segment combining double-digit volume growth with an already meaningfully positive relative margin (Asia's is 1.6%, and Europe/Integrated Verticals are negative). That is consistent with each incremental order adding profit rather than diluting it. But Delivery Hero discloses no order count, no average order value, and no acquisition cost tied to a single order or a single new customer for MENA (or the Group), so the contribution-margin-×-lifetime-vs-acquisition-cost math this section is meant to test cannot actually be built from filings — only inferred from a GMV-normalized EBITDA margin, which is a coarser proxy. **The single most valuable missing disclosure is MENA-segment (or even Group-level) absolute order count and average order value**, which would let revenue-per-order and a real per-order contribution margin be computed directly instead of inferred.

**Peak-cycle ring-fence:** FY2024's MENA acceleration (+28.8% GMV, +55.3% Adj. EBITDA, margin +0.6pp) coincided with talabat's Dec-2024 Dubai Financial Market listing and management's own comment that MENA "performed significantly better than expected" [FY24 Annual Report, p.4]. The FY2025 cross-check (Tier 5, unaudited, Capital IQ workbook) shows MENA revenue growth decelerating sharply to +14.3% (€3,527.8m → €4,033.7m) while the Adj. EBITDA/revenue margin only held flat (13.4% → ~13.5%), not expanding further [Delivery Hero SE XTRA DHER Financials.xls, Segments tab]. Read the 3.1%→3.7% GMV-margin improvement as a strong-year result that stabilized rather than compounded further in FY2025 — not yet proven as a structurally rising margin.

## 4. Sensitivity

The single input most likely to swing this read if it moved 20% is **delivery cost per order** (the largest and most volatile cost-of-sales line). Group delivery expenses grew 33.9% in FY2024 (€4,496.0m → €6,022.1m) — faster than the 23.7% growth in Group revenue — and management attributed part of the excess directly to "intensified competition in certain regions, which led to increased logistic expenses" [FY24 Annual Report, B.3 "Results of Operations", p.106]. Delivery expenses represent 67.2% of Group cost of sales (2023: 64.5%) [FY24 Annual Report, p.106], so a 20% adverse move in per-order delivery cost — the same dynamic already visible in Asia, where GMV fell −7.7% under competitive pressure and margin stayed flat despite revenue growth [FY24 Annual Report, p.105–106] — would compress MENA's 3.7% Adj. EBITDA/GMV margin far more than an equivalent move in marketing spend (€484.8m, already falling) or IT costs (1.1% of GMV). A second, structural sensitivity specific to MENA: the segment table consolidates 100% of talabat's results even though DH holds only an 80% economic interest post-listing [FY24 Annual Report, p.106] — so roughly a fifth of any MENA per-unit profit improvement accrues to talabat's public minority shareholders, not to DHER shareholders directly, which the GMV-margin proxy above does not adjust for.

## 2. Input Cost Pass-Through

Delivery Hero's largest input cost — delivery expenses (external riders, third-party logistics, and DH's own rider fleet) — rose to 67.2% of total cost of sales in FY2024, up from 64.5% in FY2023, and cost of sales grew 28.6% year-over-year versus revenue growth of 23.7% [FY24 Annual Report, p.108–109]. The filing does not describe any contractual pass-through mechanism, escalator, or indexed-pricing clause tying commission or delivery-fee rates to rider cost inflation; instead, management's stated levers are mix shift (own-delivery capturing the fee itself) and operational efficiency ("order stacking levels" and logistics centralization "help to bring down the cost per order") [FY24 Annual Report, p.135]. Where a cost shock was regulatory rather than commercial, DH did not pass it through — it absorbed it: the Europe segment's FY2024 Adjusted EBITDA miss versus the Group's own 2024 target range (€692.5m actual vs. €725–775m guided) was specifically attributed to "additional expenses recognized for rider-related reclassification risks in Italy," and Europe's segment margin stayed negative (−4.1% Adjusted EBITDA/segment revenue) [FY24 Annual Report, p.4, p.106; Segment Map, §1]. The one demonstrated lever that functions like a price increase is discount reduction: vouchers fell from 8.1% to 6.9% of Total Segment Revenue in FY2024 while GMV still grew 7.7%, showing DH can pull back subsidies without an immediate, filing-visible volume loss at group level [FY24 Annual Report, p.109].

**Supplier / input concentration (quantify).** The dominant, quantified input dependency is on external/freelance labor for delivery, not on a raw-material supplier: 93.7% of FY2024 delivery expense (€5,644.3m of €6,022.2m) went to external riders and third-party logistics providers rather than DH's own employed fleet (€377.9m, 6.3%) [FY24 Annual Report, p.109]. This is a material single-category dependency — the company runs its core last-mile function overwhelmingly through a workforce it does not directly employ, which is precisely the population subject to the reclassification risk described in §1 and §5. For the Dmarts principal business, no supplier concentration is disclosed: there is no figure in the pool for the largest goods supplier's share of Dmarts cost of sales, no single-source input identified, and no wholesale-terms disclosure — "Not proven from available data," and this is itself a disclosure gap rather than evidence of low concentration.

## 3. Customer Pricing Power

The evidence is regionally split rather than uniform. In DH's strongest market, MENA (talabat), the company both raised its margin and pulled back discounting: MENA's Adjusted EBITDA/segment revenue rose to 13.4% in FY2024 from 11.3% in 2023, and management called the segment's performance "significantly better than expected" [FY24 Annual Report, p.4, p.105]. Loyalty tools reinforce this — subscription penetration reached up to 61% of GMV in Saudi Arabia by Q1 2026, and in Korea the newly relaunched subscription program is "nearly 50% of total order volumes" [Q1 2026 Trading Statement Call; FY2025 Earnings Call, Mar 26, 2026, Q&A]. Set against that, the Asia segment's GMV fell 7.7% in FY2024 "mainly driven by rising competition in certain regions," and Adjusted EBITDA there stayed flat at a lower 9.5% margin despite 9.2% revenue growth — direct evidence that DH lost volume rather than holding price when a rival undercut it in a specific market [FY24 Annual Report, p.4, p.105–106]. No explicit list-price or commission-rate increase in the last 24 months is disclosed in the pool with an associated volume read; the only quantified pricing-adjacent action is the voucher pull-back described in §2, which management ties to "improved" margins without a stated volume cost.

## 4. Economic Control Verdict

**Mixed.**

Delivery Hero sets price and captures rising margin in its dominant regional market (MENA, 13.4% Adjusted EBITDA/segment revenue and rising) and has built a fast-growing, apparently price-setting advertising layer (nearing a €1.5bn run rate) that management explicitly uses to reduce dependence on restaurant commissions [FY24 Annual Report, p.4, p.105; FY2025 Earnings Call, Mar 26, 2026]. But it is a price-taker in its second-largest segment by revenue (Asia, where GMV fell 7.7% on competition), in its fastest-growing new business line (Dmarts/quick commerce, still at a −3.6% segment margin), and — structurally — over the labor input that makes up two-thirds of its cost of sales, where 93.7% of delivery spend runs through external/freelance riders now facing an active, multi-jurisdiction reclassification threat [FY24 Annual Report, p.4, p.106, p.109, p.126–128].

## 5. The Single Biggest Bargaining Risk

Rider employment reclassification: with 93.7% of delivery expense running through external/freelance riders and a disclosed Spain contingent liability of €440.0m–€770.0m on top of an already-triggered Italy provision, a broader shift from freelance to employed rider status across the EU or Latin America would convert DH's largest, currently most controllable cost line into a much less flexible, regulator-set one [FY24 Annual Report, p.109, p.126–128, p.203–204].

1. **Relative to peers.** Company sits at the **bottom** of named peers on group-level margin and return on capital: DHER's FY2025 EBIT margin (0.7%) trails both DoorDash (5.3%, GAAP-profitable for the first time in FY2025) and Uber (10.7%) by a wide margin, and DHER's group ROIC (0.8%–1.6%, best year) is a fraction of Uber's ROE (~35.8%, though inflated by a one-off) and below DoorDash's ROE (~9.3%) [`08_competitive-map.md`, §2]. The one place DHER does NOT sit at the bottom is its own MENA segment specifically: its 13.4% segment Adjusted EBITDA margin exceeds the ~7% EBITDA margin Uber's CEO placed on Uber's comparable Middle East business, and Jahez (the smallest named peer) reported a 61.1% YoY net-profit decline on "elevated promotional activity" in the same period DHER's MENA margin expanded [`08_competitive-map.md`, §2–3] — a segment-level, not group-level, position of strength.
2. **Absolute (the economic moat test).** The company earns a return on capital **below** its cost of capital — 0.8%–1.6% (best year) to roughly −6.1% (through-cycle average) versus a company-disclosed 10.7%–13.7%+ WACC. This is the decisive test, and it fails by a wide margin at the group level.

## 5. Moat Verdict

**No moat proven.**

The strongest candidate for an advantage — scale and distribution density in the MENA segment via talabat, evidenced by a 13.4% segment margin that beats the closest comparable Uber Middle East figure (~7%) and appears to be pressuring smaller rival Jahez on price — is real but confined to one of four segments and has not translated into a group-level return on capital anywhere close to Delivery Hero's own disclosed 10.7%–13.7% cost of capital. Per the hard rule in this agent's mandate: a return on capital at or below cost of capital caps the verdict below "Strong moat" regardless of peer-relative superiority — DHER's group ROIC of roughly 0.8%–1.6% in its best-ever year, and roughly −6.1% through the last five years, is not merely below WACC, it is negative through-cycle, which rules out even "Narrow moat" at the group level. This is the textbook **moat in structure, not economics** case: a genuine scale/distribution asset in one segment (MENA) that the group's overall capital returns do not confirm. The durability test this would need to pass over the next five years is straightforward and hard: can the MENA segment's 13.4% margin keep expanding while own-delivery/Dmarts mix-shift (which has already dragged group gross margin from 29.9% to 24.4% over two years) and Asia's outright GMV decline (−7.7% on "rising competition") stop offsetting it — all inside an industry `07_business-quality.md` scores 26/100 on rate-of-change/disruption risk (≤40, triggering the CLAUDE.md §24 Filter 5 discount), where the winners are still being sorted by live M&A (DoorDash–Deliveroo, Uber–Delivery Hero itself) rather than by demonstrated, defensible economics.

**Moat trajectory — widening (narrow, segment-specific; group-level picture is mixed).** The quantitative trend that exists is a five-year climb in return on capital from −14.5% (FY2021) to +0.8% (FY2025) [Delivery Hero SE XTRA DHER Financials.xls, Ratios tab], and MENA segment margin specifically expanded from 11.3% to 13.4% over the same broad period even as GMV kept growing [FY24 Annual Report, p.4] — both are genuine widening signals, not noise. But this is not a clean, one-directional read: group gross margin has been falling (29.9% → 24.4%, FY2023–FY2025) as mix shifts toward lower-margin Dmarts/quick-commerce [Delivery Hero SE XTRA DHER Financials.xls, Ratios tab], Asia GMV fell outright (−7.7%) on rising competition in the same window DHER's own risk disclosures rate as "High" [FY24 Annual Report, p.105], and `07_business-quality.md` itself calls the FY2025 operating-profit inflection "fragile" and "not a cyclical peak to anchor against." The honest read is that the group's capital returns are rising off a very low, deeply negative base — evidence of a widening trend, but nowhere near evidence of a moat yet, and Asia is the clear counter-example showing the same trend running in reverse in a different segment of the same company.

## 3. Most Severe New Flag

The most material new flag is the pattern of disclosed data breaches: 8 separate incidents in FY2024 alone, all involving personal data, affecting 35,343 users, on a platform that processes payment and location data for millions of consumers across 70+ countries with materially varying data-protection regimes. This sits on top of — and is a distinct exposure from — the regulatory and litigation risks already flagged upstream (rider classification, EU antitrust); a GDPR enforcement action or a larger-scale breach would be a new, separate cost and reputational event, not a continuation of the risks other specialists already priced in. The synthesizer should weight this as a standing operational-security tail risk that current disclosure shows is recurring, not isolated, even though the company's own risk rating for it eased year over year.

## 4. Cross-Cutting Patterns

Three items in this sweep and in the upstream capital-allocation-governance report point the same direction: a business whose reported statutory profitability is being smoothed by non-operating, one-off items running in both directions across periods — the €220.9m Uber Taiwan breakup-fee gain and €99.3m debt-modification gain in FY2024, against the €259.7m goodwill impairment in FY2025 and the historical €2.05bn of cumulative goodwill write-downs from the acquisition program (already flagged upstream, Filter 4). None of these individually is hidden — each is disclosed and explained — but together they mean DHER's FY2024–FY2025 statutory net-loss trajectory and "path to profitability" narrative should be read through normalized, one-off-adjusted figures, not the headline statutory numbers, when assessing whether the underlying operating business has actually turned a corner.
