# Business Model Reality Check — BURL (Synthesis)

## Abstract

Burlington Stores is a U.S. off-price retailer that sells branded apparel, footwear, home goods and other merchandise through 1,287 physical stores. Its sole retail segment produces all reported revenue, with growth coming from new stores and from transactions and basket size at mature stores. The strongest positive is flexible in-season sourcing and an FY2025 gross-margin gain of 60 basis points to 43.8%. The central weakness is that store-level value creation remains unproven while FY2025 capital spending reached $1.104 billion, or 9.5% of revenue. No automatic disqualifier triggered, but the evidence supports an average business worth deeper work only if valuation is cheap.

- The main outside variables are low-income consumer spending, weather, tariffs, freight and overseas merchandise availability. [FY2025 Form 10-K, Item 1A, pp.6–11; Q2 FY2026 Form 10-Q, MD&A — Uncertainties and Challenges, p.22]
- Capital allocation is clean in form but demanding in substance: organic capex, leases and working capital absorb large amounts of cash. [FY2025 Form 10-K, Item 7 — Capital Expenditures; Q2 FY2026 Form 10-Q, Condensed Consolidated Balance Sheets]
- It deserves deeper work only if valuation pays for average quality, unproven store-level returns and a concentrated distribution network.

# Business Identity — BURL

## 1. What The Company Actually Does

Burlington Stores is a U.S. off-price retailer that sells branded apparel, footwear, accessories, beauty, home goods, toys, gifts and coats at physical stores, generally at lower prices than other retailers. [FY2025 Form 10-K, Item 1 — Business Overview] As of August 1, 2026, it operated 1,287 stores in 47 states, Washington, D.C., and Puerto Rico. [Q2 FY2026 Form 10-Q, MD&A — Overview] More than 99% of FY2025 net sales came from its Burlington stores. [FY2025 Form 10-K, Item 1 — Our Stores] The customer is a household shopper seeking branded, current-season merchandise at a lower cash price; management also reported that stores in lower-income trade areas grew comparable sales faster than the chain in Q2 FY2026, though the filing does not disclose the customer-income mix. [Q2 FY2026 earnings call, Q&A] Burlington’s merchants seek to buy most merchandise in season and can move purchases between suppliers and categories rather than rely on long-term supplier commitments. [FY2025 Form 10-K, Item 1 — Our Off-Price Sourcing and Merchandising Model] In practical terms, it sells a changing store assortment and price gap, not online delivery: the 10-K describes the business as exclusively brick-and-mortar. [FY2025 Form 10-K, Item 1A — Competition] The company reports in U.S. dollars under U.S. GAAP. [Q2 FY2026 Form 10-Q, Note 1 — Basis of Presentation]

## 2. How The Company Makes Money

`Net sales = operating Burlington stores × net sales per store`

`Comparable-store sales = customer transactions × average basket`

`Total sales growth = comparable-store sales growth + sales from net new stores − sales lost from closed stores`

Volume is driven by store openings and visits to mature stores; in Q2 FY2026, comparable-store sales rose 2%, mainly from a higher basket while transactions were roughly flat. [Q2 FY2026 earnings call, prepared remarks and Q&A] Price and mix are set by the merchandise selected, the value offered to shoppers, and markdowns; the company says comparable sales also move with weather, the economy, fresh receipts, promotions, consumer preferences, competition, and marketing. [Q2 FY2026 Form 10-Q, MD&A — Comparable Store Sales] Gross margin — the share of sales left after merchandise and delivery costs — depends on merchandise margin, purchase discounts and allowances, import fees, and inbound and distribution-center freight; Q2 FY2026 gross margin of 46.2% included a $55.5 million tariff-refund benefit, so that reported level is not a clean measure of recurring merchant economics. [Q2 FY2026 Form 10-Q, MD&A — Gross Margin]

## 3. Business Type Classification

U.S. off-price, store-based apparel and home retailer, with revenue driven by store rollout, transactions and basket size, and in-season value buying.

## 4. What Drives Variance

Total revenue moves first with the number of stores and then with comparable-store sales at mature stores. [Q2 FY2026 Form 10-Q, MD&A — Overview and Comparable Store Sales] At comparable stores, the company identifies weather, consumer spending, timing of fresh merchandise and promotions, consumer preferences, competition and marketing as drivers; the latest quarter’s 2% comparable-sales gain came mainly from basket size rather than transaction growth. [Q2 FY2026 Form 10-Q, MD&A — Comparable Store Sales; Q2 FY2026 earnings call, Q&A] Margin can move with merchandising and markdown execution, landed merchandise costs and freight, while tariff refunds can temporarily lift the reported number; the $55.5 million Q2 FY2026 refund is a clear example of a non-recurring driver. [Q2 FY2026 Form 10-Q, MD&A — Gross Margin] Weather matters especially for outerwear, although management says it has shifted fall planning toward more weather-neutral categories and localized assortments; this is a mitigation effort, not proof that weather sensitivity has disappeared. [Q2 FY2026 earnings call, Q&A]

## 1. Stages Occupied

## 2. Input Cost Pass-Through

| Axis | What it asks | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Contractual pass-through** | Is there an escalator, indexed-pricing, or cost-plus clause that moves price automatically? | **No automatic customer clause disclosed** — the filings disclose no retail cost-plus or indexed-pricing mechanism. Burlington's supplier purchases are generally purchase-order based, with no long-term purchase contracts; that supports flexibility upstream but does not prove that no customer contract could exist. | [FY2025 Form 10-K, Item 1A — Risk Factors: merchandise supply; Item 1 — Our Off-Price Sourcing and Merchandising Model] |

The prescribed calculation cannot be completed: `realised recovery = 1 − (observed margin impact ÷ disclosed gross pre-mitigation impact)`. Q2 FY2026 gross margin rose 250 basis points, from 43.7% to 46.2%; $55.5 million of tariff refunds reduced cost of sales. Against $2,997.8 million of Q2 sales, the refund equals about 185 basis points (`55.5 ÷ 2,997.8`), leaving about 65 basis points of the reported gross-margin increase attributed only to improved merchandise margin. The unreported gross tariff/input-cost impact makes neither the 250 basis points nor the 65-basis-point residual a recovery rate. The refund is also a customs recovery, not customer price pass-through. [Q2 FY2026 Form 10-Q, Note — Tariff Refunds; MD&A — Results of Operations and Gross Margin]

Burlington's disclosed mitigation is procurement and cost control, not an automatic price escalator: it buys most merchandise in season, shifts purchases among suppliers and categories, and expects better supplier terms to offset rising goods costs. Management also said it pivoted away from highly tariff-exposed categories in the second half of FY2025, particularly Home; this reduced tariff exposure but pressured sales. Product-sourcing costs improved by 20 basis points in Q2 and 30 basis points for the first half as a share of sales from supply-chain initiatives — cost absorption, not evidence that shoppers paid more. Conversely, management intends to reinvest the roughly $55 million Q2 tariff refund into lower prices / sharper value in the second half, so the latest explicit pricing action is a planned price investment, not a price increase. The company says it is not dependent on one supplier, but it does not disclose the largest supplier's share of purchases or cost of sales, the top-three share, or a sole-source critical input. It had $2,558.5 million of purchase commitments for goods not yet received at August 1, 2026, but the filing does not allocate those commitments by supplier. The underlying risk is merchandise availability rather than a named raw material: vendors can limit retail channels, revise terms, or reduce excess inventory; although Burlington buys a majority from U.S.-based vendors, production occurs primarily overseas and is exposed to tariff changes. [FY2025 Form 10-K, Item 1 — Our Off-Price Sourcing and Merchandising Model; Item 1A — Risk Factors: merchandise supply and international operations; Q2 FY2026 Form 10-Q, Commitments]
