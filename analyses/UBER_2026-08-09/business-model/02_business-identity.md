# Business Identity — UBER

## 1. What The Company Actually Does

Uber runs an app-based marketplace that connects people who need a ride, a meal, a grocery order, or a freight shipment moved with the independent drivers, couriers, merchants, and trucking carriers who supply that service. It does not own the cars, drive the deliveries, or own the trucks — it takes a cut of each transaction for matching supply and demand, processing payment, and running the routing/pricing software. As of December 31, 2025 the network covered more than 15,000 cities in over 70 countries, with roughly 34,000 employees supporting hundreds of millions of drivers, couriers, merchants, shippers, and carriers [FY25 10-K, p.1 / Item 1]. The company operates as three reportable segments — Mobility (ride-hailing), Delivery (restaurant, grocery, and retail delivery), and Freight (connecting shippers with trucking carriers) [FY25 10-K, Note 13 (Segment Information)]. Customers pay Uber to solve a coordination problem: finding a nearby driver, courier, or truck fast, at a predictable price, without negotiating or dispatching one themselves. A single cross-platform membership, Uber One, bundles discounts and perks across rides, delivery, and grocery for a subscription fee and had reached 46 million members by year-end 2025 [FY25 10-K, p.1]. In the fourth quarter of 2025, 202 million monthly active platform consumers ("MAPCs") completed at least one Mobility ride or Delivery order [FY25 10-K, p.49].

## 2. How The Company Makes Money

Uber's core formula is a take rate on the total dollar value that flows through its platform (which it calls "Gross Bookings" — total value of rides and deliveries booked, including any Freight revenue, before paying drivers/couriers/carriers):

- **Mobility / Delivery (marketplace segments):** `Revenue = Gross Bookings × Revenue Margin (take rate)`, where Revenue Margin is the service fee Uber keeps after paying drivers/couriers, defined by the company as revenue as a percentage of Gross Bookings [FY25 10-K, p.1 / Item 7A definitions]. For FY2025: Mobility revenue of $29,670 million on Mobility Gross Bookings of roughly $97,497 million (sum of quarterly Gross Bookings disclosed in the 10-K) implies a take rate of about 30% [FY25 10-K, p.58–59 (Certain Key Metrics) and Note 13]; Delivery revenue of $17,248 million on Delivery Gross Bookings of roughly $90,864 million implies a take rate of about 19% [same source]. Blended across all three segments, FY2025 revenue of $52,017 million on Gross Bookings of $193,454 million works out to an overall take rate of about 27% [FY25 10-K, p.49].
- **Freight (logistics-broker segment):** `Revenue = shipment volume × price per shipment`, recognized on a gross basis because Uber acts as principal — arranging and pricing the shipment for the shipper and then paying the carrier as a cost of revenue. Freight revenue effectively equals Freight Gross Bookings by the company's own metric definition ($5,099 million revenue on $5,093 million Gross Bookings in FY2025) [FY25 10-K, p.59 and Note 13].
- **Secondary streams inside the marketplace segments:** advertising sold to merchants and Drivers/Merchants (reported inside Mobility and Delivery revenue) and Uber One subscription fees, both add to Revenue Margin without adding to Gross Bookings volume [FY25 10-K, p.1 (Segment descriptions); Q2 FY26 transcript, prepared remarks].

What drives the volume side is Trips (13,567 million in FY2025, up 20% year-over-year) and MAPCs (202 million in Q4 FY25, up 18%) [FY25 10-K, p.49]. What drives the price/take-rate side is the service fee schedule Uber sets for drivers and merchants, plus how much of that fee is given back as driver incentives and consumer discounts/promotions, which are netted against revenue or booked as cost of revenue depending on the type [FY25 10-K, Note 1 (Revenue Recognition)]. Margin is then a function of how much of the take rate survives after platform-participant payments, incentives, insurance, and fixed platform/technology costs — Adjusted EBITDA margin on Gross Bookings rose to $8,730 million (about 4.5% of Gross Bookings) in FY2025 from $6,484 million in FY2024 [FY25 10-K, p.49].

## 3. Business Type Classification

Two-sided, multi-vertical on-demand marketplace platform (ride-hailing, delivery, and freight brokerage) monetized through a take rate on gross transaction value, with an emerging autonomous-vehicle commercialization layer.

Valuation norm for this business type: EV/EBITDA and EV/Gross-Bookings multiples plus a free-cash-flow-based DCF are the standard cross-checks — not a REIT/bank-style method, since Uber owns no real estate or loan book at scale.

## 4. What Drives Variance

Revenue and Adjusted EBITDA moves are driven first by Gross Bookings growth (a function of Trips and MAPCs) — FY2025 Gross Bookings rose 19% (20% constant-currency), driven by Mobility trip volumes (+19%) and Delivery trip volumes (+22%), while Freight Gross Bookings fell 1% on a weak freight cycle [FY25 10-K, p.49]. Second, the take rate itself moves with driver/courier incentive spend, insurance costs (Mobility), and one-off regulatory/accounting reclassifications — the UK Mobility business-model change cut reported Q2 FY26 revenue by $1.1 billion even though the underlying Gross Bookings kept growing at 24%, so a reported revenue "miss" can be a classification effect rather than a demand effect [FY26 Q2 10-Q, p.17094–17099]. Third, mix between segments matters because take rates differ sharply — Mobility runs at roughly a 30% take rate versus roughly 19% for Delivery and effectively 100% (pass-through) for Freight, so a shift in growth toward Delivery or Freight mechanically pulls the blended take rate down even if total Gross Bookings grow [author's calculation from FY25 10-K, p.58–59 and Note 13]. Finally, currency (Uber reports both as-reported and constant-currency growth) and discrete items such as the $5.0 billion Netherlands deferred-tax-asset valuation-allowance release and unrealized gains/losses on minority equity stakes (Aurora, Lucid, Didi, Waabi, Grab) can swing reported net income well away from the operating trend [FY25 10-K, p.49].
