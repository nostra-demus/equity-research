## 2. Input Cost Pass-Through

Uber's largest input cost — payments and incentives to Drivers and Couriers — moves mechanically with Gross Bookings rather than through a negotiated pass-through clause: FY2025 cost of revenue rose $4.7 billion (18%), made up of a $1.6 billion increase in Driver payments/incentives and a $1.6 billion increase in Courier payments/incentives, both simply tracking higher Mobility and Delivery Gross Bookings [FY25 10-K, MD&A — Cost of Revenue, FY2025 vs FY2024]. Insurance — Uber's second-largest controllable-but-volatile input — is not automatically passed through either way: in FY2025 a $851 million increase in insurance expense (driven by a higher insurance rate per mile and more miles driven) pressured Mobility Adjusted EBITDA [FY25 10-K, MD&A — Mobility Segment, FY2025 vs FY2024], while by Q2 FY26 the CFO described insurance as having "become a tailwind this year," with the company choosing to "reinvest the savings from insurance back into the market" — i.e., into lower effective prices and driver/consumer incentives in markets like California, Los Angeles, and San Francisco to drive trip growth, rather than banking the windfall as margin [Q2 FY26 transcript, CFO Balaji Krishnamurthy, prepared remarks]. This is a discretionary reinvestment decision, not a contractual escalator or indexed-pricing clause — no such clause is disclosed in this pool for either Mobility or Delivery. Freight is the one segment with mechanical, near-total pass-through: Carrier payments are booked as cost of revenue and moved down $66 million in FY2025 in step with the $42 million fall in Freight revenue, consistent with Uber's stated role as principal that invoices Shippers a gross fee and separately owes Carriers a freight charge regardless of Shipper payment [FY25 10-K, MD&A — Freight Segment; Note 1 (Revenue Recognition) — Freight].

**Supplier / input concentration (quantify).** The filing does not disclose a percentage of cost of revenue or purchases attributable to any single Driver, Courier, merchant, or Carrier — Not proven from available data; the underlying supply base (individual drivers/couriers, and independent trucking carriers) is inherently fragmented by design, which limits single-supplier concentration risk on that side. The one qualitative concentration flag the 10-K itself raises is on the Delivery merchant side: "a significant amount of our Delivery Gross Bookings come from a limited number of large restaurant groups and other merchants" [FY25 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors — Driver/Merchant/Carrier Supply], but no percentage of Delivery Gross Bookings or revenue tied to the largest merchant or top-3 merchants is disclosed — a real gap, not an invented number. A second, structurally different dependency is technology infrastructure: Uber "does not control the physical operation of any of the co-located data centers we use or the operations of our third-party service providers," may "not be able to easily switch to another cloud or data center provider," and its co-located data-center agreements are "of limited durations" with "no obligation to renew... on commercially reasonable terms or at all" [FY25 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors — Third-Party Cloud/Data Center Dependency]. No specific vendor is named and no percentage of infrastructure spend is quantified, but the switching-cost and non-renewal language is itself evidence of a real, if unquantified, dependency — this is the closest thing to a material single-source input risk visible in the pool, and it feeds the bargaining-risk discussion in §5. On the AV side, the ~$10 billion multi-year commitment is spread across at least seven named partners (Waymo, Nuro/Lucid, Zoox, Wayve, Baidu, Pony, Verne, Rivian), which management frames explicitly as diversification against dependence on any one AV supplier [Q2 FY26 transcript, Q&A] — a deliberate hedge against the concentration risk that would otherwise exist in a single-partner AV strategy.

## 3. Customer Pricing Power

Uber's blended take rate was about 27% of Gross Bookings in FY2025 (Mobility ~30%, Delivery ~19%, Freight effectively pass-through) [Business Identity output, §2, from FY25 10-K, p.49, p.58–59, and Note 13], and management's own framing in the most recent call is to defend the "net" take rate rather than push it higher: asked about a nearly 500-basis-point year-on-year decline in reported Mobility revenue margin, the CFO attributed most of it to a UK business-model accounting change (an optical, not economic, effect) and to "deliberate investments... in our lower-cost offerings," and told investors to focus on the net take rate disclosed in the 10-Q, which "will largely show take rate remaining broadly stable" [Q2 FY26 transcript, CFO Balaji Krishnamurthy, Q&A]. That same call disclosed no unilateral headline price increase in the last 24 months; instead, management described actively growing lower-cost products — Wait & Save (trading time for a lower fare) in the US, and two-/three-wheeler and Moto-brand rides in Brazil and other markets — because "low-cost products continue to grow globally," which is evidence of price-sensitive demand at the margin rather than pricing power [Q2 FY26 transcript, CEO Dara Khosrowshahi, Q&A]. The UK Mobility business-model change itself cut reported Q2 FY26 revenue by $1.1 billion even as underlying Gross Bookings kept growing 24%, illustrating that a chunk of "revenue" is a regulatory/accounting classification outcome rather than a price Uber freely sets [FY26 Q2 10-Q, cited in Business Identity output, §4]. Net: Uber can raise or lower its effective take rate at the margin through incentive spend and product mix, but the direction of travel in FY2025–FY2026 has been toward cheaper products and stable-to-flat net take rate, not toward tested, standalone price increases.

## 4. Economic Control Verdict

**Mixed.** In its core Mobility and Delivery marketplaces Uber sets the fee schedule and controls the pricing/dispatch algorithm, giving it real discretion over how a cost swing (e.g., the FY2025 insurance cost spike, or the 2026 insurance tailwind) flows through to margin versus reinvestment [FY25 10-K, MD&A; Q2 FY26 transcript]. But that discretion sits inside hard constraints on both sides: driver-pay minimums and classification litigation cap how far it can squeeze the supply side, delivery-fee and surge-pricing caps proposed or enacted by municipalities cap the demand side, and Delivery is disclosed as dependent on "a limited number of large restaurant groups" [FY25 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors]. Freight is unambiguously squeezed — a pass-through brokerage earning a thin, cycle-driven spread with a ~2% Adjusted EBITDA margin on segment revenue in FY2025 (own calculation: $41 million improvement off an implied ~$75 million FY2024 base, versus $5,099 million FY2025 segment revenue) [FY25 10-K, MD&A — Freight Segment; Business Identity output, §2].

## 5. The Single Biggest Bargaining Risk

Driver/courier employment reclassification: Uber's own 10-K states that being forced to treat Drivers as employees would require "significant price increases for Riders to offset" the added cost, directly testing whether Uber's take-rate discretion survives contact with a mandated, non-negotiable cost shock across every affected jurisdiction at once [FY25 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors — Driver Classification Developments, p.11].
