# Business Model Reality Check — UBER (Synthesis)

## Abstract

Uber runs a two-sided marketplace that matches riders, eaters, and shippers with independent drivers, couriers, and carriers, taking a fee on each transaction without owning vehicles. Mobility supplies 57% of FY2025 revenue and 69% of segment profit, and its contribution margin on Gross Bookings improved to 8.10% from 7.83%. The strongest positive is capital efficiency: capex is roughly 0.6% of revenue, and Uber is the only one of its three named peers (Lyft, DiDi, Bolt) with a positive EBIT margin (12.1% LTM). The strongest negative is durability: five-year average return on capital sits at just 0.85% against a roughly 9.7% cost of capital, and a pending $14.8 billion debt-funded Delivery Hero deal caps the governance score. No disqualifier triggered; this is an average business worth deeper work only if valuation is cheap, given a narrow, still-unproven moat and open driver-classification and autonomous-vehicle risk.

## 5. Simple Summary

- **What it does:** Runs an app that matches riders, eaters, and shippers with independent drivers, couriers, and trucking carriers — Uber owns none of the vehicles.
- **How it makes money:** Takes a cut (a "take rate") of every ride and delivery booked through the app — about 30% on Mobility, 19% on Delivery, and near-zero (pass-through) on Freight, roughly 27% blended in FY2025.
- **Whether each new unit creates value:** Yes on the one measure Uber discloses — each dollar of Mobility Gross Bookings earned 8.10 cents of segment profit in FY2025, up from 7.83 cents in FY2024 — but the strict test (does the profit from each new rider or driver beat what it cost to win them) cannot be checked because Uber doesn't publish that data.
- **Which segment matters most:** Mobility — more than half of revenue and over two-thirds of segment profit; Delivery is real and growing faster but earns less; Freight is small and currently loses money.
- **Whether it has a moat, and against whom:** A narrow, still-young moat built on network effects, scale, and cost advantage against Lyft, DiDi, and Bolt — but the company's own filings admit riders and drivers can and do switch platforms with no lock-in, and the moat has only cleared its cost of capital for the last one to two years, not through a full economic cycle.
- **What external variables it depends on:** Government and regulatory policy above all (driver-classification rulings, tax/VAT rule changes already cut one quarter's revenue by $1.1 billion in the UK alone), plus roughly half of revenue converting from non-US-dollar currencies and a small but genuinely cyclical Freight segment.
- **Whether capital is allocated well:** Mixed — buybacks and low leverage look disciplined day to day, but a serial-acquisition pattern culminating in a $14.8 billion, debt-funded takeover of Delivery Hero caps the governance score at 50/100.
- **Whether it deserves deeper work:** Only if the price is cheap enough to compensate for a mixed-quality, narrow-moat business carrying open regulatory, competitive, and accounting-quality risk.

# Business Identity — UBER

## 1. What The Company Actually Does

Uber runs an app-based marketplace that connects people who need a ride, a meal, a grocery order, or a freight shipment moved with the independent drivers, couriers, merchants, and trucking carriers who supply that service. It does not own the cars, drive the deliveries, or own the trucks — it takes a cut of each transaction for matching supply and demand, processing payment, and running the routing/pricing software. As of December 31, 2025 the network covered more than 15,000 cities in over 70 countries, with roughly 34,000 employees supporting hundreds of millions of drivers, couriers, merchants, shippers, and carriers [FY25 10-K, p.1 / Item 1]. The company operates as three reportable segments — Mobility (ride-hailing), Delivery (restaurant, grocery, and retail delivery), and Freight (connecting shippers with trucking carriers) [FY25 10-K, Note 13 (Segment Information)]. Customers pay Uber to solve a coordination problem: finding a nearby driver, courier, or truck fast, at a predictable price, without negotiating or dispatching one themselves. A single cross-platform membership, Uber One, bundles discounts and perks across rides, delivery, and grocery for a subscription fee and had reached 46 million members by year-end 2025 [FY25 10-K, p.1]. In the fourth quarter of 2025, 202 million monthly active platform consumers ("MAPCs") completed at least one Mobility ride or Delivery order [FY25 10-K, p.49].

## 2. How The Company Makes Money

Uber's core formula is a take rate on the total dollar value that flows through its platform (which it calls "Gross Bookings" — total value of rides and deliveries booked, including any Freight revenue, before paying drivers/couriers/carriers):

- **Mobility / Delivery (marketplace segments):** `Revenue = Gross Bookings × Revenue Margin (take rate)`, where Revenue Margin is the service fee Uber keeps after paying drivers/couriers, defined by the company as revenue as a percentage of Gross Bookings [FY25 10-K, p.1 / Item 7A definitions]. For FY2025: Mobility revenue of $29,670 million on Mobility Gross Bookings of roughly $97,497 million (sum of quarterly Gross Bookings disclosed in the 10-K) implies a take rate of about 30% [FY25 10-K, p.58–59 (Certain Key Metrics) and Note 13]; Delivery revenue of $17,248 million on Delivery Gross Bookings of roughly $90,864 million implies a take rate of about 19% [same source]. Blended across all three segments, FY2025 revenue of $52,017 million on Gross Bookings of $193,454 million works out to an overall take rate of about 27% [FY25 10-K, p.49].
- **Freight (logistics-broker segment):** `Revenue = shipment volume × price per shipment`, recognized on a gross basis because Uber acts as principal — arranging and pricing the shipment for the shipper and then paying the carrier as a cost of revenue. Freight revenue effectively equals Freight Gross Bookings by the company's own metric definition ($5,099 million revenue on $5,093 million Gross Bookings in FY2025) [FY25 10-K, p.59 and Note 13].
- **Secondary streams inside the marketplace segments:** advertising sold to merchants and Drivers/Merchants (reported inside Mobility and Delivery revenue) and Uber One subscription fees, both add to Revenue Margin without adding to Gross Bookings volume [FY25 10-K, p.1 (Segment descriptions); Q2 FY26 transcript, prepared remarks].

What drives the volume side is Trips (13,567 million in FY2025, up 20% year-over-year) and MAPCs (202 million in Q4 FY25, up 18%) [FY25 10-K, p.49]. What drives the price/take-rate side is the service fee schedule Uber sets for drivers and merchants, plus how much of that fee is given back as driver incentives and consumer discounts/promotions, which are netted against revenue or booked as cost of revenue depending on the type [FY25 10-K, Note 1 (Revenue Recognition)]. Margin is then a function of how much of the take rate survives after platform-participant payments, incentives, insurance, and fixed platform/technology costs — Adjusted EBITDA margin on Gross Bookings rose to $8,730 million (about 4.5% of Gross Bookings) in FY2025 from $6,484 million in FY2024 [FY25 10-K, p.49].

## 3. Business Type Classification

Two-sided, multi-vertical on-demand marketplace platform (ride-hailing, delivery, and freight brokerage) monetized through a take rate on gross transaction value, with an emerging autonomous-vehicle commercialization layer.

## 4. What Drives Variance

Revenue and Adjusted EBITDA moves are driven first by Gross Bookings growth (a function of Trips and MAPCs) — FY2025 Gross Bookings rose 19% (20% constant-currency), driven by Mobility trip volumes (+19%) and Delivery trip volumes (+22%), while Freight Gross Bookings fell 1% on a weak freight cycle [FY25 10-K, p.49]. Second, the take rate itself moves with driver/courier incentive spend, insurance costs (Mobility), and one-off regulatory/accounting reclassifications — the UK Mobility business-model change cut reported Q2 FY26 revenue by $1.1 billion even though the underlying Gross Bookings kept growing at 24%, so a reported revenue "miss" can be a classification effect rather than a demand effect [FY26 Q2 10-Q, p.17094–17099]. Third, mix between segments matters because take rates differ sharply — Mobility runs at roughly a 30% take rate versus roughly 19% for Delivery and effectively 100% (pass-through) for Freight, so a shift in growth toward Delivery or Freight mechanically pulls the blended take rate down even if total Gross Bookings grow [author's calculation from FY25 10-K, p.58–59 and Note 13]. Finally, currency (Uber reports both as-reported and constant-currency growth) and discrete items such as the $5.0 billion Netherlands deferred-tax-asset valuation-allowance release and unrealized gains/losses on minority equity stakes (Aurora, Lucid, Didi, Waabi, Grab) can swing reported net income well away from the operating trend [FY25 10-K, p.49].

## 3. Segment Disclosure Quality

Geographic segment revenue is disclosed by region (US & Canada, LATAM, EMEA, APAC) but not cross-tabulated against product segment, so a Mobility-vs-Delivery split by geography cannot be derived from the filings [Uber Technologies Inc NYSE UBER Financials_Annual.xls, Segments tab, Geographic Segments section]. # Unit Economics — UBER

## 1. Natural Unit

The natural economic unit for Mobility (the dominant segment — 57.0% of FY25 revenue, 69.1% of FY25 segment profit [`03_segment-map.md`, Section 2]) is **the trip (ride)**: one rider matched to one Driver on Uber's marketplace.

Uber does not disclose Mobility-specific Trip or Monthly Active Platform Consumer ("MAPC") counts — it only reports Trips and MAPCs on a combined Mobility-plus-Delivery basis ("Trips" is defined as "the number of completed consumer Mobility rides and Delivery orders in a given period" [FY25 10-K, "Certain Key Metrics"]). That means a true Mobility-only Gross Bookings-per-Trip or contribution-margin-per-trip figure **cannot be computed from filings**. What Uber does disclose at the Mobility-segment level, every quarter, in dollars: Gross Bookings (the total dollar value of Mobility rides, before Uber's cut), Revenue (Uber's cut), and Segment Adjusted EBITDA / Segment Operating Income (profit after Driver payments, incentives, insurance and other variable costs). This report therefore builds the required table using **$1 of Mobility Gross Bookings** as the measurable proxy unit — the closest available substitute for "one trip" given the disclosure gap, and itself a legitimate unit (comparable to how an asset manager's unit is "$1 of AUM"). This substitution is stated explicitly, not smoothed over, per the missing-disclosure item named in Section 3.

Delivery's natural unit is the order (one meal, grocery, or retail delivery); Freight's is the load (one truckload booking). Both face the same disclosure limit — Uber does not report Delivery-only or Freight-only Trip/order counts either.

## 2. Unit Economics Table

Unit = $1 of Mobility Gross Bookings, unless noted. Mobility Gross Bookings are built by summing the four disclosed quarters of each fiscal year: FY24 = $18,670M + $20,554M + $21,002M + $22,798M = $83,024M; FY25 = $21,182M + $23,762M + $25,111M + $27,442M = $97,497M [FY25 10-K, "Certain Key Metrics," Gross Bookings by segment table]. This reconciles exactly to the $193,454M FY25 total Gross Bookings figure once Delivery ($90,864M) and Freight ($5,093M) are added, confirming the segment split is internally consistent.

## 3. Value Creation Read

**Creates value**, on the one basis Uber actually discloses — but the read is partial. Every disclosed year since Mobility Adjusted EBITDA was first broken out, the segment has earned a positive and slowly rising profit on every dollar of Gross Bookings it books: 7.83% in FY24 rising to 8.10% in FY25, and 7.28% rising to 7.64% on the newer Segment Operating Income basis between Q2 FY25 and Q2 FY26 [FY25 10-K, Note 13; Q2 FY26 10-Q, Note 10]. Because Driver payments, incentives, insurance and network costs — the largest variable costs tied to each ride — are already netted out of that margin, a positive and improving figure is meaningful evidence that incremental Gross Bookings are profitable at the margin, not just at scale. What the math cannot prove is the classic contribution-margin-times-lifetime-versus-acquisition-cost test required to call this "value creation" in the strict unit-economics sense: Uber discloses neither a Driver- or rider-acquisition cost nor a rider/Driver retention curve, so there is no way to check whether the profit earned per dollar of Gross Bookings recovers what was spent to win that dollar in the first place. The single most valuable missing disclosure is a Mobility-specific Trip count (or Driver-incentive spend per active Driver) split out from the combined Mobility-plus-Delivery Trips figure Uber currently reports — without it, "creates value" here rests on a marketplace-dollar profitability trend, not a proven unit-level payback.

The margin trend itself is short: it has risen for roughly three to four years running since Uber returned to consistent Mobility profitability, not across a full economic or competitive cycle, so the 8.10% figure should be read as a level achieved during a period of strong post-pandemic demand recovery and market-share consolidation, not assumed as a permanent floor.

## 4. Sensitivity

The take rate (Mobility revenue as a percentage of Mobility Gross Bookings) is the input most likely to move the value-creation read if it shifts 20%, and it has already shown it can: a single regulatory change — Uber's UK business-model change effective January 2, 2026 — cut the take rate by roughly 530 basis points in one quarter (Q2 FY25's 30.7% to Q2 FY26's 25.4%), of which management attributes about 400 basis points to an accounting reclassification (moving cost out of cost of revenue, an "optical impact" per the CFO) and the remaining roughly 100 basis points to a deliberate pricing choice — investing in lower-cost offerings such as Moto in Brazil [Q2 FY26 transcript, CFO Q&A]. On the cost side, Driver payments and incentives (+$1.6 billion in FY25) and insurance expense (+$851 million in FY25, driven by "an increase in insurance rate per mile and miles driven") are the two variable-cost lines management calls out as growing fastest [FY25 10-K, MD&A, p.54–55]; insurance cost per mile is a structural risk unique to Mobility (Uber flags that "inflation has increased and is expected to increase our insurance costs" as a general risk factor) and is not something Uber controls the way it can dial Driver incentives up or down. Of the two, the take-rate move is the more historically volatile of the two on the evidence available — it produced a five-hundred-basis-point swing in a single quarter from one jurisdiction's regulatory change, a bigger one-period move than either disclosed cost line.

**Reconciliation note (CLAUDE.md §4/§20):** this report's FY24 Mobility Adjusted EBITDA margin recomputed directly from `03_segment-map.md`'s own cited figures ($6,497M / $25,087M) is 25.9%, not the 22.4% stated in that report's Section 1 table. Both reports cite the same source (FY25 10-K, Note 13, p.114); this appears to be an arithmetic error upstream rather than a different figure. This report uses the recomputed 25.9% (and the resulting FY24→FY25 direction of "improving," not the larger jump upstream's figure would imply) — flagged here rather than silently overridden.

## 4. Read

Uber's concentration risk is geographic, not customer-based: no single customer clears even the 10% disclosure threshold because revenue comes from hundreds of millions of individual, pay-per-transaction riders and eaters [FY25 10-K, Note 2]. This geographic weight is naked in the sense that it rests on transaction volume, not contracts — there is nothing locking in next year's US or UK Gross Bookings — and the company's own risk factors flag both a "large metropolitan areas" dependency and, within Delivery, an unquantified reliance on "a limited number of large restaurant groups and other merchants" [FY25 10-K, Item 1A]. The single biggest dependency the synthesizer should know about is the US + UK combination (66.1% of FY2025 revenue) sitting inside a business model where a single regulatory or accounting change in either market — as already happened with the UK Mobility revenue-recognition change that cut Q2 FY26 reported revenue by $1.1 billion even as Gross Bookings kept growing [FY26 Q2 10-Q, p.17094–17099] — can move consolidated results without any change in underlying demand.

## 2. Input Cost Pass-Through

Uber's largest input cost — payments and incentives to Drivers and Couriers — moves mechanically with Gross Bookings rather than through a negotiated pass-through clause: FY2025 cost of revenue rose $4.7 billion (18%), made up of a $1.6 billion increase in Driver payments/incentives and a $1.6 billion increase in Courier payments/incentives, both simply tracking higher Mobility and Delivery Gross Bookings [FY25 10-K, MD&A — Cost of Revenue, FY2025 vs FY2024]. Insurance — Uber's second-largest controllable-but-volatile input — is not automatically passed through either way: in FY2025 a $851 million increase in insurance expense (driven by a higher insurance rate per mile and more miles driven) pressured Mobility Adjusted EBITDA [FY25 10-K, MD&A — Mobility Segment, FY2025 vs FY2024], while by Q2 FY26 the CFO described insurance as having "become a tailwind this year," with the company choosing to "reinvest the savings from insurance back into the market" — i.e., into lower effective prices and driver/consumer incentives in markets like California, Los Angeles, and San Francisco to drive trip growth, rather than banking the windfall as margin [Q2 FY26 transcript, CFO Balaji Krishnamurthy, prepared remarks]. This is a discretionary reinvestment decision, not a contractual escalator or indexed-pricing clause — no such clause is disclosed in this pool for either Mobility or Delivery. Freight is the one segment with mechanical, near-total pass-through: Carrier payments are booked as cost of revenue and moved down $66 million in FY2025 in step with the $42 million fall in Freight revenue, consistent with Uber's stated role as principal that invoices Shippers a gross fee and separately owes Carriers a freight charge regardless of Shipper payment [FY25 10-K, MD&A — Freight Segment; Note 1 (Revenue Recognition) — Freight].

**Supplier / input concentration (quantify).** The filing does not disclose a percentage of cost of revenue or purchases attributable to any single Driver, Courier, merchant, or Carrier — Not proven from available data; the underlying supply base (individual drivers/couriers, and independent trucking carriers) is inherently fragmented by design, which limits single-supplier concentration risk on that side. The one qualitative concentration flag the 10-K itself raises is on the Delivery merchant side: "a significant amount of our Delivery Gross Bookings come from a limited number of large restaurant groups and other merchants" [FY25 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors — Driver/Merchant/Carrier Supply], but no percentage of Delivery Gross Bookings or revenue tied to the largest merchant or top-3 merchants is disclosed — a real gap, not an invented number. A second, structurally different dependency is technology infrastructure: Uber "does not control the physical operation of any of the co-located data centers we use or the operations of our third-party service providers," may "not be able to easily switch to another cloud or data center provider," and its co-located data-center agreements are "of limited durations" with "no obligation to renew... on commercially reasonable terms or at all" [FY25 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors — Third-Party Cloud/Data Center Dependency]. No specific vendor is named and no percentage of infrastructure spend is quantified, but the switching-cost and non-renewal language is itself evidence of a real, if unquantified, dependency — this is the closest thing to a material single-source input risk visible in the pool, and it feeds the bargaining-risk discussion in §5. On the AV side, the ~$10 billion multi-year commitment is spread across at least seven named partners (Waymo, Nuro/Lucid, Zoox, Wayve, Baidu, Pony, Verne, Rivian), which management frames explicitly as diversification against dependence on any one AV supplier [Q2 FY26 transcript, Q&A] — a deliberate hedge against the concentration risk that would otherwise exist in a single-partner AV strategy.

## 3. Customer Pricing Power

Uber's blended take rate was about 27% of Gross Bookings in FY2025 (Mobility ~30%, Delivery ~19%, Freight effectively pass-through) [Business Identity output, §2, from FY25 10-K, p.49, p.58–59, and Note 13], and management's own framing in the most recent call is to defend the "net" take rate rather than push it higher: asked about a nearly 500-basis-point year-on-year decline in reported Mobility revenue margin, the CFO attributed most of it to a UK business-model accounting change (an optical, not economic, effect) and to "deliberate investments... in our lower-cost offerings," and told investors to focus on the net take rate disclosed in the 10-Q, which "will largely show take rate remaining broadly stable" [Q2 FY26 transcript, CFO Balaji Krishnamurthy, Q&A]. That same call disclosed no unilateral headline price increase in the last 24 months; instead, management described actively growing lower-cost products — Wait & Save (trading time for a lower fare) in the US, and two-/three-wheeler and Moto-brand rides in Brazil and other markets — because "low-cost products continue to grow globally," which is evidence of price-sensitive demand at the margin rather than pricing power [Q2 FY26 transcript, CEO Dara Khosrowshahi, Q&A]. The UK Mobility business-model change itself cut reported Q2 FY26 revenue by $1.1 billion even as underlying Gross Bookings kept growing 24%, illustrating that a chunk of "revenue" is a regulatory/accounting classification outcome rather than a price Uber freely sets [FY26 Q2 10-Q, cited in Business Identity output, §4]. Net: Uber can raise or lower its effective take rate at the margin through incentive spend and product mix, but the direction of travel in FY2025–FY2026 has been toward cheaper products and stable-to-flat net take rate, not toward tested, standalone price increases.

## 4. Economic Control Verdict

**Mixed.** In its core Mobility and Delivery marketplaces Uber sets the fee schedule and controls the pricing/dispatch algorithm, giving it real discretion over how a cost swing (e.g., the FY2025 insurance cost spike, or the 2026 insurance tailwind) flows through to margin versus reinvestment [FY25 10-K, MD&A; Q2 FY26 transcript]. But that discretion sits inside hard constraints on both sides: driver-pay minimums and classification litigation cap how far it can squeeze the supply side, delivery-fee and surge-pricing caps proposed or enacted by municipalities cap the demand side, and Delivery is disclosed as dependent on "a limited number of large restaurant groups" [FY25 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors]. Freight is unambiguously squeezed — a pass-through brokerage earning a thin, cycle-driven spread with a ~2% Adjusted EBITDA margin on segment revenue in FY2025 (own calculation: $41 million improvement off an implied ~$75 million FY2024 base, versus $5,099 million FY2025 segment revenue) [FY25 10-K, MD&A — Freight Segment; Business Identity output, §2].

## 5. The Single Biggest Bargaining Risk

Driver/courier employment reclassification: Uber's own 10-K states that being forced to treat Drivers as employees would require "significant price increases for Riders to offset" the added cost, directly testing whether Uber's take-rate discretion survives contact with a mandated, non-negotiable cost shock across every affected jurisdiction at once [FY25 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors — Driver Classification Developments, p.11].

(line ref ~3095)], so no consolidated global share figure is meaningful.

## 4. Competitive Shape

Uber's own 10-K frames the market as globally fragmented with low barriers to entry: "We compete on a global basis in highly fragmented markets... in the future we expect to face competition from new market entrants given the low barriers to entry that characterize these industries" [FY25 10-K, Item 1, "Competitive Environment," p.5]. In practice the shape is better described as **regionally concentrated, globally fragmented**: the US is close to a duopoly (Uber ~76% / Lyft ~24%) [Web: Bloomberg Second Measure, 2026 (unverified, dated)]; China is dominated by Didi, where Uber holds a minority stake rather than competing directly; Southeast Asia is dominated by Grab, on the same minority-stake basis; and Europe/Africa/Latin America remain more fragmented, with Bolt as the largest independent regional challenger alongside Uber, Didi (in Brazil/Mexico), and local players (e.g., Ola in India, not profiled above for lack of public financials). A separate but adjacent signal of ongoing consolidation: Uber has agreed to acquire a majority stake in Delivery Hero SE — one of the companies its own 10-K names as a Delivery competitor — for an equity value of roughly €12.9 billion ($14.8 billion), expected to close in the second half of 2027, explicitly described in deal reporting as a move to "better compete with DoorDash Inc. outside the US" [Uber-Technologies-Inc-NYSE-UBER-Key-Developments.txt, M&A announcement 2026-07-16 / pre-deal reporting]. This is a Delivery-segment, not Mobility-segment, development, but it signals that Uber is actively consolidating named competitors rather than only competing against them.

## 5. Caveat

Ola (ANI Technologies), the fourth Mobility competitor named directly in Uber's FY25 10-K, is privately held with no financial disclosures in this data pool; it could not be profiled with sourced scale or profitability figures and is therefore omitted from the three-competitor list above rather than profiled with invented numbers. The Bolt and Bloomberg Second Measure figures used in Sections 2–4 are dated web sources (Tier 10 in the source hierarchy) rather than filings or Capital IQ exports, and are labelled accordingly — they should be treated as directional, not audited. DiDi's segment-level China vs. International revenue split (needed to isolate the actual overlap with Uber in Latin America) is not broken out in the Capital IQ comp export available in this pool.

**The economic moat test.**

> Return on capital **mixed — at-to-modestly-above cost of capital on the latest year/LTM read, but below it on a 5-year through-cycle average**: FY2025 ROIC (own calc, normalized 21% tax rate, average invested capital net-of-cash) **14.7%** vs estimated WACC **~9.7%** (+~500bps); CIQ vendor "Return on Capital" **9.4%** (FY2025) / **10.6%** (LTM) vs the same ~9.7% WACC (roughly flat to +90bps); but the CIQ vendor series' own 5-year through-cycle average (FY2021–FY2025: -9.4%, -4.9%, +3.1%, +6.0%, +9.4%) is **+0.85%**, roughly **-885bps below** the same ~9.7% WACC — [Capital IQ Financials_Annual export, Ratios tab, "Return on Capital %" row, CIQ; WACC = CAPM estimate, Inference not from filings, basis below].

- **Metric match:** Uber is an operating company (asset-light two-sided marketplace, not a bank/insurer/REIT) — ROIC vs WACC is the correct pairing, consistent with `07_business-quality.md` §0's sector-overlay finding of "no match" (generic operating-company grammar applies).
- **Cross-check of the headline figure.** I computed my own NOPAT ÷ average invested capital rather than accept the CIQ vendor's "Return on Capital %" row at face value: **NOPAT (FY2025) = Operating Income $5,565M × (1 − 21% normalized tax rate) = $4,396M** [FY25 10-K, Note 13, p.114 (Operating Income); tax-rate basis below]. **Invested capital, net-of-cash** = Total Debt (LT debt + current portion + finance/operating leases, $12,302M FY2025-end) + Total Equity ($28,083M) − Cash & ST Investments ($7,633M) = **$32,752M** (FY2025-end) / **$29,843.5M** (FY2024–FY2025 average) [Financials_Annual export, Balance Sheet tab, CIQ]. That gives **ROIC = $4,396M ÷ $29,843.5M average = 14.7%** — materially higher than the CIQ vendor's own 9.4% (FY2025) / 10.6% (LTM) "Return on Capital %" figure on the same Ratios tab. **This is a real divergence and is flagged, not smoothed over**: the CIQ figure's exact denominator/tax treatment is not disclosed in the export, so it cannot be fully reconciled from this pool; possible explanations include CIQ including the ~$9.4B of non-operating long-term equity-stake investments (Aurora, DiDi, Grab — whose gains/losses already sit below Operating Income and are excluded from my NOPAT) inside its capital base, which would dilute the return, or CIQ using the actual (unnormalized) reported tax rate in some years of its average. Per the conservative default (CLAUDE.md §4), **the lower, vendor-sourced figure is used as the primary read for the verdict below**, while my own computed figure is shown for transparency.
- **Gross-capital check (cash-netting sensitivity).** Uber is close to net-debt-neutral rather than deeply net cash (CIQ "Net Debt/EBITDA" = 0.011x for FY2025, essentially zero) [Financials_Annual export, Ratios tab, CIQ], but cash-netting still matters: on a **gross-capital basis (no cash netting)** — Invested Capital = Total Debt + Total Equity, average $37,148.5M (FY2024–FY2025) — the same $4,396M NOPAT gives **ROIC = 11.8%**, about 290bps lower than the net-of-cash figure (14.7%). Shown alongside per the required net-cash sensitivity check; the effect here is real but moderate, not the dramatic collapse a true net-cash balance sheet would produce.
- **Normalized tax rate, stated and stripped.** The ROIC numerator uses a **normalized 21% structural tax rate** (the US federal statutory rate stated in Uber's own tax-rate reconciliation table) [FY25 10-K, Income Taxes note, "Federal statutory income tax rate... 21.0%"], **not** the distorted GAAP reported rates. FY2024's reported effective tax rate was **-139.6%** and FY2025's was **-74.9%** (both tax BENEFITS, not expenses) [FY25 10-K, Income Taxes note, p. (Effective tax rate table)] — driven entirely by two one-off, non-cash items: a **$6.4 billion release of US federal and state deferred-tax-asset valuation allowances in Q4 2024**, and a **$5.0 billion release of the Netherlands deferred-tax-asset valuation allowance during FY2025** [FY25 10-K, Income Taxes note, "2025 Compared to 2024"]. Using either reported rate in NOPAT would overstate the operating return by tens of percentage points on a one-off, non-recurring accounting event. Analyst consensus estimates a forward effective tax rate of roughly 18–21% for FY2026 and beyond [UberTechnologies EstimatesReport, Consensus tab, Effective Tax Rate % row, CIQ], corroborating 21% as a reasonable structural anchor. **This 21% rate is the anchor for `valuation/04_intrinsic-dcf` to reconcile to, since this module runs first.**
- **Through-cycle basis, latest year labelled peak.** `07_business-quality.md` §4 explicitly labels FY2025's margins as "near-peak, post-recovery readings on a business that ran negative EBITDA margins for its first six disclosed years (FY2016–FY2021)," and flags Freight as still cyclically depressed (FY2025 segment Adjusted EBITDA margin -0.6%, "challenging freight market cycle"). Consistent with that read, the CIQ "Return on Capital %" series was negative every year from FY2017 through FY2022 and only turned positive in FY2023 (+3.1%), rising to FY2024 (+6.0%) and FY2025 (+9.4%) [Financials_Annual export, Ratios tab, CIQ]. **Uber has not yet run a positive-return-on-capital year through a demand shock** — its one severe downturn on record (COVID, FY2020 revenue -14.3%) landed while the company was still deeply negative on this metric. The 3-year post-turnaround trend (FY2023→FY2025: 3.1%→6.0%→9.4%) is genuinely improving but is not a "through the cycle" track record in the sense of having been tested by a downturn while already profitable.
- **Cost-of-capital basis.** No company-disclosed WACC or hurdle rate exists in this pool (the 10-K's own "weighted-average cost of capital analysis" language appears only in a purchase-accounting/goodwill-impairment estimation-methodology footnote, with no rate given [FY25 10-K, Note 1 / Note 3 accounting-policy language]). **Estimated via CAPM, Inference, not from filings**: risk-free rate 4.6% [Web: 10-year US Treasury yield, ~4.6–4.65%, early August 2026 (dated, unverified)]; beta 1.15 (5-year) [Capital IQ Comparable Analysis export / Public Company Profile, as-of 2026-08-06]; equity risk premium 5.0% (standard long-run US estimate, not company-disclosed — Inference); cost of equity = 4.6% + 1.15 × 5.0% = **10.35%**. Cost of debt: pre-tax ≈ interest expense $440M ÷ LT+current debt $10,521M FY2025 = **4.2%**, after the same 21% normalized tax = **3.3%**. Weights from market values: equity $139,261.7M, total debt $14,731M (E/(D+E)=90.4%, D/(D+E)=9.6%) [Public Company Profile, CIQ, priced 2026-08-05]. **WACC = 0.904 × 10.35% + 0.096 × 3.3% ≈ 9.7%.**

## 4. Where The Company Sits

1. **Relative to peers:** Uber sits at the **top of named peers** on margin and capital efficiency. LTM gross margin (40.8%) and EBIT margin (12.1%) both beat Lyft (35.6% / -2.2%) and DiDi (15.6% / -2.6%); Uber is the only one of the three CIQ-covered names with a positive EBIT margin and a positive, disclosed return on capital [`08_competitive-map.md` §2; Section 3 table above]. Bolt is smaller and only marginally profitable (operating margin ~0.9%).
2. **Absolute (the economic moat test):** Uber's return on capital sits **at-to-modestly-above** its estimated cost of capital on the latest fiscal year and LTM read (CIQ 9.4%/10.6% vs ~9.7% WACC; own-calc 14.7% vs ~9.7% WACC), but **below** its cost of capital on a 5-year through-cycle average (+0.85% vs ~9.7% WACC) — the decisive test, since a single peak-adjacent year should not carry a moat verdict on its own (§3 above).

## 5. Moat Verdict

**Narrow moat.** The strongest evidenced source is the combination of **network effects + scale + cost advantage**: Uber's own filing names "critical mass" of Drivers and Riders as something it must actively maintain, its scale (~8x Lyft's revenue) supports a Mobility Adjusted EBITDA margin (26.6%, FY2025) far above any named public peer, and this margin gap now finally translates into a positive, peer-leading return on capital. But two things cap it at Narrow rather than Strong. First, the filing itself discloses the two features that most directly blunt a network-effect moat — low switching costs and common multi-homing across Uber, Lyft, Bolt, DiDi, and Ola — so the advantage defends share more than it defends price. Second, and decisively per the hard rule: Uber's return on capital has been above its estimated ~9.7% cost of capital only for the last 1–2 years (CIQ Return on Capital: +6.0% FY2024, +9.4% FY2025, +10.6% LTM), following a decade of value destruction including its one severe demand shock on record (COVID) landing while returns were still deeply negative; the 5-year through-cycle average (+0.85%) remains far below cost of capital. This is a moat that has only just crossed into being economic, not one proven sustained through a cycle — the durability test over the next five years is whether Uber can hold a return on capital above ~9.7% through the next demand downturn and through the unresolved autonomous-vehicle transition, where Waymo already operates a competing fleet independent of Uber's platform [`07_business-quality.md` §1, rate-of-change row, scored 32/100 — a fast-changing-industry flag per CLAUDE.md §24, Filter 5, that discounts durability further].

**Moat trajectory: Widening.** Evidence over the last five years is one-directional and specific: CIQ Return on Capital rose from -4.9% (FY2022) to +3.1% (FY2023) to +6.0% (FY2024) to +9.4% (FY2025) to +10.6% (LTM); EBIT margin rose from -5.7% (FY2022) to +2.98% (FY2023) to +6.4% (FY2024) to +10.7% (FY2025) to +12.1% (LTM); Mobility segment Adjusted EBITDA margin rose from 22.4% to 26.6% (FY2024→FY2025) [Financials_Annual export, Ratios tab, CIQ; FY25 10-K, Note 13]. Uber also grew LTM revenue faster (16.68%) than Lyft (9.36%) or DiDi (10.04%) over the same trailing window [`08_competitive-map.md` §2–3], consistent with share stability-to-gains rather than erosion. The widening trend is real but should be read alongside the low rate-of-change/disruption score (32/100) in `07_business-quality.md` — an AV-driven change in industry structure could interrupt this trajectory faster than the multi-year trend line alone would suggest, so the widening read should not be extrapolated mechanically into `valuation/04_intrinsic-dcf`'s durable-advantage period without discounting for that risk.

## 3. Classification

**Partly externally driven** — Uber has real, working management levers: it does not own vehicles (fuel/vehicle-cost risk sits with Drivers, not Uber), it hedges transaction-level FX with forward contracts, it can and does adjust fares, service fees, and Driver/Courier incentives to manage supply and demand, and it has repeatedly defended its independent-contractor model in court and at the ballot box (Prop 22 in California, the 2024 Massachusetts settlement, favorable 2025 French Supreme Court rulings). This is a company with strong internal levers sitting on top of a policy and regulatory environment that is actively, not hypothetically, moving results.

**54/100** (inverted scale — higher = worse / more dangerous external dependence)

Three items line up into one pattern the synthesizer should weight together rather than separately: (1) the disqualifier-scan's already-noted discontinuation of the full Adjusted-EBITDA-to-GAAP reconciliation starting Q1 FY2026, (2) the "Legal, non-income tax, and regulatory reserve changes and settlements" line excluded from Adjusted EBITDA at a double-digit percentage of that metric for two straight years despite being labeled non-recurring, and (3) a growing self-insurance reserve build that the company's own MD&A names as the primary driver of recent operating-cash-flow "working capital" gains. None of the three is a proven misstatement or a going-concern issue — the auditor's opinion is clean and the insurance-reserve development has actually been mildly favorable in FY2024–FY2025 (per Schedule II footnote 1) — but together they describe a company whose two most-watched performance metrics (Adjusted EBITDA and operating cash flow) both carry real, quantified, recurring adjustments that a reader taking the headline numbers at face value would miss.
