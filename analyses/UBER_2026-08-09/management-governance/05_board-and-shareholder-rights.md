**Read.** Uber's charter/bylaw basket is a genuine, disclosed set of entrenchment tools — no cumulative voting, limited shareholder right to call special meetings, board-controlled vacancy-filling, advance-notice bylaws, and blank-check preferred authority — all listed by the company itself as provisions that "could depress the trading price...by acting to discourage, delay, or prevent a change of control." These are common among US tech IPOs and are not unusual by market standards, but they are real, not theoretical. They are meaningfully offset by three shareholder-favorable structural facts: single-class stock (one vote per share, no founder super-voting block), an annually-elected (non-staggered) board, and no controlling shareholder to entrench in the first place — the tools exist, but there is no controlling holder positioned to use them against the rest of the shareholder base the way a promoter-controlled company would.

## 5. Minority-Shareholder Protection Read

On paper, Uber's board clears almost every formal governance box: 90% independent directors, a fully independent chair separate from the CEO, four committees that are 100% independent-director-composed, single-class one-share-one-vote stock, no controlling shareholder or promoter to entrench, and an annually-elected (non-staggered) board that has genuinely refreshed (2 of 9 independent directors added in the last 3 years). Related-party exposure is small (≤1.4% of net worth on any single item) and structurally different from classic promoter self-dealing — it exists because Uber holds equity or lending positions in commercial partners (Moove, Lime, Careem Technologies), not because a controller is extracting value from the company.

Set against that: the charter/bylaws contain a real, company-disclosed basket of entrenchment tools (no cumulative voting, restricted ability to call special meetings, board-controlled vacancy-filling, blank-check preferred authority), a director with a full-time executive role at one of Uber's largest historical shareholders (PIF) sits on the board carrying the "independent" label, two directors (Alnowaiser, Arora) carry board loads that raise a genuine capacity question, and — most materially — an unresolved shareholder derivative lawsuit filed in federal court (N.D. Cal., reported Jun/Jul-2026) names the sitting CEO, the independent Chairman, and every sitting director, alleging the board's own 2024–2026 proxy statements about safety oversight were false and that a safety-risk model was ignored for five years (2017–2022) [carried from `01_management-and-track-record.md`, RF-MGT-005, confidence 2/5, unadjudicated]. That allegation is unproven, but it is precisely the question this section exists to ask: is this independent-on-paper board actually functioning as a check on management, or box-ticking? Until the litigation resolves, that question cannot be answered in the board's favor. The absence of the 2026 proxy also means insider/director ownership percentages, AGM vote margins, and any RPT-approval process cannot be independently verified from this pool.

**Verdict: adequate.** The structural bones (independence, split chair, single-class stock, no promoter) are genuinely good and support real shareholder protection in the ordinary course. The verdict is not "strong protection" because of the disclosed anti-takeover basket, the PIF-affiliated "independent" director, the overboarding on two seats, and — above all — the unresolved allegation that this same board's own oversight disclosures were false. It is not "weak / entrenched" because there is no controlling shareholder, no dual-class structure, and no proven abuse on the evidence in this pool. The single biggest entrenchment/self-dealing risk to minority holders is not related-party leakage (which is small and non-promoter) — it is the unresolved question of whether the board's stated safety-oversight function was real, which is now the subject of active federal litigation naming every sitting director.
