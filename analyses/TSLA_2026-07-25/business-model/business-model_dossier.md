# Business Model Reality Check — TSLA (Synthesis)

## Abstract

Tesla designs, builds, and sells electric vehicles directly to consumers, with a smaller battery-storage business and an unmonetized bet on self-driving software and robots bolted on. Vehicle sales dominate how it makes money, at 86.5% of FY2025 revenue and 77.7% of gross profit [CIQ Financials_Annual export, Segments tab, FY2025]. Its strongest positive is a $27.4 billion net-cash balance sheet (Jun-2026) funding continued vertical integration into battery cells and lithium refining [Capital IQ export, Capital Structure Summary tab]. Its strongest negative is that return on capital, at 2.75%–3.7% (LTM), sits roughly 770–885 basis points below an estimated 11.5% cost of capital, with margins compressing for three straight years and no proven moat. No disqualifier triggered, but persistent governance red flags surround CEO pay and related-party dealings. Verdict: a cyclical, technology-cycle bet, not a proven durable compounder — worth deeper work only with a strong timing edge.

## 5. Simple Summary

- Tesla designs, builds, and sells electric vehicles direct to consumers, plus a smaller grid-battery business and an unmonetized bet on self-driving software and humanoid robots.
- It makes money mainly from `vehicles delivered × average selling price`, with a shrinking near-pure-margin government regulatory-credit line and a fast-growing usage-based services tail (used cars, Supercharging, paid FSD subscriptions).
- Each vehicle currently sells for about $6,639 more than it costs to build (Q2 FY2026), so units are not sold below cost — but no fully-loaded, unit-level economic-profit figure can be computed because R&D and SG&A are never allocated by segment or by vehicle.
- Automotive is by far the most important segment (86.5% of FY2025 revenue), though the smaller Energy segment is improving faster and now contributes 22.2% of gross profit, up from 6.5% three years ago.
- No moat is proven: Technology/IP is the strongest individual candidate (50/100) but return on capital (2.75%–3.7% LTM) sits well below an estimated ~11.5% cost of capital, and the gap has widened every year since FY2022.
- The business depends heavily on things it does not control — US regulatory-credit policy, unhedged foreign-currency swings on about half its revenue, and interest rates that move financing-subsidy costs automatically against margin.
- Capital allocation carries clean balance-sheet hygiene ($27.4bn net cash, near-zero acquisitions) alongside real governance red flags — a $132.3bn maximum CEO pay award, a 56% cumulative rise in share count with zero buybacks, and an unresolved securities-fraud class action tied to the company's core growth narrative.
- It deserves deeper work only with a strong timing edge — this is a cyclical, technology-cycle bet on EV competition and an unproven autonomous-driving story, not a durable compounder in its current, disclosed form.

# Business Identity — TSLA

## 1. What The Company Actually Does

Tesla designs, builds, and sells electric vehicles (EVs), and separately makes and sells battery systems that store electricity for homes, businesses, and power grids [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A, p. business overview, "operating and reportable segments: (i) automotive and (ii) energy generation and storage"]. It sells cars straight to buyers — through its own stores, website, and delivery network — rather than through independent franchised dealers, and it services those cars itself through company-owned service centers and mobile technicians [Tesla Inc NasdaqGS TSLA Public Company Profile.rtf, company description]. Car buyers are individual consumers (plus a smaller number of commercial fleet buyers); battery buyers range from homeowners (Powerwall) to utilities and large commercial developers (Megapack) [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A, "Energy Generation and Storage Demand, Production and Deployment"]. It sells worldwide, with the United States, China, and the rest of the world each a disclosed geographic segment [Tesla Inc NasdaqGS TSLA Financials_Annual.xls, Segments tab, Geographic Segments, FY2025]. The core problem it is paid to solve is replacing a gasoline car with an electric one people actually want to drive, and giving asset owners a way to store and dispatch electricity instead of buying it moment-to-moment off the grid. Alongside the two disclosed segments, Tesla is investing in software and robotics it does not yet meaningfully monetize — driver-assist software (FSD Supervised), an early robotaxi ride-hailing service, and a humanoid robot (Optimus) — which the company frames as its next growth leg but which today contribute a small, undisclosed slice of revenue, mostly folded into "Services and other" [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A, p. "We are focused on bringing artificial intelligence into the real world... FSD (Supervised) and Robotaxi... AI robots (including Optimus)"]. In 2026 through the second quarter, Tesla produced about 860 thousand consumer vehicles and delivered about 838 thousand of them, and deployed 22.3 gigawatt-hours (GWh) of energy storage products [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A, p. "In 2026, we produced approximately 860 thousand consumer vehicles..." and "In 2026, we deployed 22.3 GWh..."].

## 2. How The Company Makes Money

Tesla runs two disclosed revenue lines with different formulas, plus a smaller usage-based tail:

- **Vehicles (the bulk of revenue): `Revenue = vehicles delivered × average selling price` + `regulatory-credit sales` + `lease revenue`.** In Q2 FY2026, automotive sales revenue was $20,006 million, regulatory credits $146 million, and leasing $364 million, for total automotive revenue of $20,516 million out of $28,236 million total revenue [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 1 Financial Statements, Condensed Consolidated Statements of Operations]. Regulatory credits are sold to other automakers who need them to meet emissions rules, not to Tesla's own customers, and have been shrinking: down 67% year-on-year in the quarter, "impacted by ... changes in regulation" and reduced by recent U.S. government action restricting credit programs [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A, "Automotive regulatory credits revenue decreased $293 million, or 67%..."].
- **Energy storage and generation: `Revenue = megawatt-hours (MWh) of batteries/solar deployed × average price per unit`.** Q2 FY2026 energy revenue was $3,139 million, up 13% year-on-year on higher Megapack deployment volume even as the average selling price per Megapack fell [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A, "Energy generation and storage revenue increased $350 million, or 13%..."].
- **Services and other (a usage tail, not a separate formula-driven business): used-car sales, paid Supercharging, non-warranty repair/collision work, insurance, and merchandise, which move with the size of the vehicle fleet already on the road.** This line was $4,581 million in Q2 FY2026, up 50% year-on-year, "primarily due to increases in used vehicle sales volume ... non-warranty maintenance services and collision revenue and paid Supercharging sessions" [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A, "Services and other revenue increased $1.54 billion, or 50%..."].

Volume is driven by factory output and price cuts/incentives needed to move that output; price is driven by model mix (which trims and options customers choose), currency (a weaker dollar lifted Q2 FY2026 automotive ASP), and take-up of the paid FSD (Supervised) software add-on [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A, "a higher average selling price per unit primarily driven by sales mix and a positive impact from the weakening of the United States dollar ... and an increase from higher revenue from FSD (Supervised) subscriptions"]. Margin is driven by the gap between that price and per-unit manufacturing cost (steel, aluminum, battery cells, tariffs, labor, plant depreciation) and by how much of the regulatory-credit line — which is close to pure profit — is still in the mix; automotive gross margin moved from 17.2% to 16.9% quarter-on-quarter as credits shrank and cost per unit stayed roughly flat [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A, "Gross margin for total automotive decreased from 17.2% to 16.9%..."], while energy-segment gross margin fell sharply from 30.3% to 20.4% on higher per-unit cost driven by sales mix and warranty charges [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A, "Gross margin for energy generation and storage decreased from 30.3% to 20.4%..."].

## 3. Business Type Classification

Vertically integrated electric-vehicle manufacturer selling direct to consumers, with a smaller, faster-growing grid-battery business bolted on and an unproven, not-yet-monetized bet on self-driving software and robots.

## 4. What Drives Variance

The biggest swing factor quarter to quarter is vehicle delivery volume, which is itself capped by factory output and by how much Tesla has to cut price or offer incentives to sell that output — Q2 FY2026 automotive-sales revenue growth of 27% was explicitly volume-led ("an increase of approximately 25% in cash deliveries") [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A]. The second-biggest factor is the regulatory-credit line, which is small in dollars but close to 100% margin and moves on U.S. policy, not on anything Tesla's own customers do [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A]. Currency (a weaker dollar versus the yuan and euro) and per-unit cost inflation (tariffs, warranty adjustments, battery-cell input costs) are the next-largest levers on both revenue and gross margin [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A, "Cost of automotive sales revenue... On a revenue basis alone, Tesla is close to the ">85% from one segment" single-segment threshold, so it is fair to call this an automotive company with a second, smaller energy business — not a diversified conglomerate.

## 3. Segment Disclosure Quality

There is no "Other" or "Corporate" bucket — segment revenue and segment gross profit sum exactly to consolidated totals in both the Q2 FY2026 10-Q ($25,097M + $3,139M = $28,236M total revenue; $4,111M + $640M = $4,751M total gross profit) and the FY2025 annual data ($82,056M + $12,771M = $94,827M; $13,292M + $3,802M = $17,094M), so there is no unallocated residual to question. The bigger gap is depth, not consistency: Tesla discloses segment **revenue, cost of revenue, and gross profit only** — operating expenses (SG&A, R&D), operating income, and net income are never allocated to either segment, so no one outside the company can compute a true segment-level operating margin or return on capital; every profit-share figure in this report is explicitly a gross-profit share. A related gap is the sub-line "Services and other," which is economically distinct (record profitability, 46–50% YoY revenue growth in H1 FY2026) but reported only inside the Automotive segment, with no full-year cost-of-revenue breakout available in this pool — only single-quarter figures can be computed. Finally, the fast-growing forward bets Tesla talks about heavily in its shareholder letters — Robotaxi, Optimus, Cybercab, in-house AI inference chips — have **no separate segment or revenue-line disclosure at all**; whatever capital and operating expense they consume is invisible inside the Automotive segment's numbers, which is a real transparency gap given how much narrative weight management puts on these programs.

## 3. Value Creation Read

**Unclear at the fully-loaded level; creates value at the gross-margin level.** Each vehicle Tesla delivers currently sells for roughly $6,600 more than it costs to build (Q2 FY2026: ASP ~$42,335 vs. average cost per vehicle ~$35,696, a 15.7% gross margin on this basis, or 16.3% on Tesla's own non-GAAP measure excluding regulatory credits [`TSLA-Q2-2026-Update.pdf, p.30`]) — that gap comfortably covers direct manufacturing cost, so the unit is not sold below its own build cost. But Tesla does not allocate research-and-development or selling/administrative expense to any segment or to the vehicle unit [`03_segment-map.md`, Note 1], so the gross-profit figure above cannot be turned into a true fully-loaded, per-vehicle economic-profit number; consolidated operating margin (all segments combined) fell to 1.4% in Q2 FY2026 — a level management itself has not previously reported this low in the periods in this pool — while R&D spend rose 47% YoY on Optimus, Robotaxi/Cybercab, Semi, and AI-chip pre-production costs that are not tied to any single vehicle sold today [`TSLA-Q2-2026-Update.pdf, Financial Summary, p.4`]. The single most valuable missing disclosure is a segment-level operating-expense allocation (or, short of that, a stated fixed R&D/SG&A cost per vehicle) — without it, "does each vehicle create value" cannot be answered at the level a real capital-allocation decision requires, only at the gross-margin level.

This is not a peak-cycle read that needs ring-fencing on the high side — the opposite is true. Automotive segment gross margin (which includes the higher-margin Services sub-line) has fallen from a historical peak of 26.9% in FY2021 ($13,735M gross profit / $51,034M revenue [`Tesla Inc NasdaqGS TSLA Financials_Annual.xls, Segments tab`]) to 16.2% in FY2025, and the vehicle-only ASP-minus-cost margin computed in this report (14.1%-15.7% over the last five quarters) sits well below that historical level. The current per-unit read is closer to a competitive-erosion trough than a cyclical peak.

## 4. Sensitivity

**Vehicle average selling price (ASP) is the input that would most change this read if it moved 20%, and it has also been the most historically volatile of the two core levers.** A 20% ASP move (~$8,467 on the Q2 FY2026 base of $42,335) is larger than the entire calculated gross profit per vehicle (~$6,639) — a 20% ASP cut would push the unit into a loss on this basis before any operating expense is even allocated. This is not a hypothetical: Tesla's own management commentary attributes the multi-year margin compression (segment margin 18.2% FY2023 → 16.2% FY2025) explicitly to "lower vehicle average selling price (ASP)…inclusive of mix impact" in every quarterly YoY bridge reviewed in this pool [`TSLA-Q2-2026-Update.pdf, p.25`; `Annual_Report_TSLA-Q4-2025.pdf`], reflecting Tesla's repeated use of price cuts and financing incentives to defend delivery volume — a lever it has pulled harder and more often than any cost-side lever in the periods reviewed. Cost per vehicle is the second most sensitive input (a 20% move, ~$7,139, is similarly larger than the unit's gross profit) but has moved by smaller absolute amounts historically — management cites narrower swings from tariffs, warranty true-ups, and fixed-cost absorption (e.g., a ~$230M one-off tariff/warranty benefit in Q1 FY2026 that did not repeat in Q2, per the Q2 FY2026 earnings call [`Tesla, Inc., Q2 2026 Earnings Call, Jul 22, 2026`]) rather than sustained multi-year cost inflation. Regulatory-credit revenue is the single most volatile revenue line in percentage terms (down 49-67% YoY in H1 FY2026 [`03_segment-map.md`, Section 1]), but at $146M in Q2 FY2026 against $20,006M of automotive sales revenue, it is too small in dollar terms to be the primary swing factor for the per-vehicle read, even though its decline removes a cushion that had previously flattered reported automotive gross margin.

## 4. Read

Tesla's concentration risk sits almost entirely on the geography side, not the customer side: the filings show no entity holding 10% or more of receivables [FY2025 10-Q, Note — Concentration of Risk], and the business model (millions of individual buyers) makes customer concentration structurally unlikely. Geographically, the United States crossed 50% of FY2025 revenue [CIQ Financials_Annual export, Segments sheet] and carries an even larger share of long-lived assets (~79–82%) [FY2025 10-Q, Note 14], while China's revenue share fell from about 22% (FY2025) to about 17.5% (H1 2026) as "Other International" (Europe/APAC ex-China/RoW) grew to over a third of revenue [FY2025 10-Q, Note 14; Q2 2026 earnings call]. None of this geographic revenue is secured by long-term contracts — it is organic, demand-driven, and swings with regional deliveries, incentives, and tariffs each quarter. The single biggest dependency the synthesizer should carry forward: Tesla now earns roughly half its revenue and over three-quarters of its long-lived assets in one country (the United States), with no contractual protection against a US-specific demand, tariff, or policy shock.

## 2. Input Cost Pass-Through

Tesla's ability to pass rising input costs to customers is partial and lossy, not automatic. On the automotive side, management credits "effective pricing and cost management" for holding gross margin "approximately flat" quarter-on-quarter once one-off items are stripped out [Q2 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks], but the multi-year trend argues the other way: GAAP automotive gross margin fell from 18.2% in FY2023 to 16.2% in FY2025 [03_segment-map.md, citing CIQ Financials_Annual export] even as per-unit cost stayed "relatively consistent" [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A] — the gap was closed mostly by regulatory-credit contribution and cost cuts, not by raising sticker prices. Tariff cost relief has shown up as one-off, laggy recognitions rather than a structural escalator: Tesla booked "more than $250 million from certain tariffs which we paid in prior quarters" as an energy-segment benefit in Q1 FY2026 that then "did not repeat" in Q2 [Q1 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks; Q2 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks]. There is no disclosed contractual pass-through clause (escalator or indexed pricing) for retail vehicle sales in this pool — automotive sales are one-off retail transactions, not standing supply contracts [05_customer-geography.md]. On the energy side, management is explicit that trying to pass tariff-driven battery-cell cost increases through to customers is self-defeating: "import tariffs by the US government and the provisions of the OBBBA could significantly increase battery cell expenses and impact costs for our consumers, negatively impacting consumer demand" [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A]. Not proven from available data: any numeric lag (in days or quarters) between an input cost rise and a matching price change, beyond the tariff-recognition timing above.

**Supplier / input concentration (quantify).** The filings do not quantify supplier concentration — there is no disclosed percentage of cost of revenue or purchases tied to the largest supplier or the top three suppliers, and no dollar figure attached to the single-source dependency. The 10-Q states only, in general terms, that Tesla is "dependent on our suppliers, including single source suppliers," without naming them or sizing the exposure [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Notes, Supply Risk]. The one input concentration that is specifically named and material is geographic rather than a single company: for the Energy Generation and Storage segment, "most of the battery cells are procured from China," and management flags this as the reason "tariffs in this business can have outsized impacts" [Q1 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks]. That segment was 13.5% of FY2025 revenue but 22.2% of FY2025 gross profit and growing [03_segment-map.md] — so a China-battery-cell single-country dependency sits directly inside Tesla's fastest-improving profit pool. Not proven from available data: the dollar or percentage split of Tesla's total raw-material or component purchases between in-house-produced (lithium, cathode, cells) and externally-sourced inputs.

## 3. Customer Pricing Power

Tesla has not demonstrated an ability to raise prices without losing volume in the periods covered by this pool. No explicit sticker-price increase is documented in the last 24 months; instead, the automotive average selling price moved on "sales mix and a positive impact from the weakening of the United States dollar... and an increase from higher revenue from FSD (Supervised) subscriptions" [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A] — mix, currency, and software attach, not a price hike on the core vehicle. Automotive gross margin has trended down for three straight years (18.2% FY2023 → 16.9% FY2024 → 16.2% FY2025) [03_segment-map.md] even as Q2 FY2026 revenue growth was explicitly volume-led ("an increase of approximately 25% in cash deliveries") [Business identity, §4, citing Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A] — consistent with a company competing on volume, not price, against BYD and legacy-OEM EVs. On the energy side, management states plainly that "ASPs for industrial storage is coming down amidst growing competition" [Q2 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks], and Q2 FY2026 energy gross margin fell sharply from 30.3% to 20.4% on "a decrease in average selling price per Megapack unit" [Business identity, §2, citing Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A]. The one clear pricing-power exception is software, not hardware: about 55% of North America deliveries carried a paid FSD (Supervised) subscription at the time of delivery in Q2 FY2026 [Q2 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks], but that sits on top of a base vehicle price that is itself under competitive pressure, not evidence that Tesla can move the core product's price.

## 4. Economic Control Verdict

**Mixed.** Tesla controls the middle of its value chain — it owns its factories, bypasses the independent dealer network that squeezes most automakers, and increasingly monetizes proprietary infrastructure and software (Supercharger access sold to rival OEMs, paid FSD subscriptions) on terms it sets [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A; Q2 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks]. But it is a price-taker on globally traded battery-grade commodities and disclosed single-source components upstream [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Notes, Supply Risk], and it cannot raise prices to end customers without losing volume on either the automotive side (three years of margin decline despite flat unit cost) or the energy side ("ASPs for industrial storage is coming down amidst growing competition") [03_segment-map.md; Q2 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks]. The near-pure-margin regulatory-credit revenue that has historically cushioned automotive margin is set entirely by government policy, not by negotiation, and it fell 67% year-on-year in Q2 FY2026 [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A].

## 5. The Single Biggest Bargaining Risk

The Energy segment's reliance on China-sourced battery cells under an escalating US tariff and OBBBA regime is the relationship most likely to hurt Tesla if it worsens — it raises input cost in the segment where gross profit has grown fastest, and management itself says passing that cost to customers would cut demand rather than protect margin [Q1 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks; Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A].

**Losing** global battery-electric-vehicle volume share to BYD on a full calendar-year 2025 basis: BYD held roughly 12.1% of the global BEV market in 2025 versus Tesla's 8.8% (Volkswagen third at 5.2%), and BYD's battery-electric volume (2.26 million units) grew 28% year-on-year while Tesla's global deliveries (1.64 million) fell from the prior year [Web: autovistagroup/visualcapitalist EV market-share summaries, 2026, unverified — not in the data pool]. That said, Tesla's own most recent quarters show a different, more recent trend: automotive-sales revenue was down 11% year-on-year in Q4 FY2025 but up 23–27% year-on-year in Q1–Q2 FY2026 [`03_segment-map.md` §1, citing `FY26 Q2 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A`]. The data pool does not disclose Tesla's own global market-share trend directly, so the full-year-2025 share loss and the early-2026 revenue rebound are the two best available reads and they point in different directions — the rebound could reflect regained share, an industry-wide demand recovery, or both; it is not proven from available data which.

## 4. Competitive Shape

**Fragmented.** No single manufacturer holds anywhere near a dominant share of the global BEV market: BYD leads at roughly 12.1%, Tesla second at 8.8%, Volkswagen third at 5.2% — the top three combine for only about 26% of the market, with the balance split across a long tail of Chinese domestic brands (Geely, SAIC, Xiaomi, Li Auto, NIO, XPeng, Wuling — none named in this data pool) and legacy Western/Japanese/Korean OEMs building out competing EV lineups, several of which appear in Tesla's own Capital IQ peer-comp set: Ford, General Motors, Honda, Mercedes-Benz, Renault, Kia, Stellantis, plus EV pure-plays Rivian and Lucid [Web: autovistagroup/visualcapitalist EV market-share summaries, 2026, unverified; `Company Comparable Analysis Tesla Inc .xls, Business Description tab`]. This is not a 2–4 firm oligopoly and not a consolidated market — it is a large field of scaled competitors with no HHI concentration data available in this pool to quantify precisely.

## 5. Caveat

The data pool's own Tesla documents do not name specific competitors: the only Form 10-K present is a Part III-only amendment (`Tesla_Inc_-_Form_10-KA(Apr-30-2026)`), which lacks the Item 1A "Risk Factors" / "Competition" section a full annual filing would carry, and neither in-pool earnings-call transcript (Q1 or Q2 FY2026) names a specific rival by name. As a result, two of the three profiles above (Ford, GM) are sourced to the Capital IQ Comparable Analysis export rather than to Tesla's own filings, and the single most consequential real-world competitor by volume — BYD — appears nowhere in this data pool at all and is sourced only to dated, unverified web trade press. A full FY2025 10-K with its Item 1A Competition discussion, plus a peer-comp export that itself included BYD, would resolve this gap and let the `moat` agent anchor its "earns more than peers" claim to filing-grade rather than web-grade evidence for the company's most important rival.

1. **Relative to peers.** Tesla sits at the **top** of the three named competitors on gross margin (18.9% vs Ford's 7.1% and GM's 10.2%) [Capital IQ Comparable Analysis export, Operating Statistics tab] but the **bottom** among the profitable pair on EBIT margin trend direction — Tesla's EBIT margin has fallen every year for three straight years (16.8% FY2022 → 4.6% FY2025 → 4.1% LTM [`07_business-quality.md` row: Margin stability]) while GM's LTM EBIT margin (5.5%) currently exceeds Tesla's (4.1%) in absolute terms despite GM's much lower gross margin. BYD's return-on-capital cannot be compared at all — not disclosed anywhere in this pool, only a web-derived, unverified net-margin estimate exists [`08_competitive-map.md` §2].
2. **Absolute (the economic moat test).** The company earns a return on capital **below** its cost of capital: **~2.75%–3.7%** LTM (and **~8.3%–9.4%** even on the more favorable 5-year through-cycle average) **vs ~11.5%** estimated WACC — the decisive test that any structural advantage identified in §2 has not translated into economic value creation.

## 5. Moat Verdict

**No moat proven.**

The strongest candidate is **Technology / IP** (strength 50/100) — proprietary battery-cell/lithium-refining vertical integration, the NACS charging standard now licensed to rival OEMs, and a paid FSD-subscription attach rate near 55% of North America deliveries [`06_value-chain.md` §1] — followed by Distribution (45/100, direct-to-consumer with no dealer network) and Brand (40/100, evidenced by the equity multiple, not by pricing power). But **CLAUDE.md's hard rule applies**: return on capital sits below cost of capital on every basis computed here — LTM (2.75%–3.7% vs ~11.5% WACC) and even the more forgiving 5-year through-cycle average (8.3%–9.4% vs ~11.5% WACC) — so no combination of these structural advantages can be scored a Strong or even a middling Narrow moat; this is a **moat in structure, not economics**: real scale, distribution, and technology assets that have not lifted the return on capital to or above what raising that capital costs. Durability over the next 5 years would need proof that automotive gross margin (16.2% FY2025, falling every year [`07_business-quality.md` row: Margin stability]) stabilizes as the shrinking regulatory-credit line fully runs off, and that FSD/robotaxi/Optimus — still a zero-disclosed-revenue-line item — converts a technology asset into a return-on-capital improvement rather than a growing capital drag; per `07_business-quality.md`'s "fast-changing industry" flag (rate-of-change score 30, ≤40), any such durability claim is discounted further because the winners in EV, battery-chemistry, and autonomous-driving competition are not knowable in advance (CLAUDE.md §24, Filter 5).

**Moat trajectory — eroding.** Return on capital has fallen every year for three straight years and the gap to cost of capital has widened, not narrowed: from **above** WACC in FY2021–FY2022 (10.5%–18.6% vs ~11.5% WACC) to materially **below** WACC every year since (8.9% FY2023, 6.0% FY2024, 2.9% FY2025, 2.75% LTM) [`Tesla Inc NasdaqGS TSLA Financials_Annual.xls`, Ratios tab]. Pricing power is falling in parallel — automotive gross margin fell every year FY2023–FY2025 with management crediting only mix, FX, and a shrinking regulatory-credit line, not sticker-price increases, for holding margin up at all [`06_value-chain.md` §3] — and global BEV volume share was lost to BYD on a full calendar-year-2025 basis (Tesla 8.8% vs BYD 12.1% [`08_competitive-map.md` §3]). Entry/disruption pressure is rising, not falling, per `07_business-quality.md`'s Competitive intensity (28/100) and rate-of-change (30/100) rows. The one genuine counter-signal — automotive-sales revenue up 23–27% year-on-year in Q1–Q2 FY2026 after a Q4 FY2025 decline [`08_competitive-map.md` §3] — is a volume rebound, not evidence the margin or capital-return trend has turned; it does not change the trajectory call.

## 2. Classification

**Governance red flags.**

The signal profile is dominated by how the company is run rather than by how its cash is spent in the ordinary sense: a sprawling network of transactions and capital commitments with the CEO's other controlled entities (SpaceX, xAI, The Boring Company), Delaware derivative litigation alleging breach of fiduciary duty tied to those same relationships, a $132.3 billion maximum-value CEO performance award that dilutes public shareholders while a voting agreement and prior award design have preserved the CEO's own economic and voting position, and a disclosed pattern of booking only "immaterial" reserves against an actual $329 million adverse jury verdict. None of these individually clears the disqualifier-scan's hard triggers (no audit qualification, no going concern, pledging utilization under 1% of collateral value, related-party revenue an immaterial share of total revenue), but together they describe a company whose governance structure concentrates outsized, contingent economic power in one individual with extensive outside interests.

## 3. Most Material Signal

The net share count trajectory — specifically the 2025 CEO Performance Award — is the signal that would most change this classification if it worsens further. The award grants up to 423.7 million shares (about 10.7% of shares outstanding at grant) with a maximum grant-date fair value of $132.3 billion [FY2025 10-K/A, p.5192, p.4168], vesting in 12 tranches tied to market-capitalization milestones from $2.0 trillion to reportedly much higher levels. If additional tranches are certified as earned, or if a similar follow-on award is granted before this one is even fully earned (as happened with the 2018 award), the dilution to public shareholders' economic claim on the company would compound further while the CEO's own percentage ownership and control are structurally protected — this is the one lever that could turn "governance red flags" into a harder disqualifier-level concern.

## 4. Cross-Cutting Patterns

Four independent data points — the failed 2025 bylaw reform (supermajority voting and the 3% derivative-suit threshold both retained), the succession framework gated to the two most speculative pay-package tranches and self-authored by the CEO, the reported SpaceX/xAI merger discussions, and the already-flagged $132.3bn pay package and SpaceX/xAI related-party dealings — point the same direction: every recent governance decision point available in this data pool has widened, not narrowed, the concentration of unchecked economic and legal power in one individual, at the same time minority shareholders' structural tools to challenge that concentration (the derivative-suit threshold, supermajority protections) were left in place rather than reformed.
