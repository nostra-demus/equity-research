## 5. Capital-Allocation Read

Uber's capital allocation is genuinely two different records living in the same company. The organic business is capital-light and highly capital-efficient — capex has stayed near 6–7% of cumulative operating cash flow, and the incremental return on capital committed since the business turned profitable (FY2023–FY2025) works out to roughly 24.6% [§4], comfortably above any reasonable cost of capital. Buybacks were absent for three straight years (FY2021–2023) while share count kept rising on stock-based compensation, only started reducing net share count in FY2025, and even then were priced, on average, above where the stock trades today [§3] — an improving but not yet fully proven use of cash. M&A is where the record breaks down: of the two largest deals with observable post-close results in this pool, Uber Freight's $2.25bn Transplace purchase has produced three straight years of segment revenue decline and continued losses, and the $1.1bn Drizly acquisition was shut down entirely within three years [§2]. The single biggest capital-allocation signal is the pending $14.8bn Delivery Hero acquisition, financed mainly with a new €14.2bn bridge loan that would roughly double Uber's debt, to buy a business Uber already competes with directly — and closing it requires selling overlapping operations in 14 markets for $1.6bn, a quantified opportunity cost, not a hypothetical one [§2]. Net verdict: **mixed** — capital deployed inside the existing platform (organic reinvestment, and buybacks since FY2025) has created real per-share value; capital deployed to buy other companies has, on the evidence available, mostly not, and the company's largest-ever bet on that second approach is still pending.
