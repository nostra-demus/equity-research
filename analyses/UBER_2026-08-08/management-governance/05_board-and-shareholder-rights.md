**Read.** No active poison pill and no dual-class stock or classified board are genuine positives — Uber does not run the "founder-entrenchment" playbook seen at some 2019-era tech IPOs. This is a standard Delaware-IPO-era package, not an outlier, but it is not shareholder-friendly by construction.

## 5. Minority-Shareholder Protection Read

The board is structurally independent and not entrenched by ownership: 90% independent directors, a split chair/CEO, fully independent audit/comp/nominating committees, no controlling promoter or dual-class stock, and related-party balances that are small (≤2% of net worth) and unconnected to any insider group — this is a genuinely different starting point from a promoter-controlled or family-controlled company, and AGM support for pay (93.8% for) and the auditor (98.4% for) confirms shareholders are not in open revolt. Where the protection weakens is procedural: shareholders cannot call a special meeting, cannot act by written consent, cannot vote cumulatively, and cannot fill their own board vacancies — the board holds every lever an unhappy shareholder base would need to force change outside the annual meeting, and a director who loses majority support (as nearly happened to no one here, but structurally could) can have his resignation rejected by the same board he sits on. The single biggest concrete risk to minority holders right now is the July 23, 2026 shareholder derivative lawsuit (Louisiana Sheriffs' Pension & Relief Fund and two other pension funds, filed in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California) naming CEO Khosrowshahi, Chairman Sugar, all eight other sitting directors, the company president, and two former CFOs, alleging the board ignored internal tools — including a 2017-built machine-learning risk model not deployed until 2022 — while the company's disclosed sexual-assault incident count (12,522 for 2017–2022) diverged sharply from an alleged internal total of 400,181 reports over the same period; this is an unresolved, unadjudicated allegation, not a proven finding, but it directly tests whether this board's independence on paper translated into real oversight, and it is the evidence-based reason the verdict here is not "strong." **Overall verdict: adequate** — real independence and no ownership-based entrenchment, offset by shareholder-unfriendly meeting/consent mechanics and a live, board-wide fiduciary-duty allegation that has not yet been tested.
