## 2. Input Cost Pass-Through

Uber's two largest cost inputs — Driver/Courier payments and insurance — move both ways, and the evidence shows management actively chooses to pass savings AND increases through rather than banking them as pure margin. On the cost-increase side, FY2025 cost of revenue rose $4.7 billion (18%), including a $1.6 billion increase in Driver payments/incentives, a $1.6 billion increase in Courier payments/incentives (both scaling with Gross Bookings growth), and an $851 million increase in insurance expense "primarily due to an increase in insurance rate per mile and miles driven in our Mobility business" [FY25 10-K, Item 7 (MD&A), Results of Operations]. There is no disclosed automatic escalator or indexed-pricing clause that mechanically passes these costs to Riders or eaters — Uber instead resets its algorithmic fare/fee schedule discretionarily, and the 10-K warns this exposes it to regulatory caps (see §1). On the cost-decrease side, CFO Balaji Krishnamurthy told analysts that as auto-insurance renewals (effective March 2026) delivered "hundreds of millions of dollars of savings," Uber's "philosophy has been to return that goodness back to the market and consumers see improvement in the pricing environment for Uber rides," which then "translate[d] to acceleration in trip growth" in Los Angeles and San Francisco [Q1 FY26 earnings call, prepared remarks / Q&A, response to Doug Anmuth]. That is a company choosing to give an input-cost tailwind back to customers to buy volume, not a company demonstrating durable pricing power over its downstream customer.

**Supplier / input concentration (quantify).** Uber's core "suppliers" are the labor/capacity providers on its platform (Drivers, Couriers, Carriers) and, for Delivery, merchants. None of these is a single-source dependency in the traditional sense — the CIQ-disclosed supplier list spans hundreds of independent grocers, restaurant chains, and regional retailers, none individually named as material to Uber's cost base [Uber Technologies Inc NYSE UBER Suppliers.rtf, CIQ export]. The 10-K does not disclose a percentage of COGS or Gross Bookings tied to any single Driver, Courier, merchant, or Carrier — Not proven from available data. The one quantified concentration flag in the filing is directional, not sized: "a significant amount of our Delivery Gross Bookings come from a limited number of large restaurant groups and other merchants" [FY25 10-K, Item 1A]. A second disclosed dependency is airport access: 15% of 2025 Mobility Gross Bookings originated from trips starting or ending at an airport, and airport authorities can restrict or ban ridesharing within their boundaries or fail to renew operating agreements [FY25 10-K, Item 1A, "geographic concentration" risk factor] — this is a material access dependency on a small number of gatekeepers (airport authorities), distinct from a COGS-line supplier, but a real single point of leverage against Uber.

## 3. Customer Pricing Power

Uber's own filings and management commentary describe a price-elastic customer base rather than one it can reliably reprice upward. The 10-K states plainly that "consumers have a propensity to shift to the lowest-cost or highest-quality provider" and that Uber "has in the past lowered, and may continue to lower, fares or service fees" to remain competitive against well-capitalized rivals offering discounts and incentives [FY25 10-K, Item 1A, competition risk factors]. Dynamic (surge) pricing — Uber's main lever for charging more at peak demand — is "challenged, banned, limited in emergencies, and capped in certain jurisdictions," including a binding agreement not to exceed government-mandated maximum fares in major Indian cities and a Honolulu ordinance capping surge fares [FY25 10-K, Item 1A, pricing-model risk factor]. The clearest pricing-action evidence in the last 24 months runs in the opposite direction of pricing power: rather than raising fares, Uber CUT prices in Los Angeles and San Francisco in early 2026 as insurance costs eased, and the volume reaction was positive — CFO Krishnamurthy reported "really good elasticity" and accelerating trip growth, calling L.A. "the market with the most significant insurance headwinds over the last few years" now showing trip growth "significantly better than California and the rest of the country" [Q1 FY26 earnings call, Q&A]. Separately, a UK Mobility business-model change cut reported Q2 FY26 revenue by $1.1 billion even as underlying Gross Bookings kept growing 24% — a regulatory/accounting reclassification of how revenue is recognized, not a price increase [FY26 Q2 10-Q, cited in upstream business-identity output]. Taken together, the evidence supports volume-driven growth funded by cost discipline, not sustained price increases pushed through to Riders or eaters.

## 4. Economic Control Verdict

**Mixed** — Uber sets terms unilaterally against a fragmented, individually powerless base of Drivers, Couriers, and small merchants (it dictates the fee schedule; no single Driver or Courier can negotiate), but this control is bounded by driver-supply constraints that force incentive spending, by spreading minimum-pay regulation (NYC, Washington State) and driver-reclassification rulings (France), and by a small number of large restaurant-group merchants that carry real leverage [FY25 10-K, Item 1A]. On the downstream side Uber is a price-taker in practice: consumer demand is explicitly described as elastic and quick to shift to cheaper alternatives, surge pricing is capped by regulators in several markets, and the company's own FY2026 pricing action was a price CUT used to buy volume, not a price increase extracted from a captive base [FY25 10-K, Item 1A; Q1 FY26 earnings call]. The Freight segment (about 10% of FY2025 revenue: $5,099 million of $52,017 million [FY25 10-K, p.49, Note 13]) is weak on both sides — a commoditized broker in a soft freight cycle with no pricing power over Shippers or Carriers.

## 5. The Single Biggest Bargaining Risk

Driver-classification and minimum-pay regulation spreading to more jurisdictions is the relationship most likely to hurt Uber if it deteriorates further: it would force Uber to either raise Rider prices into an already price-elastic, discount-prone market or absorb materially higher Driver-side costs, and the company's own 10-K flags this as an active, expanding legal and legislative front (California, Massachusetts, Washington State, France, and ongoing DOJ/regulatory inquiries into Driver and Courier earnings) [FY25 10-K, Item 1A, "Driver Classification Developments"].
