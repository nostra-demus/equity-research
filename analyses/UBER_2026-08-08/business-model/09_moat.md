# Moat — UBER

## 1. Named Competitors

(Inherited from `08_competitive-map.md`, itself sourced to Uber's own FY25 10-K, Item 1, "Competitive Environment," p.5.)

- **Lyft, Inc.** (NasdaqGS: LYFT) — closest same-business, same-geography peer: US/Canada ridesharing only, roughly one-eighth of Uber's LTM revenue, thinner margins.
- **DiDi Global Inc.** (OTCPK: DIDI.Y) — global-scale by revenue (~62% of Uber's LTM revenue) but structurally loss-making; overlaps with Uber mainly in Latin America, not China, where Uber instead holds a minority equity stake in DiDi itself.
- **Bolt Technology OÜ** (private, Estonia) — regional challenger in Europe/Africa, ~1/11th of Uber's Mobility revenue, only just turned its first annual profit in 2025.
- Ola (ANI Technologies) is also named directly in the 10-K but omitted from scoring here — private, no financials in the data pool [`08_competitive-map.md` §5].

## 2. Moat Sources

| Possible Moat | Present? (Y/N) | Evidence | Strength /100 |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Brand | Y (weak) | Uber holds an estimated 76% US rideshare share vs. Lyft's 24% [Web: Bloomberg Second Measure, US rideshare data, 2026 (unverified, dated), cited via `08_competitive-map.md` §3], suggesting real awareness/preference. But the company's own filing undercuts brand-driven pricing power: "consumers have a propensity to shift to the lowest-cost or highest-quality provider" and Uber "has in the past lowered, and may continue to lower, fares or service fees" to compete [FY25 10-K, Item 1A, competition risk factors, cited in `06_value-chain.md` §3]. A brand strong enough to defend share but not strong enough to defend price is a weak moat source. | 35 |
| Cost advantage | Y | Mobility segment Adjusted EBITDA margin rose from 22.4% (FY24) to 26.6% (FY25) [FY25 10-K, Note 13, p.114, cited in `07_business-quality.md` §1] — a real, growing cost efficiency versus Lyft's roughly break-even LTM EBITDA margin (-0.1%) on one-eighth the scale [`08_competitive-map.md` §2, Competitor A]. The advantage looks like scale economics (fixed platform/tech and insurance costs spread over ~8x more volume) rather than a structurally lower unit cost that a well-funded rival cannot replicate — Lyft and DiDi run the same driver-payment and insurance cost structure, just at smaller scale. | 55 |
| Distribution | Y | 15,000+ cities in 70+ countries, 202 million MAPCs (Q4 FY25), and 13,567 million trips (FY2025) [FY25 10-K, p.1, p.49, cited in `02_business-identity.md` §1] give Uber a distribution footprint no named competitor matches outside China/Southeast Asia (where Uber does not operate directly). Uber One (46 million members) cross-sells Mobility, Delivery, and grocery off the same distribution base [FY25 10-K, p.1]. | 60 |
| Scale | Y | Uber's LTM total revenue ($55,227M) is roughly 8x Lyft's ($6,516.6M) and about 1.6x DiDi's ($34,391.3M) [`08_competitive-map.md` §2, Capital IQ Comparable Analysis export, as-of 2026-08-06]. Scale alone is not proof of a moat (DiDi is larger than Lyft and still loss-making), but combined with the cost-advantage row above it is a real, evidenced gap against Lyft specifically. | 60 |
| Technology / IP | N | The 10-K frames autonomous-vehicle technology as a competitive THREAT, not an owned advantage: Uber "may fail to offer autonomous vehicle technologies on our platform at competitive scale, fail to offer such technologies... before our competitors" and Waymo "has already launched its own commercialized robotaxi fleet independent of Uber's platform" [FY25 10-K, p.2229, p.2744, cited in `07_business-quality.md` §1]. No patent portfolio or proprietary-technology claim is cited anywhere in the pool as a barrier to Lyft, DiDi, or Bolt, all of whom run comparable matching/routing/dispatch apps. | 15 |
| Licenses / regulation | N | Regulation reads as a shared cost burden across all named competitors, not a differentiator that favors Uber over them — the 10-K describes ridesharing being "blocked, capped, or suspended" in multiple countries and active driver-classification litigation across many jurisdictions [`10_external-dependency.md` §1, "Regulation" and "Government policy" rows, both rated High dependence]. Lyft, DiDi, and Bolt already hold operating licenses in their respective markets; this is not a barrier that keeps them out. | 15 |
| Network effects | Y | Uber's own filing names the mechanism directly: it must maintain "critical mass" of Drivers and Riders in each city or "supply liquidity" degrades [FY25 10-K, Item 1A, "critical mass" risk factor, cited in `06_value-chain.md` §1]. More drivers online shortens wait times, which attracts more riders, which attracts more drivers — a textbook two-sided network effect, and Uber's larger scale (row above) means this loop runs faster for Uber than for Lyft in shared markets. The effect is real but bounded: riders and drivers "commonly multi-home across Uber, Lyft, Bolt, Didi, Ola with no lock-in mechanism beyond habit" [FY25 10-K, p.1763, p.2502, cited in `07_business-quality.md` §1] — multi-homing lets supply and demand split across platforms instead of tipping to one winner. | 50 |
| Switching costs | N | The 10-K states this as a risk factor in its own words: "the low switching costs between competitor platforms or services" threatens Driver and consumer retention [FY25 10-K, p.2666, cited in `07_business-quality.md` §1]. No contracts bind riders, eaters, or drivers to Uber [`07_business-quality.md` §1, Repeat/recurring revenue row]. This is a company admission, not an inference. | 10 |
| Natural resource access | N | Not applicable — Uber owns no vehicles, fuel supply, or extractable resource; Drivers and Carriers own and fuel their own vehicles [`07_business-quality.md` §1, Capital intensity row]. | 0 |
| Location advantage | Y (narrow) | Airport curbside access is a real, scarce point of leverage: 15% of FY2025 Mobility Gross Bookings originated at airports, where "airport authorities can restrict or ban ridesharing... or fail to renew operating agreements" [FY25 10-K, Item 1A, "geographic concentration" risk factor, cited in `06_value-chain.md` §2]. Holding an existing airport operating agreement is a real advantage over a would-be new entrant, but the filing frames it as a risk Uber must defend, not a proven exclusivity versus the three named competitors (Lyft also holds airport agreements in its US/Canada markets). | 25 |

No single source above clears a "strong" bar (≥61) on its own. The closest to a real, durable edge — network effects reinforced by scale and cost advantage — is capped by explicit, filing-disclosed multi-homing and low switching costs on both sides of the marketplace.

**The economic moat test.**

> Return on capital **mixed — at-to-modestly-above cost of capital on the latest year/LTM read, but below it on a 5-year through-cycle average**: FY2025 ROIC (own calc, normalized 21% tax rate, average invested capital net-of-cash) **14.7%** vs estimated WACC **~9.7%** (+~500bps); CIQ vendor "Return on Capital" **9.4%** (FY2025) / **10.6%** (LTM) vs the same ~9.7% WACC (roughly flat to +90bps); but the CIQ vendor series' own 5-year through-cycle average (FY2021–FY2025: -9.4%, -4.9%, +3.1%, +6.0%, +9.4%) is **+0.85%**, roughly **-885bps below** the same ~9.7% WACC — [Capital IQ Financials_Annual export, Ratios tab, "Return on Capital %" row, CIQ; WACC = CAPM estimate, Inference not from filings, basis below].

- **Metric match:** Uber is an operating company (asset-light two-sided marketplace, not a bank/insurer/REIT) — ROIC vs WACC is the correct pairing, consistent with `07_business-quality.md` §0's sector-overlay finding of "no match" (generic operating-company grammar applies).
- **Cross-check of the headline figure.** I computed my own NOPAT ÷ average invested capital rather than accept the CIQ vendor's "Return on Capital %" row at face value: **NOPAT (FY2025) = Operating Income $5,565M × (1 − 21% normalized tax rate) = $4,396M** [FY25 10-K, Note 13, p.114 (Operating Income); tax-rate basis below]. **Invested capital, net-of-cash** = Total Debt (LT debt + current portion + finance/operating leases, $12,302M FY2025-end) + Total Equity ($28,083M) − Cash & ST Investments ($7,633M) = **$32,752M** (FY2025-end) / **$29,843.5M** (FY2024–FY2025 average) [Financials_Annual export, Balance Sheet tab, CIQ]. That gives **ROIC = $4,396M ÷ $29,843.5M average = 14.7%** — materially higher than the CIQ vendor's own 9.4% (FY2025) / 10.6% (LTM) "Return on Capital %" figure on the same Ratios tab. **This is a real divergence and is flagged, not smoothed over**: the CIQ figure's exact denominator/tax treatment is not disclosed in the export, so it cannot be fully reconciled from this pool; possible explanations include CIQ including the ~$9.4B of non-operating long-term equity-stake investments (Aurora, DiDi, Grab — whose gains/losses already sit below Operating Income and are excluded from my NOPAT) inside its capital base, which would dilute the return, or CIQ using the actual (unnormalized) reported tax rate in some years of its average. Per the conservative default (CLAUDE.md §4), **the lower, vendor-sourced figure is used as the primary read for the verdict below**, while my own computed figure is shown for transparency.
- **Gross-capital check (cash-netting sensitivity).** Uber is close to net-debt-neutral rather than deeply net cash (CIQ "Net Debt/EBITDA" = 0.011x for FY2025, essentially zero) [Financials_Annual export, Ratios tab, CIQ], but cash-netting still matters: on a **gross-capital basis (no cash netting)** — Invested Capital = Total Debt + Total Equity, average $37,148.5M (FY2024–FY2025) — the same $4,396M NOPAT gives **ROIC = 11.8%**, about 290bps lower than the net-of-cash figure (14.7%). Shown alongside per the required net-cash sensitivity check; the effect here is real but moderate, not the dramatic collapse a true net-cash balance sheet would produce.
- **Normalized tax rate, stated and stripped.** The ROIC numerator uses a **normalized 21% structural tax rate** (the US federal statutory rate stated in Uber's own tax-rate reconciliation table) [FY25 10-K, Income Taxes note, "Federal statutory income tax rate... 21.0%"], **not** the distorted GAAP reported rates. FY2024's reported effective tax rate was **-139.6%** and FY2025's was **-74.9%** (both tax BENEFITS, not expenses) [FY25 10-K, Income Taxes note, p. (Effective tax rate table)] — driven entirely by two one-off, non-cash items: a **$6.4 billion release of US federal and state deferred-tax-asset valuation allowances in Q4 2024**, and a **$5.0 billion release of the Netherlands deferred-tax-asset valuation allowance during FY2025** [FY25 10-K, Income Taxes note, "2025 Compared to 2024"]. Using either reported rate in NOPAT would overstate the operating return by tens of percentage points on a one-off, non-recurring accounting event. Analyst consensus estimates a forward effective tax rate of roughly 18–21% for FY2026 and beyond [UberTechnologies EstimatesReport, Consensus tab, Effective Tax Rate % row, CIQ], corroborating 21% as a reasonable structural anchor. **This 21% rate is the anchor for `valuation/04_intrinsic-dcf` to reconcile to, since this module runs first.**
- **Through-cycle basis, latest year labelled peak.** `07_business-quality.md` §4 explicitly labels FY2025's margins as "near-peak, post-recovery readings on a business that ran negative EBITDA margins for its first six disclosed years (FY2016–FY2021)," and flags Freight as still cyclically depressed (FY2025 segment Adjusted EBITDA margin -0.6%, "challenging freight market cycle"). Consistent with that read, the CIQ "Return on Capital %" series was negative every year from FY2017 through FY2022 and only turned positive in FY2023 (+3.1%), rising to FY2024 (+6.0%) and FY2025 (+9.4%) [Financials_Annual export, Ratios tab, CIQ]. **Uber has not yet run a positive-return-on-capital year through a demand shock** — its one severe downturn on record (COVID, FY2020 revenue -14.3%) landed while the company was still deeply negative on this metric. The 3-year post-turnaround trend (FY2023→FY2025: 3.1%→6.0%→9.4%) is genuinely improving but is not a "through the cycle" track record in the sense of having been tested by a downturn while already profitable.
- **Cost-of-capital basis.** No company-disclosed WACC or hurdle rate exists in this pool (the 10-K's own "weighted-average cost of capital analysis" language appears only in a purchase-accounting/goodwill-impairment estimation-methodology footnote, with no rate given [FY25 10-K, Note 1 / Note 3 accounting-policy language]). **Estimated via CAPM, Inference, not from filings**: risk-free rate 4.6% [Web: 10-year US Treasury yield, ~4.6–4.65%, early August 2026 (dated, unverified)]; beta 1.15 (5-year) [Capital IQ Comparable Analysis export / Public Company Profile, as-of 2026-08-06]; equity risk premium 5.0% (standard long-run US estimate, not company-disclosed — Inference); cost of equity = 4.6% + 1.15 × 5.0% = **10.35%**. Cost of debt: pre-tax ≈ interest expense $440M ÷ LT+current debt $10,521M FY2025 = **4.2%**, after the same 21% normalized tax = **3.3%**. Weights from market values: equity $139,261.7M, total debt $14,731M (E/(D+E)=90.4%, D/(D+E)=9.6%) [Public Company Profile, CIQ, priced 2026-08-05]. **WACC = 0.904 × 10.35% + 0.096 × 3.3% ≈ 9.7%.**

## 4. Where The Company Sits

1. **Relative to peers:** Uber sits at the **top of named peers** on margin and capital efficiency. LTM gross margin (40.8%) and EBIT margin (12.1%) both beat Lyft (35.6% / -2.2%) and DiDi (15.6% / -2.6%); Uber is the only one of the three CIQ-covered names with a positive EBIT margin and a positive, disclosed return on capital [`08_competitive-map.md` §2; Section 3 table above]. Bolt is smaller and only marginally profitable (operating margin ~0.9%).
2. **Absolute (the economic moat test):** Uber's return on capital sits **at-to-modestly-above** its estimated cost of capital on the latest fiscal year and LTM read (CIQ 9.4%/10.6% vs ~9.7% WACC; own-calc 14.7% vs ~9.7% WACC), but **below** its cost of capital on a 5-year through-cycle average (+0.85% vs ~9.7% WACC) — the decisive test, since a single peak-adjacent year should not carry a moat verdict on its own (§3 above).

## 5. Moat Verdict

**Narrow moat.** The strongest evidenced source is the combination of **network effects + scale + cost advantage**: Uber's own filing names "critical mass" of Drivers and Riders as something it must actively maintain, its scale (~8x Lyft's revenue) supports a Mobility Adjusted EBITDA margin (26.6%, FY2025) far above any named public peer, and this margin gap now finally translates into a positive, peer-leading return on capital. But two things cap it at Narrow rather than Strong. First, the filing itself discloses the two features that most directly blunt a network-effect moat — low switching costs and common multi-homing across Uber, Lyft, Bolt, DiDi, and Ola — so the advantage defends share more than it defends price. Second, and decisively per the hard rule: Uber's return on capital has been above its estimated ~9.7% cost of capital only for the last 1–2 years (CIQ Return on Capital: +6.0% FY2024, +9.4% FY2025, +10.6% LTM), following a decade of value destruction including its one severe demand shock on record (COVID) landing while returns were still deeply negative; the 5-year through-cycle average (+0.85%) remains far below cost of capital. This is a moat that has only just crossed into being economic, not one proven sustained through a cycle — the durability test over the next five years is whether Uber can hold a return on capital above ~9.7% through the next demand downturn and through the unresolved autonomous-vehicle transition, where Waymo already operates a competing fleet independent of Uber's platform [`07_business-quality.md` §1, rate-of-change row, scored 32/100 — a fast-changing-industry flag per CLAUDE.md §24, Filter 5, that discounts durability further].

**Moat trajectory: Widening.** Evidence over the last five years is one-directional and specific: CIQ Return on Capital rose from -4.9% (FY2022) to +3.1% (FY2023) to +6.0% (FY2024) to +9.4% (FY2025) to +10.6% (LTM); EBIT margin rose from -5.7% (FY2022) to +2.98% (FY2023) to +6.4% (FY2024) to +10.7% (FY2025) to +12.1% (LTM); Mobility segment Adjusted EBITDA margin rose from 22.4% to 26.6% (FY2024→FY2025) [Financials_Annual export, Ratios tab, CIQ; FY25 10-K, Note 13]. Uber also grew LTM revenue faster (16.68%) than Lyft (9.36%) or DiDi (10.04%) over the same trailing window [`08_competitive-map.md` §2–3], consistent with share stability-to-gains rather than erosion. The widening trend is real but should be read alongside the low rate-of-change/disruption score (32/100) in `07_business-quality.md` — an AV-driven change in industry structure could interrupt this trajectory faster than the multi-year trend line alone would suggest, so the widening read should not be extrapolated mechanically into `valuation/04_intrinsic-dcf`'s durable-advantage period without discounting for that risk.
