## 2. Classification

**Capital allocation concerns.** The governance mechanics — auditor tenure and opinion, absence of a controlling promoter, independent board chair, orderly CFO succession, working-capital trend, and capex discipline — are all clean. The problem is specifically how capital is being deployed: Uber has become a serial acquirer (roughly a dozen deals since 2019) and is now committing to the largest of them, a $14.8 billion takeover of Delivery Hero funded mainly with a new €14.2 billion bridge loan against an existing debt base of $12.6 billion — a deal that would roughly double Uber's debt to fund a business (food delivery) it already competes in directly through Uber Eats. This is a capital-allocation issue, not a governance-opacity or ownership issue.

## 3. Most Material Signal

The acquisition pattern — and specifically the pending Delivery Hero takeover — is the single signal that would most change this classification if it worsens. Uber already competes with Delivery Hero in overlapping markets through Uber Eats, and the deal structure requires selling the overlapping businesses in 14 markets to a third party (SSW Partners, $1.6 billion) to get it done — itself a sign the combination isn't a clean bolt-on. If the bridge financing converts into permanent debt at scale (management states it "expect[s] to incur additional debt in connection with our proposed acquisition of Delivery Hero" [Q2 FY26 10-Q, p.23727 region]) while integration lags or the deal fails to close as expected (the offer isn't expected to close until H2 2027, per the 10-Q), this moves from "concerns" toward a genuine balance-sheet and capital-discipline problem.
