# External Dependency Check — UBER

## 1. Dependency Table

| External Variable | Dependency Level (Low / Mid / High) | Why It Matters | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commodity prices (fuel) | Mid | Uber does not buy fuel directly, but fuel is a driver's biggest out-of-pocket cost. When fuel prices rise, drivers spend less time on the platform or leave, which shrinks driver supply ("network liquidity") and pushes up what Uber has to pay in incentives to keep drivers online. The 10-K names the 2022 Russia-Ukraine and Middle East conflicts as past fuel-price shocks that raised driver costs. This is a pass-through risk, not a direct cost line on Uber's own income statement. | [FY25 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors — "Increases in fuel, food, labor, energy, and other costs due to inflation..."] |
| Interest rates | Low | Uber's credit agreement was fully undrawn as of Dec 31, 2025, so a rate move does not hit interest expense on that facility. A hypothetical 100 basis point (0.01 percentage point times 100 — a common unit for rate moves) rise in rates would cut the fair value of Uber's fixed-rate notes by $538 million — a balance-sheet mark, not a cash cost, since the notes carry fixed coupons. Uber also holds $9.6 billion of cash and $9.4 billion of short-term/restricted investments, so higher rates raise interest income too, partly offsetting the debt-side effect. | [FY25 10-K, Item 7A Quantitative and Qualitative Disclosures About Market Risk — "Interest Rate Risk"] |
| FX | Mid | About 49% of FY2025 revenue came from outside the US and Canada (EMEA $16.4bn + LATAM $3.3bn + APAC $5.9bn, out of $52.0bn total), so a large share of results are earned in currencies other than the dollar and then translated back. In Q2 FY26, reported revenue grew 12% year-over-year versus 11% on a constant-currency basis (the growth rate with currency swings stripped out) — a roughly 1 percentage-point FX tailwind that quarter; reported Gross Bookings grew 24% versus 22% constant-currency, a roughly 2 percentage-point tailwind. Uber uses forward contracts to hedge some of this exposure but states the hedges "do not entirely eliminate" the effect. | [FY25 10-K, Item 7A — "Foreign Currency Risk"; Q2 FY26 10-Q, MD&A — Financial and Operational Highlights table] |
| Freight / logistics rates | Mid | Uber Freight is Uber's own segment that brokers truckload capacity between shippers and carriers — its revenue per load moves directly with the trucking freight market. FY2025 Freight Gross Bookings fell 1% (constant currency) "as a result of challenging freight market cycles," and the segment posted a small EBITDA loss (-$33 million) on $5,099 million of revenue (about 10% of total company revenue). Freight's small share of total revenue and profit limits how much this can move the whole company's results. | [FY25 10-K, MD&A — Results of Operations, Gross Bookings discussion; FY25 10-K, Financials_Annual export, Segments tab] |
| Government policy | High | Whether Drivers are legally independent contractors or employees is the single largest policy variable in the business model. The 10-K lists ongoing litigation, legislation, and regulatory challenges to driver classification "in the United States and abroad," and states that if Drivers had to be reclassified as employees, Uber "would incur significant additional expenses" for wages, benefits, and taxes. Cities and states have also imposed direct policy costs: a Chicago per-trip surcharge, a San Francisco ridesharing surcharge, and a Washington state minimum-pay law for drivers; California's Prop 22 already requires a guaranteed minimum-earnings floor and driver insurance subsidies. | [FY25 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors — "Our business would be adversely affected if Drivers were classified as employees..."; FY25 10-K, Item 1A — city/state surcharge and minimum-pay examples] |
| Regulation | High | Beyond driver classification, Uber operates under license and permit regimes that can be revoked, capped, or denied city by city and country by country. The 10-K lists ridesharing being "blocked, capped, or suspended" in Argentina, Germany, Italy, Japan, South Korea, and Spain at various points, airport-specific licensing and outright ridesharing bans at some non-US airports, and payment-services licensing requirements in the EU, UK, and Mexico that could force Uber to "cease or limit business operations" if licenses are denied or revoked. An increasing number of municipalities have also proposed delivery fee caps and Mobility surge-pricing caps. | [FY25 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors — "In certain jurisdictions... our ridesharing business model has been blocked, capped, or suspended"; FY25 10-K, Item 1A — airport and payment-licensing risk factors] |
| Weather | Low | The 10-K flags weather events and climate-related physical risk (storms, flooding, wildfires) as one of several "catastrophic event" categories, alongside disease and war, that could disrupt operations, and notes rising insurance costs tied to more frequent extreme weather. It is grouped with other tail risks rather than singled out as a recurring driver of results, and no dollar impact is quantified. | [FY25 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors — "We face climate-related physical and transition risks..."; Item 1A — "Occurrence of a catastrophic event...weather event...could adversely impact our business"] |
| Geopolitics | Low-Mid | The 10-K names the Russia-Ukraine war and conflicts in the Middle East as sources of sanctions, embargo, and regional-instability risk, and as a past driver of higher fuel prices for Drivers and Carriers. These are cited as background risks that could "heighten" other risk factors rather than a standalone driver management has quantified for FY2025 results. | [FY25 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors — "the broader consequences of conflicts in the Middle East and the conflict between Russia and Ukraine..."] |
| Consumer cycle | Mid-High | Uber states plainly that "consumer preferences tend to shift to lower-cost alternatives during recessionary periods," that Delivery spending is "generally considered discretionary" and would see a "disproportionate effect" from a pullback, and that a slowdown could also work in reverse — better economic conditions can pull Drivers into other jobs and shrink driver supply. Roughly half of FY2025 Gross Bookings mix, and one-third of revenue ($17.2bn of $52.0bn), sits in Delivery, the segment most exposed to discretionary-spending swings. | [FY25 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors — "Our performance is subject to economic conditions and their impact on levels of discretionary consumer spending"] |
| Industrial cycle | Mid | This overlaps with the Freight row above: Uber Freight's revenue per load and Gross Bookings track the broader trucking/industrial freight cycle, which the 10-K describes as "challenging" through FY2025. Because Freight is about 10% of total revenue and EBITDA-negative, a further industrial-cycle downturn would weigh on one loss-making segment rather than the consolidated growth story, which is being carried by Mobility (57% of FY2025 revenue) and Delivery (33%). | [FY25 10-K, MD&A — Results of Operations; Financials_Annual export, Segments tab] |

## 2. Sensitivity, If Disclosed

| Variable | Disclosed Sensitivity | Citation |
|---|---|---|

A hypothetical 100bp change in rates "would not have a material effect" on the fair value of Uber's marketable debt securities. | [FY25 10-K, Item 7A — Interest Rate Risk / Investment Risk] |
| FX (revenue growth) | Q2 FY26: reported revenue growth of 12% year-over-year versus 11% on a constant-currency basis (about +1 percentage point FX tailwind). Gross Bookings: reported growth of 24% versus 22% constant-currency (about +2 percentage point FX tailwind). FY2025 full year: Gross Bookings grew 19% reported versus 20% constant-currency (about -1 percentage point FX headwind for the year, the opposite direction from the most recent quarter — evidence the FX effect swings both ways and is not a one-way drag). | [Q2 FY26 10-Q, MD&A — Financial and Operational Highlights table; FY25 10-K, MD&A — Results of Operations] |

No commodity-price, freight-rate, or weather sensitivity table (e.g., "a 10% rise in X changes revenue by $Y") is disclosed anywhere in the pool. Uber does not publish a fuel-price or driver-cost sensitivity table.

## 3. Classification

**Partly externally driven.** Uber carries real, named exposure to government policy (driver classification), local regulation (licenses, permits, fee caps), FX translation, and the consumer spending cycle — none of these are noise. But management has demonstrated actual levers against every one of them: pricing (fare and delivery-fee increases to offset cost inflation), FX forward hedges, geographic diversification (no single region dominates results), and a track record of adapting the business model to adverse rulings without existential damage — e.g., California's Prop 22 (2020) and the UK Supreme Court's 2021 worker-status ruling both raised driver-related costs but did not stop Uber's Gross Bookings, revenue, or EBITDA from continuing to grow through FY2025 ($52.0bn revenue, up 18% year-over-year; $8.7bn total segment EBITDA, up 35%). The one dependency Uber cannot fully hedge or price around is a sweeping, multi-jurisdiction loss on driver classification — that is the single item that could force a structural change to the business model rather than a cost that gets passed through.

## 4. External Dependency Risk Score

**48/100** (higher = worse — this score is inverted; a high number means more dangerous external dependence, not that the company scores well).

This sits in the 41-60 "material external exposure, mixed mitigation" band. It is not lower because government-policy risk (driver classification, city/state minimum-pay and fee-cap rules) and regulatory risk (licenses that can be revoked market by market) are both rated High with direct cited evidence of past cost and market-access impact — these are not hypothetical. It is not higher because FX is hedged and geographically diversified, Freight/industrial-cycle exposure sits in only ~10% of revenue, interest-rate exposure is mostly a non-cash balance-sheet mark against a large net-cash position, and the company's own multi-year record shows it absorbing regulatory shocks (Prop 22, UK worker-status ruling) while continuing to grow revenue and EBITDA.

## 5. The Single Biggest Lever

Government policy on driver classification: if courts or legislators in a large market (most consequentially the US) forced Uber to treat Drivers as employees rather than independent contractors, the 10-K itself says Uber "would incur significant additional expenses" for wages, benefits, and taxes and might not retain "a majority of the Drivers currently using our platform" — the one external variable with the power to force a structural change to the business model rather than a cost Uber can price through [FY25 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors — driver classification risk factor].
