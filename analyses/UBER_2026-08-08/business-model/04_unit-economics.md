# Unit Economics — UBER

## 1. Natural Unit

The natural economic unit for Mobility (the dominant segment — 57.0% of FY25 revenue, 69.1% of FY25 segment profit [`03_segment-map.md`, Section 2]) is **the trip (ride)**: one rider matched to one Driver on Uber's marketplace.

Uber does not disclose Mobility-specific Trip or Monthly Active Platform Consumer ("MAPC") counts — it only reports Trips and MAPCs on a combined Mobility-plus-Delivery basis ("Trips" is defined as "the number of completed consumer Mobility rides and Delivery orders in a given period" [FY25 10-K, "Certain Key Metrics"]). That means a true Mobility-only Gross Bookings-per-Trip or contribution-margin-per-trip figure **cannot be computed from filings**. What Uber does disclose at the Mobility-segment level, every quarter, in dollars: Gross Bookings (the total dollar value of Mobility rides, before Uber's cut), Revenue (Uber's cut), and Segment Adjusted EBITDA / Segment Operating Income (profit after Driver payments, incentives, insurance and other variable costs). This report therefore builds the required table using **$1 of Mobility Gross Bookings** as the measurable proxy unit — the closest available substitute for "one trip" given the disclosure gap, and itself a legitimate unit (comparable to how an asset manager's unit is "$1 of AUM"). This substitution is stated explicitly, not smoothed over, per the missing-disclosure item named in Section 3.

Delivery's natural unit is the order (one meal, grocery, or retail delivery); Freight's is the load (one truckload booking). Both face the same disclosure limit — Uber does not report Delivery-only or Freight-only Trip/order counts either.

## 2. Unit Economics Table

Unit = $1 of Mobility Gross Bookings, unless noted. Mobility Gross Bookings are built by summing the four disclosed quarters of each fiscal year: FY24 = $18,670M + $20,554M + $21,002M + $22,798M = $83,024M; FY25 = $21,182M + $23,762M + $25,111M + $27,442M = $97,497M [FY25 10-K, "Certain Key Metrics," Gross Bookings by segment table]. This reconciles exactly to the $193,454M FY25 total Gross Bookings figure once Delivery ($90,864M) and Freight ($5,093M) are added, confirming the segment split is internally consistent.

## 3. Value Creation Read

**Creates value**, on the one basis Uber actually discloses — but the read is partial. Every disclosed year since Mobility Adjusted EBITDA was first broken out, the segment has earned a positive and slowly rising profit on every dollar of Gross Bookings it books: 7.83% in FY24 rising to 8.10% in FY25, and 7.28% rising to 7.64% on the newer Segment Operating Income basis between Q2 FY25 and Q2 FY26 [FY25 10-K, Note 13; Q2 FY26 10-Q, Note 10]. Because Driver payments, incentives, insurance and network costs — the largest variable costs tied to each ride — are already netted out of that margin, a positive and improving figure is meaningful evidence that incremental Gross Bookings are profitable at the margin, not just at scale. What the math cannot prove is the classic contribution-margin-times-lifetime-versus-acquisition-cost test required to call this "value creation" in the strict unit-economics sense: Uber discloses neither a Driver- or rider-acquisition cost nor a rider/Driver retention curve, so there is no way to check whether the profit earned per dollar of Gross Bookings recovers what was spent to win that dollar in the first place. The single most valuable missing disclosure is a Mobility-specific Trip count (or Driver-incentive spend per active Driver) split out from the combined Mobility-plus-Delivery Trips figure Uber currently reports — without it, "creates value" here rests on a marketplace-dollar profitability trend, not a proven unit-level payback.

The margin trend itself is short: it has risen for roughly three to four years running since Uber returned to consistent Mobility profitability, not across a full economic or competitive cycle, so the 8.10% figure should be read as a level achieved during a period of strong post-pandemic demand recovery and market-share consolidation, not assumed as a permanent floor.

## 4. Sensitivity

The take rate (Mobility revenue as a percentage of Mobility Gross Bookings) is the input most likely to move the value-creation read if it shifts 20%, and it has already shown it can: a single regulatory change — Uber's UK business-model change effective January 2, 2026 — cut the take rate by roughly 530 basis points in one quarter (Q2 FY25's 30.7% to Q2 FY26's 25.4%), of which management attributes about 400 basis points to an accounting reclassification (moving cost out of cost of revenue, an "optical impact" per the CFO) and the remaining roughly 100 basis points to a deliberate pricing choice — investing in lower-cost offerings such as Moto in Brazil [Q2 FY26 transcript, CFO Q&A]. On the cost side, Driver payments and incentives (+$1.6 billion in FY25) and insurance expense (+$851 million in FY25, driven by "an increase in insurance rate per mile and miles driven") are the two variable-cost lines management calls out as growing fastest [FY25 10-K, MD&A, p.54–55]; insurance cost per mile is a structural risk unique to Mobility (Uber flags that "inflation has increased and is expected to increase our insurance costs" as a general risk factor) and is not something Uber controls the way it can dial Driver incentives up or down. Of the two, the take-rate move is the more historically volatile of the two on the evidence available — it produced a five-hundred-basis-point swing in a single quarter from one jurisdiction's regulatory change, a bigger one-period move than either disclosed cost line.
