## 2. Non-GAAP / Adjustment Aggressiveness

| Signal | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Recurring "one-off" addbacks | Amazon does NOT publish a formal adjusted EBITDA or adjusted EBIT. The company's only official non-GAAP measure is Free Cash Flow. In Q3 2025, management verbally described underlying operating income ($21.7B ex-charges) but did not issue a press-release table for it. Recurring "Other operating expense" has appeared every year ($62M FY2021, $163M FY2022, $767M FY2023, $763M FY2024, $4,639M FY2025) — treating this as perpetually "non-recurring" overstates normalized earnings. | Does adjusted framing flatter reality? | Management does NOT issue adjusted EPS — this is actually conservative relative to peers. Analysts and the financial press use GAAP EPS in headlines. Operating income — the clean line — was never distorted. **Assessment:** Amazon's non-GAAP posture is notably conservative by large-cap standards — no adjusted EBITDA, no adjusted EPS, no formal exclusion of SBC or restructuring. The investment-gain distortion in reported EPS is real and recurring, but it is disclosed clearly and is a GAAP accounting consequence of a large strategic investment portfolio, not a management-engineered adjustment. The server useful-life changes are the one area where the accounting-estimate choices moved earnings in convenient directions, though each was disclosed and the two-year net effect was roughly neutral.

## 3. Disclosure Transparency

Segment economics are disclosed in useful detail: AWS revenue, operating income, and operating margin are reported separately each quarter with sequential and year-over-year comparisons. North America and International operating income are also disclosed. One transparency gap stands out: **Amazon does not disclose the split of capital expenditures between maintenance and growth**, forcing analysts to treat all capex as equivalent — this matters significantly when $131.8B in gross capex includes both sustaining and growth components, and when management argues that FCF will recover as growth capex moderates. A second gap: **advertising revenue is reported as a single line without a regional or format breakdown** (sponsored products, DSP, video). Both of these limitations are consistent with peer practice in e-commerce/cloud and do not represent deliberate obscuring of known-bad data.

The AWS backlog ($364B at Q1 2026, up from $244B at Q4 2025) and remaining performance obligation disclosure in the 10-K Note 10 is a positive transparency signal — management discloses forward revenue commitments that provide independent evidence for the capex thesis, going beyond what most companies disclose. The FY2025 10-K also discloses total purchase obligations of $439.7B, which gives investors visibility into the full committed spend level. There is no evidence of disclosure becoming less transparent over the observable three-year period; the backlog and commitment disclosures have expanded over FY2023–FY2025 alongside the growing investment cycle.

## 4. Tone in Bad Times

**Q3 2025 was the worst quarter in the observable record in terms of headline miss** — reported operating income of $17.4B looked $4.3B below underlying performance due to the FTC settlement and severance. The tone in Jassy's prepared remarks was direct: the charges were named in the first sentence, quantified, and the underlying performance ($21.7B, "$1.2B above the high end of our guidance range") was then stated clearly. The CFO repeated both figures. There was no promotional language attempting to draw attention away from the headline number before disclosing the charges. Q&A on the Q3 call did not address the charges directly (no analyst pushed on FTC specifics), and management did not volunteer additional context on the settlement beyond what was in the press release. The tone was matter-of-fact, not defensive.

On the worst FCF data ($7.7B strict FCF in FY2025; negative LTM strict FCF at Q1 2026), the Q4 2025 Q&A was probed directly by Mark Mahaney (Evercore) who asked about "financial guardrails or governors" on capex. Jassy responded with a substantive answer about monetization rate and ROIC confidence, grounded in the $244B backlog and Trainium unit economics, rather than deflecting or pivoting to a generic growth narrative. He acknowledged: "the faster AWS grows, the more short-term capex we'll spend" and "we're going to invest aggressively here." This is direct acknowledgment of the FCF cost of the strategy, not cheerleading.

## 5. Candor Read

Management can be trusted to tell the truth when results are bad. The strongest evidence is the Q3 2025 call, where the CEO opened his remarks by immediately quantifying the $4.3B in charges and stating the underlying performance figure in the same breath — this is the opposite of spin. The FCF compression was pre-disclosed in prior-year shareholder letters before it happened, and the subsequent letters confirmed the outcome without softening it. The single most important positive signal: Amazon's shareholder letters (FY2025 pp.214–231) address the "over-hyped AI, are we in a bubble?" question directly, present the bear case against themselves ("reasonable people can disagree"), and then make an evidence-based case for their position. This is atypically candid shareholder communication for a large-cap company.

The one blemish: management does not issue voluntary adjusted EPS, which means that in years with large Anthropic mark-ups, the headline GAAP EPS beat is optically large and misleading for investors who do not read the non-operating income line carefully. This is a presentation choice management could make differently without any disclosure obligation; their peers (Alphabet, Meta) commonly issue adjusted metrics to avoid this distortion. The server useful-life flip also signals some earnings-smoothing at the margin. Neither rises to the level of a credibility concern, but both are costs to the candor grade.

**Verdict: candid / high-trust.** The biggest candor signal is that charges were disclosed in the opening sentence of the Q3 2025 prepared remarks, not buried — this is where management proves whether they tell the truth when results are bad.
