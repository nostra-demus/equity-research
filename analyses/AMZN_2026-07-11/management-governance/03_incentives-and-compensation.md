**Conclusion:** Amazon's incentive structure is the simplest among S&P 500 mega-caps. There is no annual bonus, no explicit performance metric of any kind, and a single vehicle — time-vested RSUs — whose value tracks the stock price unconditionally. The structure cannot reward empire-building (revenue bonuses do not exist), but it also cannot structurally penalize an executive who produces mediocre but stable results, because RSUs vest regardless of operating performance. The implicit alignment to per-share value is directionally correct; the absence of any explicit performance gate is the structural gap.

## 4. Incentive Alignment Read

Amazon runs the simplest executive compensation model in the S&P 500 mega-cap group: no annual bonus of any kind, no explicit performance metrics, and a single vehicle — time-vested RSUs — that ties executive wealth entirely to the stock price over a multi-year period. The absence of any size-based metric (revenue, EBITDA, deal count) is a structural positive: Jassy cannot enrich himself by growing revenue while destroying margins, and there is no bonus to protect by hitting an EBITDA target at the expense of long-run investment. The back-loaded 10-year vesting on the CEO's original 2021 grant — with more than 80% of the grant vesting in years six through ten — is the strongest long-term orientation mechanism among the peer group, stronger than annual equity grants with 3- or 4-year vesting.

The biggest structural limitation is that vesting is unconditional. There is no ROIC minimum, no relative TSR hurdle, and no operating income floor. An executive who presided over stable-but-mediocre results would still vest the full grant as long as they remained employed. In practice, this has not mattered during the Jassy era — operating income grew 6.5x from FY2022 to FY2025 and the stock rose 4.1x — but the structural protection is weaker than at Apple, Microsoft, or Meta, all of which use performance-conditioned RSUs. Institutional investors flagged this for four consecutive years (22–44% say-on-pay opposition from 2022 to 2025), before dissipating to 6% in 2026 as operating performance validated the model.

The secondary concern is dilution: company-wide SBC is declining ($19.5B in FY2025 vs $24.0B in FY2023) but still represents 2.7% of FY2025 revenue, and net share count rose by ~348M shares over FY2023–FY2025 (3.4% dilution) with the $6.1B buyback program sitting unused.

**Verdict: Mixed.** The structure is directionally per-share-oriented — equity only, no revenue or EBITDA bonuses, back-loaded multi-year vesting — but lacks the explicit performance conditions (ROIC, TSR, EPS floor) that would constitute genuine returns-based pay. The absence of size-based metrics is a strong positive; the absence of any performance hurdle is the gap that kept say-on-pay opposition elevated for four years.
