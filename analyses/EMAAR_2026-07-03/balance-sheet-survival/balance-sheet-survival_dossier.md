## 1A. Module Disconfirmation

**Strongest bear point:** The 2026 maturity wall is 50.1% of gross debt (AED 5,317 mn). If Dubai's property cycle reverses sharply in H2-2026 (after Sukuk 3 is repaid in September), pre-sales could stall and the operating cash engine would slow — at the same time the AED 8.9 bn declared dividend exits the balance sheet. The transition from a pre-cycle-reversal to a post-cycle-reversal world compresses the liquid buffer faster than the static balance-sheet snapshot suggests. The restricted escrow of AED 42.9 bn cannot serve as a backup; if buyers cancel at scale, that cash would need to be refunded, not deployed for corporate use.

**Strongest bull point:** The company's in-hand liquid assets of AED 28.3 bn at FY2025 (growing to AED 34.7 bn at Q1-2026) already exceed Emaar's entire gross debt stack of AED 10.1 bn — meaning the company could retire every single debt instrument with existing liquid assets and still hold AED 18-24 bn in cash. This is not leverage management; this is a structurally near-debt-free operating business that happens to carry some fixed-rate sukuk. The broad net cash position of AED 17.3 bn is a strategic asset in the Section 24 Filter 3 sense: it funds counter-cyclical action when the Dubai market turns and removes any dependence on external debt markets.

**Single killer risk specific to solvency/survival:** A sudden and severe cancellation wave across Emaar's AED 154.8 bn off-plan backlog (with Emaar India / Egypt exposures compounding via local-currency facility risk), triggering large buyer-refund obligations from restricted escrow accounts and simultaneously cutting off the pre-sale instalment inflows that fund operating cash flow. This scenario would need to be historically unprecedented in scale — the COVID-2020 shock produced only a 34% revenue drop and did not threaten solvency — but it is the one pathway to financial stress, given that the escrow is not available for corporate purposes and refund obligations would be immediate.

**Disconfirming evidence already visible in specialist outputs:** The AED 42.9 bn of "Net Cash" disclosed in the company's own FY2025 investor presentation is a gross-liquidity figure that includes restricted buyer escrow. The company's headline "net cash" of AED 61.7 bn includes AED 43 bn of buyer money that cannot be used for debt service. An investor relying on the company's own disclosure without stripping the escrow would overstate freely usable liquidity by approximately 5x on the strict basis. Specialist 01 explicitly corrects for this, and this synthesis carries the correction through all sections.

**Commentary on the next-12–24 month wall:**

The 2026 maturity is real but is already covered several times over by existing liquid assets alone, without any reliance on new market access or operating cash flow. At Dec-31-2025, Emaar held AED 27,902 mn in unrestricted cash and liquid investments against AED 5,317 mn due within 12 months — a coverage ratio of 5.2x. The Sukuk 3 repayment of USD $750 mn (~AED 2,753 mn) in September 2026 is the largest single event; at current run-rate FCF of AED 24–32 bn per annum, the group generates the equivalent of Sukuk 3's face value in operating cash every 5–7 weeks.

The international subsidiary facilities (EGP, PKR, AED working capital lines) described in the investor presentation as "rolled over on annual basis" represent a structural revolving obligation rather than a true maturity event. Their non-renewal would be the main operational risk, but these are local-currency facilities supported by local operating cash flows in Egypt, Pakistan, and India.

The rating posture is supportive. S&P upgraded Emaar to BBB+ (stable) and Moody's to Baa1 (stable) in 2025 (FY2025 Investor Presentation, p.8; confirmed by web-sourced credit commentary). At this rating, the GCC primary debt market — which issued AED ~55 bn in bond and sukuk in Q1-2026 alone (web-sourced) — is fully open to Emaar. No new issuance has been publicly announced or committed; the plan appears to be cash repayment of Sukuk 3, consistent with the scale of the liquid balance sheet.

**Verdict:** The near-term wall (2026) is **self-funded / low refi risk**. Emaar does not need market access to retire Sukuk 3 in September 2026. Its liquid assets alone (AED 27,902 mn at Dec-31-2025) exceed the entire 2026 maturity by 5.2x. Remaining post-2026 maturities are small (2027–2028: AED 947 mn combined) until the Sukuk 4 bullet of AED 1,837 mn in 2029 — which, at FY2025 normalised FCF of AED 24 bn per annum, is generated in approximately three weeks of operations.

## 5. Refinancing Read

The only true maturity event in the next 24 months is Sukuk 3 (USD $750 mn, AED ~2,753 mn), maturing September 2026. It will almost certainly be repaid from cash on hand rather than refinanced: Emaar's unrestricted cash and liquid investments of AED 27.9 bn at Dec-31-2025 (rising to AED 35.0 bn by Q1-2026) make a new issuance unnecessary, and no replacement sukuk has been publicly announced. If Emaar were to refinance rather than repay, the cost step-up is approximately 109 bps above the existing 3.72% weighted sukuk coupon (to an estimated ~4.81%, based on the 5-yr US Treasury yield of 4.14% as of Jun-29-2026 plus a GCC BBB+ spread of ~67 bps — both web-sourced and indicative) — a moderate increase that is completely immaterial given interest coverage of 45–49x EBIT/EBITDA to interest.

The single biggest refinancing risk is not the 2026 sukuk but rather a scenario where Dubai's property cycle reverses sharply after the sukuk is repaid, liquidity is drawn down to fund project delivery obligations, and Sukuk 4 ($500 mn, 2029) comes due in a closed market at the same time as a working-capital crunch from stalled pre-sales. Even that scenario is remote: the AED 154.8 bn revenue backlog (FY2025 Investor Presentation, p.6) provides 3–4 years of forward cash conversion, the revolver (AED 7.3 bn undrawn, matures 2030) provides an emergency buffer, and the group would need to lose over 90% of its liquid assets before facing any maturity gap.

Under a "market closure" scenario (no new unsecured issuance for 12 months from Jul-2026): Emaar survives comfortably. Cash and liquid investments at Q1-2026 of AED 35.0 bn exceed total gross debt of AED 10.1 bn by 3.5x. The company could repay every single instrument in the stack out of its own balance sheet without touching operating cash flow. Market closure for 12 months is irrelevant to survival.

### Seasonality / Peak Liquidity Need (Hard Check)

Emaar's revenue is **seasonally concentrated in Q4** (32%–33% of annual revenue, per `earnings/01_historical-financials.md` Section 5). Q1 is the weakest quarter (approximately 21% of annual revenue). EBITDA follows the same Q4 concentration, and CFO is therefore also back-weighted.

## 4. Survival Read

Emaar's structure does not break at any EBITDA decline short of a near-total-collapse of the business — roughly −94% from FY2025 levels. That is approximately 2.8x worse than the actual COVID trough and has no historical basis in Emaar's own EBITDA series or in UAE real estate history. The first thing to break at an extreme level is the labeled minimum interest coverage covenant (at −93.9% EBITDA), not liquidity and not a maturity event — and that labeled threshold is an assumed market-standard floor for a BBB+-rated developer, not a disclosed actual covenant.

A 30–40% EBITDA decline — a normal recession, not a tail — is survivable on its own, without a covenant waiver, an equity raise, or a distressed asset sale. At −40% EBITDA, interest is still covered 29.4x and the company holds AED 19,441 mn of committed liquidity surplus above all 12-month obligations. At −60% EBITDA, coverage is 19.6x and the liquidity surplus is identical. Even at the actual FY2021 post-COVID EBITDA of AED 7,803 mn — a 67.7% decline from today — ICR is 15.9x and committed liquidity comfortably covers every obligation.

Market closure test: assume no new unsecured refinancing for 12 months from July 2026. At Q1-2026, committed liquidity of AED 42,025 mn (in-hand + revolver) covers gross debt of AED 10,064 mn by 4.18x and covers all 12-month uses of AED 16,153 mn by 2.60x. The company could retire every instrument in the stack from its own balance sheet without any market access. Sukuk 3 (USD $750 mn, AED ~2,753 mn, Sep-2026) — the only maturity event in the next 12 months — will almost certainly be repaid from cash rather than refinanced; at Q1-2026 unrestricted cash alone of AED 12,180 mn, coverage of this single event is 4.4x. Market closure for 12 months is irrelevant to survival and does not trigger any break point.

On the §15 broad-basis net-debt figure — the canonical one per `01` — Emaar is in significant net cash of AED 17,287 mn at FY2025 and AED 24,619 mn at Q1-2026. This net cash is a strategic asset: it removes dependence on debt markets during a Dubai property downturn, funds continued project delivery without external capital, and gives the company the capacity to increase launches, absorb cost inflation, or pursue selective bolt-on deals precisely when competitors face pressure (per CLAUDE.md §24, Filter 3 and MODULE_RULES §8). Even on the strict basis (unrestricted cash only), the company reached net cash of AED 2,115 mn at Q1-2026. The net cash position is not a trivial or structural feature — it reflects AED 8.4 bn of gross debt reduction since FY2021 and four consecutive years of normalised FCF well above obligations, and it deepens as operating cash flow continues.

The single genuine downside concern is not a balance-sheet break: it is the risk that if the Dubai property cycle reverses sharply, the AED 8.9 bn declared dividend continues to be paid from a shrinking liquid buffer over multiple years while pre-sales stall. Even in that scenario, the AED 35–42 bn committed liquidity buffer would take several years of simultaneous earnings collapse and dividend maintenance to exhaust — the structure remains intact throughout any historically observed property cycle.
