Bargaining power bands:
- **Strong:** Company sets price, dictates terms, has alternatives
- **Mid:** Negotiated outcomes, no extreme leverage either way
- **Weak:** Price-taker, terms imposed, few alternatives

## 2. Input Cost Pass-Through

Tesla's ability to pass rising input costs to customers is partial and lossy, not automatic. On the automotive side, management credits "effective pricing and cost management" for holding gross margin "approximately flat" quarter-on-quarter once one-off items are stripped out [Q2 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks], but the multi-year trend argues the other way: GAAP automotive gross margin fell from 18.2% in FY2023 to 16.2% in FY2025 [03_segment-map.md, citing CIQ Financials_Annual export] even as per-unit cost stayed "relatively consistent" [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A] — the gap was closed mostly by regulatory-credit contribution and cost cuts, not by raising sticker prices. Tariff cost relief has shown up as one-off, laggy recognitions rather than a structural escalator: Tesla booked "more than $250 million from certain tariffs which we paid in prior quarters" as an energy-segment benefit in Q1 FY2026 that then "did not repeat" in Q2 [Q1 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks; Q2 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks]. There is no disclosed contractual pass-through clause (escalator or indexed pricing) for retail vehicle sales in this pool — automotive sales are one-off retail transactions, not standing supply contracts [05_customer-geography.md]. On the energy side, management is explicit that trying to pass tariff-driven battery-cell cost increases through to customers is self-defeating: "import tariffs by the US government and the provisions of the OBBBA could significantly increase battery cell expenses and impact costs for our consumers, negatively impacting consumer demand" [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A]. Not proven from available data: any numeric lag (in days or quarters) between an input cost rise and a matching price change, beyond the tariff-recognition timing above.

**Supplier / input concentration (quantify).** The filings do not quantify supplier concentration — there is no disclosed percentage of cost of revenue or purchases tied to the largest supplier or the top three suppliers, and no dollar figure attached to the single-source dependency. The 10-Q states only, in general terms, that Tesla is "dependent on our suppliers, including single source suppliers," without naming them or sizing the exposure [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Notes, Supply Risk]. The one input concentration that is specifically named and material is geographic rather than a single company: for the Energy Generation and Storage segment, "most of the battery cells are procured from China," and management flags this as the reason "tariffs in this business can have outsized impacts" [Q1 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks]. That segment was 13.5% of FY2025 revenue but 22.2% of FY2025 gross profit and growing [03_segment-map.md] — so a China-battery-cell single-country dependency sits directly inside Tesla's fastest-improving profit pool. Not proven from available data: the dollar or percentage split of Tesla's total raw-material or component purchases between in-house-produced (lithium, cathode, cells) and externally-sourced inputs.

## 3. Customer Pricing Power

Tesla has not demonstrated an ability to raise prices without losing volume in the periods covered by this pool. No explicit sticker-price increase is documented in the last 24 months; instead, the automotive average selling price moved on "sales mix and a positive impact from the weakening of the United States dollar... and an increase from higher revenue from FSD (Supervised) subscriptions" [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A] — mix, currency, and software attach, not a price hike on the core vehicle. Automotive gross margin has trended down for three straight years (18.2% FY2023 → 16.9% FY2024 → 16.2% FY2025) [03_segment-map.md] even as Q2 FY2026 revenue growth was explicitly volume-led ("an increase of approximately 25% in cash deliveries") [Business identity, §4, citing Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A] — consistent with a company competing on volume, not price, against BYD and legacy-OEM EVs. On the energy side, management states plainly that "ASPs for industrial storage is coming down amidst growing competition" [Q2 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks], and Q2 FY2026 energy gross margin fell sharply from 30.3% to 20.4% on "a decrease in average selling price per Megapack unit" [Business identity, §2, citing Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A]. The one clear pricing-power exception is software, not hardware: about 55% of North America deliveries carried a paid FSD (Supervised) subscription at the time of delivery in Q2 FY2026 [Q2 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks], but that sits on top of a base vehicle price that is itself under competitive pressure, not evidence that Tesla can move the core product's price.

## 4. Economic Control Verdict

**Mixed.** Tesla controls the middle of its value chain — it owns its factories, bypasses the independent dealer network that squeezes most automakers, and increasingly monetizes proprietary infrastructure and software (Supercharger access sold to rival OEMs, paid FSD subscriptions) on terms it sets [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A; Q2 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks]. But it is a price-taker on globally traded battery-grade commodities and disclosed single-source components upstream [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Notes, Supply Risk], and it cannot raise prices to end customers without losing volume on either the automotive side (three years of margin decline despite flat unit cost) or the energy side ("ASPs for industrial storage is coming down amidst growing competition") [03_segment-map.md; Q2 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks]. The near-pure-margin regulatory-credit revenue that has historically cushioned automotive margin is set entirely by government policy, not by negotiation, and it fell 67% year-on-year in Q2 FY2026 [Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A].

## 5. The Single Biggest Bargaining Risk

The Energy segment's reliance on China-sourced battery cells under an escalating US tariff and OBBBA regime is the relationship most likely to hurt Tesla if it worsens — it raises input cost in the segment where gross profit has grown fastest, and management itself says passing that cost to customers would cut demand rather than protect margin [Q1 FY2026 transcript, prepared remarks; Q2 FY2026 10-Q, Item 2 MD&A].
