Total uses of capital over the period ($228,679mm) exceed cumulative cash from operations ($98,175mm) by roughly $130,504mm. This is the single structural fact behind every row below: Oracle is now funding its dividend, and a large share of its capex, with borrowed and freshly-raised capital, not with cash the business is throwing off.

The dividend itself is not large relative to earnings (33.4% payout, and falling) — the problem is not the dividend's size, it is that operations no longer generate enough cash to pay it. Both FY2025 and FY2026 dividends were effectively funded from the $115.8bn of debt, preferred stock, and common-stock-plan proceeds raised over the period (Section 1), not from free cash flow. **RF-CAP-003 (dividends not covered by FCF) is triggered for FY2025–FY2026**, consistent with the flag already raised in `business-model/11_capital-allocation-governance.md` and `01_management-and-track-record.md` (finding 01-013).

Incremental ROIC of 5.3%–7.0% describes the return the last several years of net new capital have earned so far. That return is being generated while the newest and largest tranche of capital — the FY2026 capex ramp ($55.7bn, more than 2.6x FY2025's level) — has barely begun to be reflected in revenue (RPO of $638bn is contracted-but-not-yet-recognized backlog, not realized revenue). This module does not compute or compare against a cost-of-capital figure (that is the valuation module's job), but flags that the incremental-ROIC figure so far is thin relative to the scale of capital being deployed, and its trajectory over the next 2–3 reporting periods is the single most important number for testing whether this capital allocation cycle creates or destroys per-share value.

## 5. Capital-Allocation Read

Capital allocation at Oracle has been mixed, tilting value-destructive on a per-share basis over the most recent two years, after a genuinely value-creative stretch from FY2017–FY2023. The best use of capital in the period was the historical buyback program, which cut diluted shares by 34.4% from FY2017 to the FY2023 trough — real, verifiable per-share value creation — but that program is now effectively dead (FY2026 spend of $93mm is 0.5% of the FY2022 peak), and diluted share count has risen every year since, undoing part of that gain while stock-based compensation and a convertible-preferred overhang keep adding new shares. The worst use of capital is the dividend, not because of its size but because of what funds it: Oracle paid $5.8bn of dividends in FY2026 while free cash flow was −$23.7bn, meaning the payout — and a meaningful share of the AI-infrastructure capex build — is now financed by the $115.8bn of debt, preferred stock and equity-plan proceeds raised over the period, not by the business's own cash generation. The single biggest capital-allocation signal is the sequencing: the debt-funded Cerner acquisition (FY2023) consumed leverage capacity three years before management needed that same capacity for a far larger, AI-driven capex cycle, forcing FY2026's 54% one-year debt increase and the subsequent S&P downgrade to BBB-. Read together with a computed incremental ROIC of only 5.3%–7.0% on the last several years' net new capital, this is not evidence of disciplined per-share compounding — it is a business betting its capital structure on demand (the $638bn RPO backlog) converting to cash on a schedule that has not yet been tested. Verdict: **mixed**, leaning value-destructive on the most recent two years' allocation choices, against a genuinely value-creative earlier record.
