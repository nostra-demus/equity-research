## 2. Input Cost Pass-Through

Oracle's pass-through mechanism is structural and forward-looking, not retroactive, and it runs through contract design rather than list-price increases. CEO Clay Magouyrk described it directly on the Q4 FY26 call: "When we're selling stuff at a time period where we have certainty... whether it be space and power costs, energy costs, people costs, component costs... we will then do fixed-price contracts. At times that we don't know those costs because it's out too far in the future or we have too much supply chain risk... we then do not do fixed-price contracts with our customers and we have a mechanism whereby those costs end up being floating" [Q4 FY26 transcript, CEO Magouyrk]. In plain terms: on new, uncertain-cost contracts Oracle indexes price to its own input costs (a real pass-through clause); on contracts already locked at a fixed price, a subsequent rise in memory, SSD or GPU costs is absorbed by Oracle, not passed on — the analyst who raised the question noted "component costs have gone up a lot, especially memory" [Q4 FY26 transcript, John DiFucci, Guggenheim]. This lag shows up at the margin line: management flagged a roughly 5-point full-year gross-margin step-down in FY2026 and guided to a further FY2027 step-down "due to timing for the ramp-up of our data center projects into their full revenue contribution plus impacts from mix" [Q4 FY26 transcript, CFO Maxson] — i.e., new capacity is expensed as it comes online but takes time to earn its full contracted revenue, so input-cost absorption runs ahead of pass-through in the near term. On the legacy software side, support renewals reprice roughly annually as a percentage of the original license fee, but the filings disclose no explicit CPI-linked or indexed escalator clause — pricing there is set by Oracle's own list price and discounting practice, not a contractual pass-through mechanism [02_business-identity.md §2].

**Supplier / input concentration (quantify).** The 10-K discloses only qualitative concentration language — "we do rely on sole and/or single sources for certain hardware components" [FY26 10-K, Item 1, p.3952] — and does not disclose a percentage of COGS or capital spend tied to any single supplier. NVIDIA Corporation is named as a supplier in the CIQ relationship data (Semiconductors) [Oracle-Corporation-NYSE-ORCL-Suppliers.txt], and Oracle's own disclosures describe its largest AI clusters as built on "hundreds of thousands of NVIDIA GPUs" [10_external-dependency.md, citing Key Developments 2025-10-14]. Given that GPU accelerators are the single largest driver of the AI-infrastructure capex program (net capex outlay of roughly $48 billion in FY2026, guided to ~$70 billion in FY2027 [Q4 FY26 transcript, CFO Maxson]), NVIDIA is very likely Oracle's largest single hardware-related dependency by dollar value — but this is **Inference, not from filings**: no filing states a specific percentage, and downstream agents should not invent one. What the filings do state is a genuine, named risk: memory and SSD suppliers are also named as a source of recent cost inflation [Q4 FY26 transcript, John DiFucci], and Oracle "at times has to accept less favorable terms with suppliers to minimize supply constraints" [FY26 10-K, Item 1, p.5907] — a disclosed, material single-input dependency that is a genuine bargaining risk carried into §5 below.

## 3. Customer Pricing Power

Oracle currently shows real, if concentrated and unquantified, pricing power on the OCI side: global GPU utilization is 97.5%, and in the most recent quarter "35,000 GPUs from 59 separate customers were up for renewal. 49% of those customers renewed for 92% of those GPUs" with the remainder "subsequently sold to other customers in the same quarter" [Q4 FY26 transcript, CEO Magouyrk] — a supply-constrained market in which Oracle is not chasing volume with discounts. Management also introduced new monetization layers in Q4 FY26 — outcome-based pricing and token bundles — with "33 customers... repurchase[d] tokens to have access to more advanced reasoning and models" [Q4 FY26 transcript, CEO Sicilia], but this is a 33-customer early rollout, not a disclosed broad-based price increase with a measured volume reaction; treat it as **Not proven from available data** as an economy-wide pricing action. On the legacy license/support book, the opposite signal holds: software revenue fell 1% in FY2026 "as customers migrate to cloud" [Q4FY26 Earnings Press Release, p.1], which is evidence of customers voting with their feet rather than Oracle successfully raising price without losing volume. No explicit "we raised price X%, volume moved Y%" disclosure exists anywhere in this data pool for either business — that comparison is **Not proven from available data**.

## 4. Economic Control Verdict

**Mixed.** Oracle is a price-taker on the physical inputs that now drive its growth — sole/single-sourced GPU, memory and component vendors, and a global power market it does not control, where the company itself says it "accept[s] less favorable terms with suppliers to minimize supply constraints" [FY26 10-K, Item 1, p.5907]. On the customer side it currently holds real leverage in its fastest-growing business (OCI, 97.5% utilization, capacity "instantly snapped up") and a sticky, high-switching-cost legacy database franchise, but that leverage is concentrated in a small number of very large AI-infrastructure counterparties (four customers over $8 billion each in a single quarter) who have visible alternatives entering the market (competing hyperscalers, "NeoCloud, SpaceX" building their own data centers, per the Bernstein analyst's own framing on the call) [Q4 FY26 transcript, Mark Moerdler, Bernstein].

## 5. The Single Biggest Bargaining Risk

The GPU/power supply relationship sitting upstream of OCI: Oracle has already disclosed that it accepts worse terms from sole/single-source chip and component vendors to keep AI-infrastructure supply flowing [FY26 10-K, Item 1, p.5907], and if that squeeze tightens further (NVIDIA allocation shifting toward its own cloud or larger hyperscalers, continued memory/SSD cost inflation, or power-supply constraints — "constrained globally" per the 10-K's own risk factors [10_external-dependency.md]) it would compress margins on the very backlog ($638 billion of RPO) that the market is now pricing Oracle on, before Oracle's downstream pricing power over its concentrated set of AI-infrastructure customers has a chance to offset it.
