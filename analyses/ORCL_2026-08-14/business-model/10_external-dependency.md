## 3. Classification

**Mostly externally driven.**

Two years ago this would have read "Partly externally driven" — a large, diversified enterprise software company with real pricing power and only moderate FX and government-budget exposure. That is no longer the shape of the business the market is pricing. FY26 remaining performance obligations exploded 363% to $638 billion on the back of a handful of AI-infrastructure customers (four contracts over $8 billion each in a single quarter, contracted counterparties publicly named as AMD, Meta, NVIDIA, OpenAI, TikTok and xAI). Funding that backlog into delivered capacity requires Oracle to keep winning a scarce, contested physical input (data-center power) and a scarce, single-sourced-by-vendor physical input (NVIDIA GPUs), while raising roughly $40 billion a year in fresh debt and equity — on top of $129.5 billion of debt already outstanding — into whatever capital-markets conditions and credit-rating outcome prevail at the time. Management retains real levers (which sites to build, which customers to prioritize, hedging of FX and some interest-rate exposure, pricing of new contracts), which is why this is not scored as a pure pass-through — but the dominant swing factors in next twelve to thirty-six months of results (whether RPO actually converts to revenue, whether power and GPU supply keep pace, whether debt gets raised on acceptable terms) sit substantially outside management's control.

## 4. External Dependency Risk Score

**72/100** (higher = worse; inverted scale — see note at top of report)

Drivers of the score: customer/counterparty concentration inside the AI infrastructure backlog (High), physical-input scarcity in power and GPUs gating whether that backlog can be delivered (High), and a debt load that has nearly doubled in a year with no slowdown in appetite for more (High) together outweigh the genuinely well-managed and disclosed FX hedging program (Mid) and the historically diversified, if policy-exposed, government and regulatory book (Government policy High, Regulation Mid). This sits in the 61–80 "mostly externally driven, limited management levers" band rather than the 81–100 "pure pass-through" band because Oracle still chooses which data-center sites to build, which customers to prioritize capacity for, and retains a large legacy database/applications franchise (cloud and software was 87% of FY26 revenue) that is not directly hostage to the AI capex cycle in the same way OCI is.

## 5. The Single Biggest Lever

If AI-infrastructure demand from Oracle's largest named OCI customers (AMD, Meta, NVIDIA, OpenAI, TikTok, xAI) pulled back 20% from what is embedded in the $638 billion remaining-performance-obligation backlog, the damage would exceed any single FX, rate or power-cost move: Oracle would be left holding data-center leases, power-purchase commitments and ~$129.5 billion of debt sized to a demand level that no longer exists, with margins already compressed from the current build-out ramp — the classic "locked into multi-year commitments for excess data center space and related capital expenditures... without receiving corresponding revenue" scenario the company itself flags [FY26 10-K, Item 1A, p.18].
