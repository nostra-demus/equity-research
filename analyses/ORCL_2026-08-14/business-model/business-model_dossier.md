# Business Model Reality Check — ORCL (Synthesis)

## Abstract

Oracle sells databases, business software, and cloud computing capacity, and increasingly the raw GPU capacity used to train AI models. The Cloud and Software segment supplies 86.9% of FY2026 revenue and 90.7% of segment profit, with cloud infrastructure (OCI) now the whole growth story. The clearest positive is switching-cost lock-in: software-support revenue held flat at $19,804 million even as license revenue fell 9%. The clearest negative is that OCI's own economics have not proven out — return on capital fell to 8.2% against an estimated 11.2% cost of capital while debt rose 54% to $167.4 billion. No disqualifier triggered, but this reads as a technology-cycle bet, not a durable compounder.

- **Cleanest unit-economics read:** cannot be derived from audited evidence alone. The only clean, filed proxy is the declining blended Cloud and Software segment margin (64.1%→58.9% over three years); the more favorable project-level numbers (30–40% contract margin, "high-20s" steady-state ROIC) are management's own unaudited, forward-looking claims that appear nowhere in the 10-K [`04_unit-economics.md` §3].

- **Main external dependency:** the AI/industrial-capex cycle, gated by two scarce physical inputs Oracle does not control — data-center power ("constrained globally") and NVIDIA GPUs (sole/single-sourced) — combined with a debt load that rose 54% in one year and a further ~$40 billion/year borrowing plan [`10_external-dependency.md` §1, §4].
- **Most important capital allocation or governance signal:** the FY26 dividend ($5.8bn paid) is now funded by debt and preferred-stock issuance rather than free cash flow, which turned to -$24.5 billion (levered basis) as capex ($55.7bn) outran operating cash flow ($32.0bn) — this, not a fraud or related-party signal, is the dominant governance concern here [`11_capital-allocation-governance.md` §2].

- **Whether the business deserves deeper work, and what would change the answer:** Worth deeper work only with a strong, evidenced view on the AI-infrastructure demand cycle over the next 24 months — not as a default durable-compounder thesis. What would change the answer toward higher quality: audited (not management-claimed) evidence that OCI project-level returns clear their cost of capital as FY26/FY27 capacity ramps to full contractual revenue, and a capex/revenue ratio that stops rising faster than RPO conversion into recognized, cash-generative revenue. What would change the answer toward lower quality: a further credit-rating downgrade below investment grade, a pullback from any of the small number of named AI-infrastructure counterparties, or evidence that the disclosed FY26 cybersecurity incidents were more material than currently characterized.

## 5. Simple Summary

- **What it does:** Sells databases and business software that companies run on, and rents out cloud computing capacity (increasingly GPU capacity for AI) that they run their AI models on.
- **How it makes money:** A blend of five formulas — cloud infrastructure billed by usage/contract ($18.1bn, +77%), cloud application subscriptions ($15.9bn, +11%), on-premise software license (up front, $4.7bn, -9%) plus annual support renewals (~flat, $19.8bn), and hardware/services add-ons — with cloud infrastructure now the whole growth story.
- **Whether each new unit creates value:** Not provable from audited numbers. Management's own forward-looking claim (30–40% contract margin, "high-20s" steady-state ROIC) says yes; the only audited proxy — declining blended segment margin (64.1%→58.9% over three years) and companywide return on capital falling for four straight years to 8.2%, below an estimated ~11.2% cost of capital — says no, not yet.
- **Which segment matters most:** Cloud and Software (86.9% of revenue, 90.7% of profit), and within it specifically cloud infrastructure (OCI), which is the only capital-intensive, fast-growing piece.
- **Whether it has a moat, and against whom:** A narrow, eroding moat. Real switching-cost lock-in in the legacy database/ERP support annuity (flat revenue despite shrinking license sales), but no proven cost, scale, or network-effect advantage against Microsoft/Azure or Amazon/AWS in cloud infrastructure, where Oracle holds roughly 3% share against their 28% and 21%.
- **What external variables it depends on:** GPU supply (sole/single-sourced), data-center power ("constrained globally"), capital-markets access to keep raising ~$40 billion/year in debt on top of $167.4 billion already outstanding, and continued commitment from a small number of named AI-infrastructure customers.
- **Whether capital is allocated well:** Not currently. The dividend is funded by new debt and preferred stock, not free cash flow, which turned deeply negative as capex outran operating cash flow, and S&P has already cut the credit rating once.
- **Whether it deserves deeper work:** Yes, but only as a cyclical, technology-cycle bet with a real timing edge on AI-infrastructure demand — not as a default durable-compounder thesis.

# Business Identity — ORCL

## 1. What The Company Actually Does

Oracle sells the computing and software that other organizations run their operations on. It has three businesses: cloud and software (87% of FY2026 revenue), hardware (5%), and services (8%) [FY26 10-K, Item 7 Business Overview, p.40-41]. The cloud and software business is itself two things bolted together — cloud infrastructure (Oracle Cloud Infrastructure, or OCI, which rents out computing power, storage and networking, increasingly to train and run AI models) and cloud/on-premise applications (finance, HR, supply-chain and customer-management software, sold as Oracle Fusion, NetSuite, and industry-specific suites) [FY26 10-K, Item 1 Business, p.1-4]. Customers are businesses of all sizes, government agencies, and educational institutions, bought either directly through Oracle's sales force or through partner resellers [FY26 10-K, Item 1 Business, p.2]. Geographically the business is concentrated in the Americas, which contributed 88% of Oracle's constant-currency revenue growth in FY2026, with EMEA and Asia Pacific contributing 5% and 7% respectively [FY26 10-K, Item 7, p.41 area]. The problem customers pay Oracle to solve is running mission-critical databases and business applications reliably, and — increasingly — getting the raw computing capacity (GPUs and data-center space) needed to train and deploy AI models without building that infrastructure themselves [FY26 10-K, Item 1 Business, p.1; Q4 FY26 earnings call, CEO Clay Magouyrk prepared remarks].

## 2. How The Company Makes Money

Oracle runs several distinct revenue formulas under one roof:

- **Cloud infrastructure (OCI / IaaS):** `Revenue = compute & storage capacity delivered (GPUs, gigawatts) × usage or contracted rate`. FY2026 Cloud Infrastructure revenue was $18.1 billion, up 77% [Q4FY26 Earnings Press Release, p.1].
- **Cloud applications (SaaS):** `Revenue = subscribing customers × modules/seats × subscription price`, recognized ratably over one-to-five-year contracts. FY2026 Cloud Applications revenue was $15.9 billion, up 11% [Q4FY26 Earnings Press Release, p.1].
- **On-premise software license + support:** `Revenue = new licenses sold (recognized up front) + installed base × support renewal rate (~one-year contracts, priced as a % of the license fee)`. FY2026 software revenue (license + support combined) was $24.5 billion, down 1% as customers migrate to cloud [Q4FY26 Earnings Press Release, p.1; FY26 10-K, Item 1 Business, p.2].
- **Hardware:** `Revenue = units of Engineered Systems/servers/storage sold × price, plus hardware support priced as a % of the hardware fee`. FY2026 hardware revenue was $3.1 billion, up 5% [Q4FY26 Earnings Press Release, p.1].
- **Services:** `Revenue = consulting/customer-success hours or fixed-fee engagements billed`, generally lower-margin than the other businesses. FY2026 services revenue was $5.7 billion, up 10% [Q4FY26 Earnings Press Release, p.1].

Total FY2026 revenue was $67.4 billion, up 17% [Q4FY26 Earnings Press Release, p.1]. Volume is now driven overwhelmingly by AI infrastructure demand: Oracle's Remaining Performance Obligations (RPO — contracted, not-yet-recognized revenue, a forward order book) jumped from $138 billion to $638 billion year over year, up 363%, mostly from large AI infrastructure contracts where customers prepaid or supplied their own GPUs [FY26 10-K, Note 1 / p.22532 area; Q4FY26 Earnings Press Release, p.2]. Price is set by long-term contracted rates on cloud infrastructure and by list pricing/discounting on software; margin is driven by the mix shift toward lower-gross-margin, capital-intensive infrastructure revenue (management flagged a ~5-point full-year gross-margin step-down in FY2026 from data-center ramp-up) [Q4 FY26 earnings call, CFO Hilary Maxson prepared remarks] and by the multi-year lag between building data-center capacity and that capacity earning its full contracted revenue.

## 3. Business Type Classification

Enterprise software and database incumbent pivoting into a capital-intensive AI cloud-infrastructure (IaaS) supplier, funded by a record forward order book.

## 4. What Drives Variance

The single biggest driver of revenue and margin swings right now is the pace and mix of AI infrastructure (OCI) build-out: cloud infrastructure revenue grew 77% for the year and 93% in Q4 alone, while gross margin stepped down about 5 points because new data-center capacity is expensed as it comes online but only earns its full contracted revenue over time [Q4FY26 Earnings Press Release, p.1; Q4 FY26 earnings call, CFO prepared remarks]. Volume is set less by organic customer growth than by the timing of a small number of very large AI infrastructure contracts (management cited four customers contracting for more than $8 billion each in a single quarter) [Q4 FY26 earnings call, CEO Clay Magouyrk prepared remarks], which makes quarter-to-quarter results lumpy. Price/margin in the legacy software business is comparatively stable (support renewal rates, software license pricing), so mix — how fast infrastructure grows relative to the higher-margin software base — is the key swing factor for consolidated margins, alongside foreign-currency movement (constant-currency growth ran about 1 point below reported-currency growth in FY2026) [Q4FY26 Earnings Press Release, p.1 area].

## 2. Dominant Segment

Cloud and Software dominates on both counts: 86.9% of FY26 revenue and 90.7% of FY26 total segment margin [FY26 10-K, Note 13, p.100]. That clears the >85%-from-one-segment bar in both directions, so Oracle is, in substance, a single reportable-segment company — Hardware and Services are non-core, low-growth-to-shrinking adjuncts. Within Cloud and Software itself, the profit pool is shifting fast: cloud infrastructure (OCI) grew 77% in FY26 to $18.1B and is now nearly as large as software support ($19.8B, +1%), while software license revenue is shrinking (-9%) [FY26 10-K, Item 7 MD&A, "Revenues by Offerings," p.45]. The value driver going forward is not "cloud and software" as a blended label — it is specifically cloud infrastructure/OCI, which is also the only piece of the business carrying meaningful capital intensity ($48B net capex in FY26, ~$70B guided for FY27) [Q4 FY26 investor deck (Jun-10-2026), slide 15].

## 3. Segment Disclosure Quality

The three segments — Cloud and Software, Hardware, Services — have been reported on a consistent basis for at least FY2017–FY2026 per the ten-year CIQ segment history, with the only change being a name swap (Cloud and License → Cloud and Software) that both the FY26 10-K and the Mar-2026 10-Q describe as "formerly referred to as" — i.e., no dollar restatement, just relabeling [FY26 10-K, Note 13, p.99; FY26 10-Q (filed Mar-11-2026), p. (MD&A segment discussion); CIQ Financials_Annual.xls, Segments tab, FY2017–FY2026]. Revenue is fully allocated across the three segments with no "Other"/Corporate revenue bucket, so there is no revenue-side disclosure gap. Profit disclosure is a different story: segment "Margin" is explicitly defined to include only "the direct controllable costs of each line of business" and specifically excludes research and development, general and administrative expenses, stock-based compensation, amortization of intangible assets, restructuring, interest expense and other non-operating items [FY26 10-K, Note 13, p.100–101, footnote 1]. In FY26 those unallocated items totaled at least $13.9B (R&D $10,272M + G&A $1,618M + segment-level stock-based comp $1,618M + other allocations $395M), equal to 36.6% of the $38,018M total segment margin, before further deductions for amortization of intangibles and restructuring bring the total down to $24,115M of consolidated operating profit before tax [FY26 10-K, Note 13, p.101 (reconciliation table); CIQ Financials_Annual.xls, Segments tab, FY2026]. That means the segment margin rates in the table above (e.g., Hardware's 65.4%) are cost-allocation constructs, not GAAP operating margins, and cannot be compared directly to a peer's reported segment operating margin without the same adjustment. Oracle also does not disclose segment-level assets or capital expenditure ("We do not track our assets for each business" [FY26 10-K, Note 13, p.100]), which is a real gap given how capital-intensive the cloud-infrastructure buildout inside Cloud and Software has become — downstream capital-allocation and unit-economics work will need to rely on company-wide capex and RPO disclosures rather than a segment-level split.

# Unit Economics — ORCL

## 1. Natural Unit

The natural economic unit for Oracle's dominant segment, Cloud and Software (86.9% of FY26 revenue, 90.7% of FY26 segment profit [FY26 10-K, Note 13, p.100]), is a contracted Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (OCI) data-center capacity project — the segment's growth engine and its only capital-intensive piece, sized in gigawatts (GW) of power/compute capacity and funded per project as gross capital expenditure. This follows directly from the segment-map finding that within Cloud and Software the profit pool is shifting toward cloud infrastructure (OCI grew 77% in FY26 to $18.1B, nearly as large as the $19.8B software-support book, while software license shrank 9%) [FY26 10-K, Item 7 MD&A, "Revenues by Offerings," p.45], and that OCI is the only piece of the business carrying meaningful capital intensity.

The Cloud and Software segment also contains two capital-light, secondary unit types that this table does not cover: (1) a software-support renewal contract (the legacy, high-margin, low-capex annuity book, $19,804M revenue FY26, +1%) and (2) a SaaS/cloud-application subscription customer (Fusion, NetSuite, Industry apps; $15,888M revenue FY26, +11%) [FY26 10-K, Item 7 MD&A, "Revenues by Offerings," p.45]. Oracle does not disclose a customer count, average revenue per customer, or churn rate for either, so no per-unit table can be built for them from filings.

## 3. Value Creation Read

**Unclear from audited evidence — creates value only if management's own unproven, forward-looking framework holds.** Management's project-level metrics (a 30–40% contract margin profile, and a "high-20s" steady-state ROIC — after-tax operating margin plus depreciation, over gross project capex) imply that each new OCI capacity unit creates real value once it ramps to full contractual revenue [Q4 FY26 transcript, CFO Q&A]. But this is a self-defined, unaudited, forward-looking claim about a "steady state" the FY26 capacity additions have not yet reached — it appears nowhere in the 10-K. The only audited proxy available today, the blended Cloud and Software segment margin, is moving the other way: 64.1% (FY24) → 62.8% (FY25) → 58.9% (FY26) [FY26 10-K, Note 13, p.100], consistent with a business absorbing capex-heavy, not-yet-fully-utilized new capacity ahead of revenue recognition, exactly the risk Oracle itself flags ("multi-year commitments for excess data center space...without receiving corresponding revenue") [FY26 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors, p.5]. This is a peak-buildout year, not a steady-state one: do not read the "high-20s" ROIC or the 30–40% margin profile as a proven structural constant — they are management's target for a state the business has not yet demonstrated in audited results. The single most valuable missing disclosure is a segment-level (or OCI-specific) capital-expenditure and asset base — Oracle states plainly that "we do not track our assets for each business" [FY26 10-K, Note 13, p.100] — without which neither the market nor this analysis can independently verify management's ROIC claim against actual project-level cash returns.

## 4. Sensitivity

The input most likely to change this read if it moved 20% is the cost to build the unit. GAAP capital expenditure already moved far more than 20% in a single year — from $21.2B (FY25) to $55.7B (FY26), +163% — and management guides a further ~$70B (net cash outlay basis) for FY27 [FY26 10-K, MD&A "Capital Expenditures," p.22; Q4 FY26 deck slide 15]; this is the most historically volatile input in the record by a wide margin, and a 20% overrun on the FY27 guide (roughly $14B) would materially delay the point at which management says infrastructure margin will "improve rapidly," which it has explicitly tied to reaching "full contractual revenue levels" at each data center [Q4 FY26 transcript]. The second most sensitive input is the effective useful life of the underlying assets (currently six years for servers and networking equipment) [FY26 10-K, Note 8, p.46]: Oracle's own risk factors note that data-center lease terms "typically do not align with the duration and pricing of customer contracts" [FY26 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors, p.5], so any shortening of the effective asset life — whether from technology turnover or lease/contract mismatch — would raise depreciation and directly compress the steady-state ROIC management cites, without requiring any change in revenue or price per unit.

## 2. Input Cost Pass-Through

Oracle's pass-through mechanism is structural and forward-looking, not retroactive, and it runs through contract design rather than list-price increases. CEO Clay Magouyrk described it directly on the Q4 FY26 call: "When we're selling stuff at a time period where we have certainty... whether it be space and power costs, energy costs, people costs, component costs... we will then do fixed-price contracts. At times that we don't know those costs because it's out too far in the future or we have too much supply chain risk... we then do not do fixed-price contracts with our customers and we have a mechanism whereby those costs end up being floating" [Q4 FY26 transcript, CEO Magouyrk]. In plain terms: on new, uncertain-cost contracts Oracle indexes price to its own input costs (a real pass-through clause); on contracts already locked at a fixed price, a subsequent rise in memory, SSD or GPU costs is absorbed by Oracle, not passed on — the analyst who raised the question noted "component costs have gone up a lot, especially memory" [Q4 FY26 transcript, John DiFucci, Guggenheim]. This lag shows up at the margin line: management flagged a roughly 5-point full-year gross-margin step-down in FY2026 and guided to a further FY2027 step-down "due to timing for the ramp-up of our data center projects into their full revenue contribution plus impacts from mix" [Q4 FY26 transcript, CFO Maxson] — i.e., new capacity is expensed as it comes online but takes time to earn its full contracted revenue, so input-cost absorption runs ahead of pass-through in the near term. On the legacy software side, support renewals reprice roughly annually as a percentage of the original license fee, but the filings disclose no explicit CPI-linked or indexed escalator clause — pricing there is set by Oracle's own list price and discounting practice, not a contractual pass-through mechanism [02_business-identity.md §2].

**Supplier / input concentration (quantify).** The 10-K discloses only qualitative concentration language — "we do rely on sole and/or single sources for certain hardware components" [FY26 10-K, Item 1, p.3952] — and does not disclose a percentage of COGS or capital spend tied to any single supplier. NVIDIA Corporation is named as a supplier in the CIQ relationship data (Semiconductors) [Oracle-Corporation-NYSE-ORCL-Suppliers.txt], and Oracle's own disclosures describe its largest AI clusters as built on "hundreds of thousands of NVIDIA GPUs" [10_external-dependency.md, citing Key Developments 2025-10-14]. Given that GPU accelerators are the single largest driver of the AI-infrastructure capex program (net capex outlay of roughly $48 billion in FY2026, guided to ~$70 billion in FY2027 [Q4 FY26 transcript, CFO Maxson]), NVIDIA is very likely Oracle's largest single hardware-related dependency by dollar value — but this is **Inference, not from filings**: no filing states a specific percentage, and downstream agents should not invent one. What the filings do state is a genuine, named risk: memory and SSD suppliers are also named as a source of recent cost inflation [Q4 FY26 transcript, John DiFucci], and Oracle "at times has to accept less favorable terms with suppliers to minimize supply constraints" [FY26 10-K, Item 1, p.5907] — a disclosed, material single-input dependency that is a genuine bargaining risk carried into §5 below.

## 3. Customer Pricing Power

Oracle currently shows real, if concentrated and unquantified, pricing power on the OCI side: global GPU utilization is 97.5%, and in the most recent quarter "35,000 GPUs from 59 separate customers were up for renewal. 49% of those customers renewed for 92% of those GPUs" with the remainder "subsequently sold to other customers in the same quarter" [Q4 FY26 transcript, CEO Magouyrk] — a supply-constrained market in which Oracle is not chasing volume with discounts. Management also introduced new monetization layers in Q4 FY26 — outcome-based pricing and token bundles — with "33 customers... repurchase[d] tokens to have access to more advanced reasoning and models" [Q4 FY26 transcript, CEO Sicilia], but this is a 33-customer early rollout, not a disclosed broad-based price increase with a measured volume reaction; treat it as **Not proven from available data** as an economy-wide pricing action. On the legacy license/support book, the opposite signal holds: software revenue fell 1% in FY2026 "as customers migrate to cloud" [Q4FY26 Earnings Press Release, p.1], which is evidence of customers voting with their feet rather than Oracle successfully raising price without losing volume. No explicit "we raised price X%, volume moved Y%" disclosure exists anywhere in this data pool for either business — that comparison is **Not proven from available data**.

## 4. Economic Control Verdict

**Mixed.** Oracle is a price-taker on the physical inputs that now drive its growth — sole/single-sourced GPU, memory and component vendors, and a global power market it does not control, where the company itself says it "accept[s] less favorable terms with suppliers to minimize supply constraints" [FY26 10-K, Item 1, p.5907]. On the customer side it currently holds real leverage in its fastest-growing business (OCI, 97.5% utilization, capacity "instantly snapped up") and a sticky, high-switching-cost legacy database franchise, but that leverage is concentrated in a small number of very large AI-infrastructure counterparties (four customers over $8 billion each in a single quarter) who have visible alternatives entering the market (competing hyperscalers, "NeoCloud, SpaceX" building their own data centers, per the Bernstein analyst's own framing on the call) [Q4 FY26 transcript, Mark Moerdler, Bernstein].

## 5. The Single Biggest Bargaining Risk

The GPU/power supply relationship sitting upstream of OCI: Oracle has already disclosed that it accepts worse terms from sole/single-source chip and component vendors to keep AI-infrastructure supply flowing [FY26 10-K, Item 1, p.5907], and if that squeeze tightens further (NVIDIA allocation shifting toward its own cloud or larger hyperscalers, continued memory/SSD cost inflation, or power-supply constraints — "constrained globally" per the 10-K's own risk factors [10_external-dependency.md]) it would compress margins on the very backlog ($638 billion of RPO) that the market is now pricing Oracle on, before Oracle's downstream pricing power over its concentrated set of AI-infrastructure customers has a chance to offset it.

## 3. Competitive Position

Oracle's position varies sharply by sub-market. In cloud infrastructure, Oracle holds roughly 3% of global cloud infrastructure spending versus AWS's 28%, Azure's 21%, and Google Cloud's 14% [Web: Synergy Research Group, cloud market share commentary, 2026 (unverified, dated)] — a small base, but Oracle's reported infrastructure growth (77% FY2026, 93% in Q4 alone) and its 363% RPO surge to $638 billion outpace AWS's 20-37% and sit closer to Google Cloud's fastest-in-class 63% [Q4FY26 Earnings Press Release, p.1-2; Web: Synergy Research Group, 2026 (unverified, dated)]. This is best read as **gaining share off a small base**, not holding or losing — but the RPO figure itself is a proxy for future, not realized, revenue, and $75 billion of the RPO increase is prepaid or customer-supplied-hardware contracts, a different risk profile from steady-state infrastructure revenue [FY26 10-K, Note 1 area; business-identity upstream, Section 3a]. In cloud applications, Oracle's 11% SaaS growth trails SAP's 22-27% cloud/backlog growth, which reads as **holding at best, more likely losing relative share** in the applications sub-market, though no head-count or seat-share data is disclosed by either company to confirm this directly. No official market-share disclosure exists from Oracle itself in this pool; the reads above rely on the third-party Synergy Research proxy and on relative growth-rate comparisons, both labelled and dated.

## 4. Competitive Shape

Cloud infrastructure (OCI's market) is an **oligopoly**: the "Big Three" (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud) together hold 63% of a $129 billion-per-quarter global market, with Oracle and a set of smaller "neoclouds" splitting a low-single-digit-percent remainder [Web: Synergy Research Group, cloud market share commentary, 2026 (unverified, dated)]. Cloud/on-premise enterprise applications (Oracle Fusion, NetSuite vs. SAP, Salesforce, Workday, Microsoft Dynamics) is more **fragmented at the margin but consolidated at the top** — a handful of large vendors (SAP, Oracle, Microsoft, Salesforce, Workday) dominate large-enterprise ERP and CRM, each named directly as a competitor in Oracle's own risk factors, with lower barriers to entry in narrower point-solution niches, per Oracle's own description: "due to the low barriers to entry in many of our market segments, new technologies and new and growing competitors frequently emerge" [FY26 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors, p.~4].

No company-disclosed cost of capital exists (checked FY26 10-K, transcripts, investor deck — no hurdle rate or WACC stated). The CAPM estimate above is clearly labeled inference. Because the gap is close (a few tens to a couple hundred basis points, well inside the uncertainty band of a CAPM estimate whose beta, ERP and debt-cost inputs are all approximations), the honest read is that Oracle's return on capital sits **at, or modestly below, its estimated cost of capital** — not comfortably above it.

**Through-cycle read.** CIQ's own "Return on Capital %" series has declined every year for four straight years: FY22 12.35% → FY23 10.54% → FY24 10.07% → FY25 9.62% → FY26 8.22% [CIQ Financials_Annual.xls, Ratios tab]. The 5-year average is **~10.2%** — itself close to or below the ~11.2% WACC estimate, so this is not a case of a single depressed peak year distorting the read; the trend has been declining for four years running, and FY26 (the year with by far the largest capital deployment — capex 82.6% of revenue [`07_business-quality.md` §1]) is the low point of that trend, not an outlier peak. Per `07_business-quality.md` §4, this decline coincides with, and is caused by, the OCI capital build-out outrunning the earnings it has not yet produced.

## 4. Where The Company Sits

1. **Relative to peers:** Oracle sits at the **bottom of named peers** on both margin and computed return on capital. LTM EBIT margin (33.2%) trails Microsoft (46.8%) and is roughly in line with SAP (28.8%, Oracle ahead) [`08_competitive-map.md` §2]; on return on capital, Oracle's own-computed ~8.5–10.5% trails Microsoft's third-party-estimated ~21% and SAP's ~12–14%, and is roughly comparable to or below Amazon's consolidated ~11–12% (noting Amazon's AWS-segment margin of 35.4–39.4% is itself well above Oracle's consolidated margin) [§3 above].
2. **Absolute (the economic moat test):** the company earns a return on capital **at or modestly below** its estimated cost of capital (~8.5–10.5% vs ~11.2% WACC, Inference, not from filings) — the decisive test, and it is not comfortably cleared.

## 5. Moat Verdict

**Narrow moat**, capped there by the hard rule in §5 of this report's instructions: Oracle's own-computed and CIQ-cross-checked return on capital (~8.5–10.5%, through-cycle average ~10.2%) sits at or modestly below its estimated ~11.2% cost of capital, so a "Strong moat" verdict is foreclosed regardless of the genuine peer-relative weakness that already argues against it. This is close to the **"moat in structure, not economics"** case: **switching costs** are real and evidenced (support revenue held flat at $19,804M even as license revenue fell 9%, the signature of customers locked into a legacy database/ERP annuity they are not expanding but cannot leave [FY26 10-K, Item 7 MD&A, p.45]), but that structural advantage sits inside a consolidated business whose capital-weighted return does not clear its cost of capital, because the dominant, capital-hungry part of the business (OCI) is being built at a pace and cost that has pulled the whole company's return on capital down for four straight years. The durability test over the next five years is whether the ~$638 billion RPO order book converts to recognized, cash-generative revenue fast enough for the return on capital to durably clear its cost of capital — or whether the legacy switching-cost annuity keeps shrinking as a share of the business while the low-return infrastructure buildout keeps growing as a share of it. Because the industry rate-of-change/disruption row in `07_business-quality.md` scored 33 (≤40, Filter 5), this durability test must be discounted further: a moat judgment made mid-AI-infrastructure-cycle is inherently harder to underwrite than the same judgment in a stable, slow-moving industry (CLAUDE.md §24, Filter 5).

**Moat trajectory: eroding.** Return on capital vs. cost of capital has moved the wrong way for four consecutive years (CIQ Return on Capital % 12.35% FY22 → 8.22% FY26, a period during which the estimated ~11.2% WACC did not move nearly as much) [CIQ Financials_Annual.xls, Ratios tab; §3 above]. Market-share evidence points the same direction in the segment that increasingly defines the company: Oracle holds ~3% of cloud infrastructure spend against a "Big Three" holding 63% [`08_competitive-map.md` §3-4], and in applications, SAP's cloud/backlog growth (27%) is outpacing Oracle's cloud-applications growth (11%) [`08_competitive-map.md` §2-3]. Pricing power is explicitly contested by the 10-K's own language on competitor discounting [FY26 10-K, Item 1A Risk Factors, p.~5994]. The one countervailing, evidenced fact — the support-renewal annuity holding flat despite a shrinking license base — is a sign the legacy switching-cost moat itself is stable, not eroding on its own; it is the consolidated economics that are eroding because the legacy annuity is a shrinking share of a business now dominated by a low-return capital build-out. This eroding trajectory is the early-warning signal that should feed the permanent-impairment / declining-perpetuity checks in the valuation module's DCF (CLAUDE.md §24, Filter 5).
