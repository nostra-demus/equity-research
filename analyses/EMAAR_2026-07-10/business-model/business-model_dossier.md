## Abstract

Emaar Properties is a Dubai-based property developer that master-plans land into branded communities and sells homes off-plan, alongside a smaller recurring malls-and-hotels business. It makes most of its money from build-to-sell real estate — 80% of FY2025 revenue (AED 39.6bn of AED 49.6bn [FY2025 Annual Report (IFRS), Note 3, p.187–188]). Its strongest edge is a capital-light model funded by buyer instalments, backed by net cash and a ~AED 163bn contracted backlog that gives about four years of revenue visibility. Its biggest weakness is single-city concentration: 93% of revenue rides one Dubai property cycle, now at a peak, with through-cycle return on capital (~7.5–9.5%) sitting at or below an estimated ~11–12% cost of capital. Verdict: a cyclical business worth deeper work only with a strong timing edge; no disqualifier triggered.

## 4. Note To The Final Synthesizer

- **Strongest business-model positive:** the funding model is the real edge, not just the brand — buyers pay in instalments into regulated escrow (~AED 44bn held) before they receive the home, so the customer finances Emaar's working capital; combined with a large net-cash position (Total Debt/EBITDA 0.40x) and a ~AED 163bn contracted backlog, this is a business that can keep building and survive a downturn without external finance. It means the near-term downside is about *returns*, not *solvency*.
- **Strongest business-model negative:** the wide peer-margin lead does not reliably convert into economic value creation through the cycle — after the new 15% UAE minimum tax, through-cycle return on capital is roughly level with, or below, the estimated cost of capital, so most of today's headline return reflects the Dubai cycle being near its top, not a durable spread. Treat current margins and ROIC as peak, not run-rate.
- **Most important segment:** Real Estate (off-plan build-to-sell) — ~80% of revenue and ~75% of segment profit, and the swing factor for the whole group. The ~69%-margin Leasing/Retail annuity (~15.5% of revenue) is what carries the quality and cushions the downside; it is small but it is the part a buyer should value most highly.
- **Cleanest unit-economics read:** derivable and positive — each ~AED 3.9m home earns ~45% contribution margin on minimal own capital (buyers pre-fund the build), clearing the value-creation bar even at the ~32% trough gross margin seen in 2020–21. The one missing input is a clean annual homes-sold count and average selling price; economics still compute from pipeline value ÷ unit count, so this is not a data-gap case, just a precision limit.
- **Where it sits vs. named peers on margin / ROIC:** at the top on margin and scale — ~2x #2 DAMAC by sales, and ~18 EBIT-margin points above its only audited listed peer, Aldar (55% gross / ~45% EBIT vs. 34.9% / 27.2%). But DAMAC and Sobha are private (margins undisclosed), so the peer-superiority claim rests mainly on the Aldar anchor; being top-of-peer-set on margin does not, by itself, lift the *absolute* through-cycle return above its cost of capital.
- **Main external dependency:** one variable dominates — Dubai residential property demand (price × volume × off-plan absorption), driven by foreign-capital inflows, government visa/population policy and the property cycle, none of which management sets. Own-balance-sheet interest-rate and FX risks are immaterial (a ±100bp rate move affects profit before tax by only ~0.1%; the dirham is pegged to the US dollar) — this is an operating-demand bet, not a financial-market bet [refreshed `10_external-dependency.md` §1–§2].
- **Most important capital allocation or governance signal:** the balance-sheet and distribution record is genuinely conservative (net cash, falling debt, a dividend covered roughly 2x by earnings, flat share count, a clean unqualified audit), but minority alignment is the weak spot — the Government of Dubai controls the company (block just moved from ICD to Emirates Power Investment/Dubai Holding, group stake 29.73%), the founder-MD runs a competing developer (Eagle Hills), and the IAS 24 election leaves most government-ecosystem related-party dealings unquantified. Today the channel is not visibly extractive; it is the signal that would change the classification if it deteriorated.

- **Which rejector filters (§24) tripped, and the cap each applied:** None. Filter 1 (crooks/integrity) — no proven fraud, no cap or lock. Filter 4 (serial acquirers) — acquisition-pattern severity 42 (< 70), no cap applied to capital allocation & governance or overall usefulness. Secondary: cap rate / WALE / FFO for the recurring segment, and the absent CIQ ownership/insider export.
- **Whether the business deserves deeper work, and what would change the answer:** Yes, but only conditionally — this is a clear, well-disclosed, financially resilient franchise, not a low-quality avoid; it is, however, a cyclical single-city developer at a cycle peak with a narrow, eroding economic moat, so deeper work pays off only with a specific view on the Dubai property cycle's timing. ## 5. Simple Summary

- **What it does:** Builds and sells homes off-plan in Dubai master-planned communities (Downtown Dubai, Dubai Hills, Emaar Beachfront), and separately owns The Dubai Mall, ~41 hotels and the Burj Khalifa attractions.
- **How it makes money:** ~80% from selling homes before they are built (buyers pay in instalments during construction); ~20% from mall rent and hotels.
- **Does each new unit create value:** Yes — each ~AED 3.9m home earns ~45% contribution margin and is largely paid for by the buyer during the build, so little of Emaar's own capital is at risk — but today's returns are read at a cycle peak, not a run-rate.
- **Which segment matters most:** Real Estate (build-to-sell) — ~80% of revenue and ~75% of profit; the smaller ~69%-margin malls annuity carries the quality.
- **Does it have a moat, and against whom:** A narrow, eroding moat — brand + irreplaceable Downtown-Dubai location + cheap legacy land let it out-earn listed peer Aldar by ~18 margin points, but the economic edge clears the estimated cost of capital only at the cycle peak.
- **What external variables it depends on:** One above all — Dubai residential property demand (prices, volumes, foreign-buyer inflows), plus government visa and tax policy. Interest-rate and FX exposure on its own books is immaterial (USD peg, large net-cash position).
- **Is capital allocated well:** On the balance sheet, yes (net cash, falling debt, a covered dividend); on alignment, not fully — the government controls the company, the founder-MD runs a competing developer, and most government-related-party deals are left unquantified.
- **Does it deserve deeper work:** Only with a strong Dubai-cycle timing edge — it is a cyclical bet at a peak, not a durable compounder, and roughly a fifth of consolidated profit belongs to minority shareholders, not Emaar's own owners.

## business-model / 02_business-identity.md

# Business Identity — EMAR

*Emaar Properties PJSC (DFM: EMAAR) · reports under IFRS in UAE dirham (AED) · fiscal year ends 31 December · the dirham is pegged to the US dollar (the FY2025 press-release conversions imply ~3.67 AED/USD).*

## 1. What The Company Actually Does

Emaar is a Dubai-based property developer. Its main business is holding large tracts of land in and around Dubai, master-planning them into branded communities (Downtown Dubai, Dubai Hills Estate, Emaar Beachfront, Dubai Creek Harbour, The Valley), and selling apartments, villas, commercial units and land plots — mostly "off-plan," meaning sold before or during construction and paid for in instalments [FY2025 Annual Report (IFRS), Notes — segment note; FY2025 Annual Report, Corporate Governance Report — Principal Activities]. Buyers are a mix of local and international individual investors and end-users, and about 93% of revenue is earned inside the UAE, with the rest from Egypt, India and other markets [CIQ Financials → Segments (Geographic), 12M Dec-31-2025]. Alongside the build-and-sell business, Emaar owns and runs a recurring-income portfolio: shopping malls (The Dubai Mall) with about 1.4 million sq m of leasable retail and commercial space, 41 hotels and resorts with roughly 10,000 keys (owned and managed), and leisure and entertainment attractions (the Burj Khalifa observation decks, Dubai Fountain, Dubai Aquarium) [Q4 2025 press release, 12 Feb 2026]. So the customer is really paying for one of two things: a home or plot in a master-planned community (the large majority of revenue), or access as a shopper, tenant or tourist to Emaar's destinations (the smaller, recurring stream). The problem the developer side solves is supplying finished, branded, ready-to-live-in communities in a fast-growing city where much demand is off-plan and forward-funded by buyers. Emaar also holds a controlling stake in separately-listed Emaar Development PJSC (DFM: EMAARDEV), the UAE build-to-sell arm, so a slice of group profit is owned by outside (minority) shareholders of that subsidiary [Q4 2025 earnings call summary — non-controlling interest AED 1,532m in 4Q25].

## 2. How The Company Makes Money

Three revenue lines, three different formulas:

- **Property development (build-to-sell) — the dominant line, AED 39.6bn revenue FY2025.** `Revenue ≈ units sold off-plan × price × share of construction completed in the period`. Emaar recognises property revenue either at a point in time or over time; where over time, it books revenue by percentage of completion (costs incurred to date ÷ total estimated costs) [FY2025 Annual Report, Auditor's Report — Key Audit Matter, revenue recognition; CIQ Financials → Segments, 12M Dec-31-2025, Real Estate AED 39,550m]. Cash arrives earlier than revenue: buyers pay instalments during construction, so a sale today turns into recognised revenue over roughly the next four years. Contracted pre-sales ("property sales") — a leading indicator, not yet IFRS revenue — were AED 80.4bn in FY2025 and AED 22.4bn in Q1 2026, and the sold-but-not-yet-recognised balance ("revenue backlog") was AED 163.4bn at 31 March 2026 [Q4 2025 & Q1 2026 press releases].
- **Leasing / retail (malls and commercial) — AED 7.7bn revenue FY2025.** `Revenue ≈ leasable area × occupancy × rent per sq m + turnover rent + service charges` [CIQ Financials → Segments, 12M Dec-31-2025, Leasing AED 7,681m]. Mall occupancy was about 98% at 31 March 2026 [Q1 2026 press release].
- **Hospitality, leisure & entertainment — AED 2.3bn revenue FY2025.** `Revenue ≈ (rooms × occupancy × room rate) + attraction tickets + food & beverage + hotel-management fees` [CIQ Financials → Segments, 12M Dec-31-2025, Hospitality AED 2,326m]. UAE hotel occupancy was about 82% in FY2025 and 69% in Q1 2026 [Q4 2025 & Q1 2026 press releases].

Volume in the development engine is driven by new project launches and how fast buyers absorb them (management cited 90–100% absorption on FY2025 launches) and by the pace of construction; price is driven by Dubai residential prices and the community/brand premium; margin is driven by the gap between historically low land cost and current selling prices, with housing cost of sales at about 45% of sales (roughly a 55% gross margin) in FY2025 [CIQ Financials → Industry-Specific, 12M Dec-31-2025; Q4 2025 earnings call summary]. The leasing and hospitality lines turn into much steadier annual cash because they depend on occupancy and footfall rather than one-off sales.

## 3. Business Type Classification

Master-planned residential real-estate developer (build-to-sell, off-plan) concentrated in Dubai, wrapped around a smaller recurring mall-and-hospitality annuity — economics track the Dubai/UAE property cycle.

## 4. What Drives Variance

Reported revenue moves mostly with the timing of construction progress on already-sold units, not with current-quarter demand: a big bookings quarter shows up as revenue over the following ~4 years, so revenue can rise while new sales cool, or the reverse [FY2025 Annual Report, Auditor's Report — percentage of completion; Q1 2026 press release]. New bookings (property sales) are the real leading indicator, and they swing with Dubai residential demand, launch timing, off-plan buyer appetite and population inflows (Dubai recorded over 270,000 transactions worth AED 892bn and passed 4m population in 2025) [Q4 2025 earnings call summary]. Margin swings with mix (development vs recurring, and villa vs apartment) and with the spread between historically cheap land and today's selling prices — 4Q25 gross margin slipped about 250 bps on mix even as absolute EBITDA rose 34% [Q4 2025 earnings call summary]. FX is a minor driver for the ~93% UAE business because the dirham is pegged to the US dollar; the small international book (Egypt, India) carries real currency risk.

## 3. Value Creation Read

**Creates value — but at peak-cycle returns.** Each home carries a contribution margin of ~45% on a ~AED 3.9m unit and is largely paid for by the buyer during construction (advances of AED 40.7bn, ~AED 44bn in escrow), so the cash Emaar itself risks per unit is small and is recovered at or before handover. A one-time sale earning ~45% on minimal tied-up capital clears the value-creation bar comfortably — contribution far exceeds the ~6%-of-value cost to win and the modest own-capital cost to build. The math can be done, so this is not a data-gap case.

Ring-fence the read: **2025 was the top of the Dubai cycle**, not a normal year — the emirate saw a record ~270,000 transactions worth AED 892bn, and Emaar's own group return on capital (ROIC — profit earned on each AED of capital) rose to 12.5% in FY2025 (13.7% LTM) from 4.7% in FY2021 [CIQ Financials→Ratios / `ciq_facts.json`]. The 49% residential gross margin is elevated but **below the ~59% 2023 peak**, and the through-cycle range is wide: residential gross margin was ~32% in the 2020–21 trough, ~40% in 2022, ~59% in 2023, ~50% in 2024, ~49% in 2025 [CIQ Financials→Industry Specific, annual FY2020–FY2025]. Even at the ~32% trough margin, with the build pre-funded by buyers, the unit still creates value — just at a much lower return on capital. So: strongly value-creating at peak, still positive at trough. The single most valuable missing disclosure is a **clean annual figure for homes sold and their average contracted price (ASP) each year** — the company reports value aggregates (property sales, backlog) and cumulative unit counts (189,200+ sold since 2002), but not a per-year price per home, so the ASP above has to be inferred from pipeline value ÷ pipeline unit count.

## 4. Sensitivity

The read swings most on **price per home (ASP)**, and that is also the input with the most history of moving. Roughly 90%+ of the unit's economics ride on the Dubai off-plan price: land and construction costs are largely fixed once committed, so a 20% fall in ASP would compress the ~49% gross margin by far more than 20 percentage points, and would simultaneously slow the 90–100% absorption and the buyer pre-funding that make the model capital-light — a double hit to both margin and the negative-working-capital advantage. Price is not a hypothetical lever: residential gross margin has already swung from ~32% (2020–21) to ~59% (2023) to ~49% (2025), a range driven mainly by the Dubai price cycle. By contrast, the cost to acquire the sale (~4% commission + ~2% marketing) is small and steady — a 20% move there barely touches the conclusion — and construction cost, while the second-most-important lever, has been less volatile than price. Net: the whole unit-economics case is, at its core, a bet on the Dubai residential price cycle.

## 2. Input Cost Pass-Through

Emaar has **no contractual mechanism to pass rising input costs back to buyers who have already bought.** Off-plan units are sold at a fixed price at launch, before construction, and the buyer pays fixed instalments during the ~4-year build; the pool discloses no cost-escalator or indexed-price clause in buyer contracts. So when steel, cement, labour and contractor prices rise during construction, Emaar absorbs the increase on the units already sold — the sold-but-not-yet-built backlog. That absorption is visible: gross margin *(sales minus the cost of the land and building in what was sold)* fell from 63% in FY2023 to 55% in FY2025, and management now guides "low 50s sustainably" over the medium term [ciq_facts.json margin_trend; Q4'25 earnings-call summary]. The offset — and it is a real one — is that Emaar re-prices at every **new** launch: it raised prices on FY2025 launches while still clearing 90–100% of units [Q4'25 earnings-call summary]. In plain terms, it cannot pass cost inflation *backward* into the ~AED 155bn signed backlog, but it does pass it *forward* into new launches, with a lag of one launch cycle. Construction cost itself runs through cost of sales of AED 22,330m on AED 49,557m revenue, ~45% [FY2025 AR, Industry-Specific tab, Total Cost of Sales AED 22,329.96m; ciq_facts.json segments].

**Supplier / input concentration (quantify).** The pool does **not** quantify supplier concentration — there is no disclosed share of cost of sales from the largest supplier or the top 3, and no single-source or sole-source component is flagged. *Not proven from available data* for a numeric input-concentration figure. What the pool does show is that construction is outsourced to a competitive, fragmented pool of specialist contractors (Ahluwalia Contracts, Innovators Façade, Otis for elevators, plus Indian-subsidiary-level contractors) with no one contractor sized as material [Suppliers.xls; FY2025 AR operations narrative]. Steel and cement are commodities bought largely *through* those contractors, so Emaar is a price-taker on the commodity but not dependent on any one vendor. The one genuinely concentrated, critical input is **land**, and the critical counterparty is the **Government of Dubai / Investment Corporation of Dubai (ICD)** — Emaar's 22.3% controlling shareholder with "significant influence," the city's master urban planner, and the source of incremental land (allocations and JVs such as the 50-50 Emaar Hills deal) [FY2025 AR related-party note; Q3'25 earnings-call summary]. A ~600m sq ft legacy land bank at low historical cost buffers this dependency for 10–15 years, but on *new* land Emaar takes the government's terms. This land + government node — not any raw material — is the material single-source-style dependency to carry into §5.

## 3. Customer Pricing Power

Emaar can raise prices without losing volume, and it has done so in the last 24 months. FY2025 launches "achieved 90–100% absorption rates alongside increased prices, reflecting strong demand with no pressure on pricing," and total property sales rose to a record AED 80bn, +15.6% YoY, with Q1 2026 up a further 16% to AED 22.4bn — price and volume rising together, the textbook signature of pricing power [Q4'25 & Q1 2026 earnings-call summaries / press releases]. The customer base carries no concentration risk: buyers are individual retail and high-net-worth investors, ~80% of them first-time buyers, the largest single nationality only ~20% (India) — so no buyer can dictate terms [Q3'25 earnings-call summary; Customers.xls shows no material single external customer]. Emaar's leverage over the buyer is unusually hard: buyers **forward-fund** the build through instalments into regulated escrow (~AED 44bn held), so customers pay before they receive the finished home — the customer finances Emaar's working capital, not the other way round [Q3'25 earnings-call summary]. On the mall side the same holds — ~98% occupancy and 110m+ annual visitors give Emaar the whip hand over retail tenants [Q1 2026 & Q4'25]. The one caveat is that this pricing power is **cyclical, not structural**: it rides a record Dubai up-cycle, and consensus long-term growth of −14.8% signals the market expects the cycle to roll over, at which point absorption and pricing weaken regardless of brand (base rate: the 2015–2019 and 2009 Dubai downturns) [ciq_facts.json consensus_view; 10_external-dependency §3].

## 4. Economic Control Verdict

**Mixed.**

Emaar is dominant over its customers and only a partial price-taker on its inputs — strong on one side, weaker on the other. Downstream it sets price and payment terms, raised prices into 90–100% absorption, faces a fragmented buyer base with no concentration, and is forward-funded by buyer cash in escrow — this is close to full control of its own output economics [Q4'25 earnings-call summary; Q3'25 escrow AED 44bn]. It is held out of a clean "Controls economics" verdict by two upstream facts: it absorbs commodity and labour cost inflation on its fixed-price sold backlog with no pass-through clause (gross margin 63% → 55%, guided low-50s), and its single most critical input — land, plus the visa/population and tax policy that manufactures demand — is set by the Government of Dubai / ICD, its own 22.3% controller, not by Emaar (the new 15% top-up tax alone added AED 2,114m of tax in 2025, imposed by the state) [ciq_facts.json margin_trend; FY2025 AR tax note; 10_external-dependency §1]. It is nowhere near "Squeezed": a competitive contractor pool, a huge low-cost land buffer, and ~AED 25bn net cash *(cash above all debt, CIQ broad basis)* mean no supplier can corner it either [ciq_facts.json net_debt/total_debt].

## 5. The Single Biggest Bargaining Risk

The **Government of Dubai / ICD upstream relationship** — the counterparty that simultaneously supplies Emaar's scarcest input (land + master-plan approvals) and sets the visa, population and tax policy that creates its buyer demand, while holding 22.3% control — is the one value-chain link where Emaar takes terms rather than sets them, and its deterioration (tighter land allocation, adverse policy, or further tax) would hurt most.

Supporting proxies point the same way: a revenue backlog (sold, not yet recognised) of AED 163.4bn at 31-Mar-2026 and 90–100% absorption on FY2025 launches [Q1 2026 press release; Q4 2025 earnings call summary]. The reason this is "holding" and not clearly "gaining": DAMAC (record AED 36bn) and Sobha (+~50% in 2024) grew faster in percentage terms off smaller bases, so Emaar's ~2x lead is stable rather than expanding.

## 4. Competitive Shape

**Consolidated-but-competitive, led by one dominant player — a "leader-plus-cluster" oligopoly of branded developers sitting above a state-owned master-developer bloc.** A handful of large names supply most new Dubai off-plan stock: by 2025 sales value, Emaar AED 80.4bn, DAMAC ~36, Sobha ~30, Binghatti ~26, with state-owned Nakheel (~AED 13bn Jan–Aug) and Meraas (~AED 10bn Jan–Aug) — both now consolidated under Dubai Holding Real Estate — behind them [Q4 2025 press release; Web: Time Homes / royalpproperties rankings, 2025–2026 — unverified]. Sobha alone put its 2024 share at ~10%, which frames the order of magnitude: no single firm monopolises, but the top ~6 branded developers dominate new supply. A formal concentration index (HHI) and official per-developer shares are not published — the Dubai total of 270,000+ transactions worth AED 892bn in 2025 mixes resale and all segments, a different base from developer primary sales, so it is not a clean share denominator [Q4 2025 earnings call summary]. One structural twist: the state-owned bloc is both rival and partner — Emaar's UAE Development and Rove Hotels run as joint ventures with Meraas (co-opetition) [FY2025 Annual Report, Note / segment and hospitality sections].

## 5. Caveat

Two limits on this map, both disclosed rather than papered over:
1. **Emaar's own filings do not name competitors in a rivalry sense.** Aldar, Meraas and Nakheel appear only as JV partners, an ESG co-participant, or in director biographies — not in a "our competitors are…" disclosure. Competitor identification therefore leans on the in-pool CIQ comp set (Aldar) plus industry press (DAMAC, Sobha, Binghatti).
2. **The two largest *direct* Dubai rivals (DAMAC, Sobha) are private, so their margins and returns are not audited-public.** DAMAC's revenue/profit figures conflict across web sources and are unverified; Sobha discloses only sales and a sukuk rating. Only Aldar carries a clean, sourced peer margin — so the moat's per-peer profitability comparison rests mainly on Aldar, with DAMAC/Sobha as directional at best. **What would resolve it:** DAMAC's DIFC sukuk-issuer accounts (Damac Real Estate Development Ltd) and Sobha/PNC Investments sukuk disclosures for audited peer margins and returns; Dubai Land Department developer-level sales data for hard market shares.

**Return-metric note (management-headline cross-check).** The 12.5%/13.7% ROIC is a Capital-IQ vendor computation, not a management headline; it reconciles with the business-quality through-cycle read (FY2021→LTM: 4.7% → 5.7% → 8.5% → 9.8% → 12.5% → 13.7%). [`10_external-dependency.md` §1 (PBT AED 25,656m); `04_unit-economics.md`]

**NOPAT tax normalization (anchor for the DCF).** NOPAT = EBIT × (1 − tax rate). The normalized **structural tax rate used here is 15%** — the UAE Domestic Minimum Top-up Tax (the OECD "Pillar Two" jurisdictional minimum), now the forward rate. What is stripped: FY2025's *reported* ~13% effective rate was cushioned by a one-off relief, and — the larger distortion — **FY2021–FY2023 carried near-zero corporate tax** (UAE had no corporate income tax before FY2024), so the CIQ ROIC series is *tax-flattered in its early years*. Normalizing the whole series to 15% trims the LTM peak from 13.7% to **~13.4%** and the raw through-cycle average from ~8.2% to **~7.5%** (ex-COVID-trough FY2023–25 normalized ≈ 9.5%). This 15% rate is the anchor `valuation/04_intrinsic-dcf` (§1) must reconcile its NOPAT to — the moat ROIC and the DCF must not diverge on the tax rate.

**Net-cash / gross-capital check.** Emaar holds ~AED 25bn net cash / ~AED 35bn gross cash (total debt AED 10.1bn − net debt −AED 25.0bn = AED 35.0bn cash). The classic net-cash distortion — where netting cash *out* of the capital base collapses the denominator and inflates ROIC — **does not bite here**: CIQ computes ROIC on total capital (debt + equity) *without* netting cash, so the large cash pile sits *inside* the base and if anything **dilutes** the reported ROIC. The 13.7%/12.5% headline is therefore the conservative (cash-in-base, gross-capital) read; an operating ROIC that stripped the idle cash would be *higher*, not lower. Either way the conclusion holds: through the cycle, after 15% tax, the return is ~7.5–9.5%. *(Convention note: Inference on CIQ's invested-capital definition, not from filings.)*

**The economic moat test (required).**

> Return on capital **at / below** cost of capital through the cycle: **~7.5–9.5% through-cycle ROIC** (15%-tax-normalized; 13.4% LTM *peak*) vs **~11–12% WACC** — a gap of roughly **−200 to −450 bps through the cycle**; the return clears the WACC by only ~+150–220 bps *at the cyclical peak* and shrinks to roughly parity once tax-normalized. [WACC basis: CAPM estimate = risk-free ~4.5% (US 10Y, AED pegged to USD) + beta ~1.15 × UAE equity-risk-premium ~6.0% ≈ **~11.4%**; net-cash balance sheet means WACC ≈ cost of equity; plausible range ~10–12.5%. **Inference, not from filings** (CLAUDE.md §3) — no company-disclosed hurdle rate, WACC or cost of equity in the pool.]

The verdict is robust across the WACC range: even at the low end (~10%), the tax-normalized through-cycle ~7.5–9.5% sits at or below cost of capital, and only the *peak* year clears it.

## 4. Where The Company Sits

1. **Relative to peers:** **Company sits at the top of named peers on margin and (where disclosed) capital efficiency** — 55% gross / ~45% EBIT vs Aldar's 34.9% / 27.2% (an ~18-point EBIT gap), and ~2x #2 DAMAC's sales; DAMAC/Sobha margins are undisclosed (private) but Emaar's sales lead and premium positioning place it top-of-set. The peer-superiority is real and wide.
2. **Absolute (the economic moat test):** **The company earns a return on capital *above* its cost of capital only at the cyclical peak (13.4% normalized vs ~11.4% WACC) and *at or below* it through the cycle (~7.5–9.5% vs ~11.4%).** This is the decisive test — and it shows the wide peer-margin lead does **not** reliably convert into economic value-creation once the Dubai cycle and the new 15% tax are through-cycled.

## 5. Moat Verdict

**Narrow moat.**

The strongest moat is the **Emaar brand (68/100), inseparable from the irreplaceable Downtown-Dubai location (65) and the low-cost legacy land bank (60)** — one integrated advantage that lets Emaar out-earn its one audited peer (Aldar) by ~18 EBIT-margin points and hold ~2x the #2's sales. That advantage is genuine and evidenced. But it is capped at Narrow by the economic test: through the Dubai cycle, and after the new 15% structural tax, the return on capital is only **~7.5–9.5%** against a **~11–12% cost of capital** — it clears the hurdle only at the cyclical peak. **Hard rule applied (CLAUDE.md / module §3): because through-cycle ROIC sits at or below WACC, the verdict cannot be "Strong moat" despite the wide peer-margin superiority — this is closer to a moat in *structure* (brand, location, scale) than in *economics* through the cycle, and Narrow is the ceiling.** The durability test it must pass over the next 5 years: hold pricing and absorption as ~167,000 new Dubai units complete into 2026–27 (consensus already models −14.8% long-term growth), and defend the margin as the cheap-legacy-land spread narrows toward market-priced new land. Industry rate-of-change is low (business-quality 72/100), so §24 Filter 5 does **not** trip and durability is *not* discounted for disruption — the risk is the property cycle, not obsolescence.

**Moat trajectory — eroding.** Separate from the *level* (Narrow), the *direction* of the **economic** moat is eroding, even though headline returns sit at a cyclical peak: (1) the cost-advantage spread is structurally narrowing — gross margin 63% (FY2023) → 55% (FY2025), guided "low 50s," as new land is bought at market (AED 120bn GDV in 2025); (2) the new 15% DMTT tax permanently lowers after-tax returns versus the pre-2024 zero-tax regime the historical ROIC was earned under; (3) competitive intensity is rising at the peak (DAMAC record AED 36bn, Sobha +~50%, plus Binghatti/Danube); (4) consensus prices a −14.8% long-term contraction, i.e. the return-vs-WACC gap is cyclically elevated now and expected to compress. The one *stable* pillar is the brand + Downtown-Dubai location, which is not disappearing. Net: the economic advantage (return above cost of capital) is eroding behind a peak-cycle headline — the early-warning signal for the peak-return / declining-perpetuity treatment in `valuation/04` (§5) and `valuation/07`; peak-year returns must be anchored, not extrapolated.
