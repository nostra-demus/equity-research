## 2. Input Cost Pass-Through

| Axis | What it asks | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Contractual pass-through** | Is there an escalator, indexed-pricing, or cost-plus clause that moves price automatically? | **No — for zero percent of disclosed revenue.** Nothing in the pool discloses an escalator, an index-linked price, a raw-material surcharge, or a cost-plus term on any contract. The only contract-price language runs the *other* way: fixed-price project bids, where a cost overrun above the bid estimate is nVent's loss. Pricing is set "at or prior to the time of sale… at the agreed-upon net selling price", then reduced by negotiated volume rebates. | FY24 10-K, Item 1A — dependence on subcontractors ("fixed-price contracts"); Item 8, Note 1 — Revenue recognition |
| **Realised recovery** | Of a given input-cost increase, how much did the company actually recover — through price, mix, hedging, re-sourcing, or a cost programme? | **2022→2023 raw-material shock: more than 100% recovered.** **2025→2026 tariff and inflation shock: roughly 40% recovered in Q1 FY26, roughly 79% in Q2 FY26, roughly 58% across the half — and rising each quarter as price actions took hold.** Not zero, and not full, on the current shock. | Arithmetic below |

**The 2022→2023 shock — over-recovered.** Group price rose **+5.5%** in 2023 versus 2022 while volume fell only **0.4%**, and gross margin expanded from **35.9% to 40.3%, up 440 basis points** (a basis point is one hundredth of a percent; 100bp = 1 percentage point). The filing's own segment bridges attribute **+4.2 points** of Enclosures margin and **+4.0 points** of Electrical & Fastening margin to price alone. Then in 2024, when input pressure eased, group price went to **−0.2%** and Enclosures price to **−0.4%** (costing 0.3 points of margin) — price is cost-linked, given back when cost falls, not a permanent ratchet [FY24 10-K, Item 7 — Consolidated Results of Operations; Net sales components of change; Enclosures and Electrical & Fastening Solutions segment income components].

**The 2025→2026 shock — partial and improving. Here is the arithmetic, quarter by quarter.** Management disclosed the gross pre-mitigation input-cost impact on both calls; the filings give the margin that actually landed.

> `realised recovery = 1 − (observed margin impact ÷ disclosed gross pre-mitigation impact)`

| Period | Disclosed gross inflation, pre-mitigation | ÷ net sales | = pre-mitigation impact | Observed gross margin change | **Realised recovery** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 FY26 | "nearly $60 million, including approximately $40 million in tariff impact" | $1,242.0m | ~483bp | 35.9% vs 38.8% = **−290bp** | 1 − 290/483 ≈ **40%** |
| Q2 FY26 | "more than $50 million, including more than $30 million in tariff impact" | $1,471.3m | ~340bp | 37.9% vs 38.6% = **−70bp** | 1 − 70/340 ≈ **79%** |
| H1 FY26 | ~$110m combined | $2,713.3m | ~405bp | 37.0% vs 38.7% = **−170bp** | 1 − 170/405 ≈ **58%** |

[Q1 FY26 transcript, 2026-05-01, prepared remarks (Corona); Q2 FY26 transcript, 2026-07-31, prepared remarks (Corona); Q1 FY26 10-Q, MD&A — Consolidated Results of Operations; Q2 FY26 10-Q, MD&A — Consolidated Results of Operations.]

Three qualifiers travel with those numbers and must not be dropped downstream. **(a)** The inflation figures are management statements on a call (tier 6), not filed figures, and two are floors ("more than $50 million", "nearly $60 million"), so the recovery rates are conservative — the true denominators are at least this large, which makes the recovery at least this high. **(b)** The observed gross-margin change is *not* purely input cost: the 10-Q lists unfavourable product mix and investments in capacity alongside inflation as the drags, and higher volume leverage plus productivity as the offsets, so part of the residual is mix and capacity spend rather than unrecovered inflation — the pure price-cost recovery is higher than the table shows. **(c)** Q2 gross profit included roughly **$25 million** of IEEPA tariff reimbursements, which the filing says "were offset by other incremental tariffs compared to the prior year periods" — the $25.8m is separately excluded from segment income, so it does not flatter the segment-level read.

**The mechanism, the lag, and the tools.** nVent does not pass cost through automatically; it **prices it back with a one-to-two-quarter lag**. The 10-K describes the standing playbook: "We strive for productivity improvements and implement increases in selling prices to help mitigate cost increases in raw materials, freight, energy, wage and other costs" [FY24 10-K, Item 1A — cost and other inflation]. The Q2 FY26 10-Q repeats it for the tariff era: "We have taken pricing actions, and may take additional pricing actions going forward, and implemented… supply chain optimization and other productivity improvements that have helped… offset expected cost increases" [Q2 FY26 10-Q, MD&A — Key Trends and Uncertainties]. The lag is visible in the table above (40% → 79% across two quarters) and in the Electrical Connections segment, whose margin fell 390bp year on year in Q1 FY26 and 140bp in Q2 FY26 but "improved sequentially back into the high 20s" as "the pricing and productivity actions… take hold" [Q1 FY26 10-Q, MD&A — Electrical Connections; Q2 FY26 transcript, prepared remarks and Q&A (Corona)].

The disclosed tool kit is narrow, and one common tool is **absent**. Item 7A names only interest-rate and foreign-currency exposures and says derivatives are used "for the purpose of hedging interest rate and currency exposures" — **there is no commodity hedging programme disclosed anywhere in the pool**, so steel, copper and aluminium move straight into cost of goods sold [FY24 10-K, Item 7A — Quantitative and Qualitative Disclosures About Market Risk]. What is left is: (i) **list-price increases**, the measured 5.5% group action of 2023 and the unquantified "selective increases in selling prices" of 2026 [FY24 10-K, Item 7 — Net sales; Q2 FY26 10-Q, MD&A — Net sales]; (ii) **productivity and supply-chain re-sourcing**, credited in the 10-Q for "increased productivity as a result of supply chain management and manufacturing efficiencies" and listed as a 2026 objective ("supply chain resiliency") [Q2 FY26 10-Q, MD&A — Gross profit; Key Trends]; and (iii) **restructuring savings**, which cut SG&A by 460bp of sales in Q2 FY26 [Q2 FY26 10-Q, MD&A — SG&A].

**Which is it — the customer paying more, or nVent's own cost falling?** Both, and the split is not disclosed. Management reports the two together every quarter — "price plus productivity offset inflation of more than $50 million" — and has never separated them [Q2 FY26 transcript, prepared remarks (Corona)]. That matters: a productivity programme can run out, a price increase can stick. On the 2023 precedent the filing *did* split it, and price alone carried 4.0–4.2 points of segment margin, so **price** was the dominant lever then. Whether that still holds in 2026 is **not proven from available data**.

**Scale of the cost being fought.** FY2026 tariff impact is guided at approximately **$100m**, raised from $80m, on top of **$90m in 2025** — "$170 million all-in" on the CFO's Q1 count. On guided FY26 reported sales growth of 37–39% applied to FY2025 revenue of $3,893.1m (~$5.34–5.41bn), the FY26 tariff alone is roughly **185bp of revenue** before any mitigation. Management's stated expectation is full offset: "Pricing is expected to offset the impact of inflation, including tariffs" [Q2 FY26 transcript, prepared remarks and Q&A (Corona); Q1 FY26 transcript, Q&A (Corona); FY2025 revenue from Capital IQ Financials → Segments tab — tier-5 vendor export, not a filing]. That is guidance, not a measurement; the measurement is the 40% / 79% / 58% table above.

**Supplier / input concentration (quantify).** The engine **cannot** quantify it, and the reason is disclosure, not effort. The FY24 10-K names the inputs — "mild steel, stainless steel, electronic components, copper, aluminum and paint (powder and liquid)" — but gives **no share of cost of goods sold for the largest supplier, the top three, or any single raw material. Not proven from available data**, and with no CIQ supplier export in the pool there is no vendor cross-check either. What the filing does say points away from a single-source dependency: materials are bought "on the open market and the majority is available through multiple sources which are in adequate supply", electronic components and packaging come "from a number of suppliers", and "alternate sources of supply at competitive prices are available for most materials for which long-term commitments exist" [FY24 10-K, Item 1 — Raw materials]. **No sole-source or single-source input, and no key component, is disclosed as a material dependency.** The sizing evidence that does exist is thin and indirect: raw materials and supplies inventory of $146.4m at 2024-12-31 (from $139.7m), and contractual purchase obligations of $66.7m for 2025 against $1,797.0m of FY24 cost of goods sold [FY24 10-K, Item 8, Note — Inventories; Item 7 — Contractual obligations]. Two supply-side dependencies are real but are **country** exposures rather than supplier exposures: goods sourced from Mexico and China, which is what makes tariffs a $170m two-year bill; and project **subcontractors**, whose failure to deliver forces re-sourcing "at a higher price" on a fixed-price contract [FY24 10-K, Item 1A]. Both are carried to §5.

## 3. Customer Pricing Power

nVent can raise prices without losing volume in its catalogue business, and the filing proves it with a number rather than an adjective: in 2023 the group put through **+5.5% price** while volume moved only **−0.4%**, and the Electrical & Fastening segment put through **+5.9% price** against a **2.3%** volume decline — a demand response far smaller than the price move, which is what price power looks like [FY24 10-K, Item 7 — Net sales components of change, consolidated and by segment]. The pricing actions of the **last 24 months** are the harder read, because the company stopped disclosing the split: the FY24 10-K gives price and volume separately, the FY26 10-Qs do not, reporting only that organic growth "includes selective increases in selling prices" [Q2 FY26 10-Q, MD&A — Net sales]. On the calls, management confirmed price actions were taken in Q1 FY26 in response to fuel and copper ("we've taken actions in the quarter on pricing… we have a playbook to do this") and that pricing was still "taking hold" through Q2 [Q1 FY26 transcript, Q&A (Corona); Q2 FY26 transcript, Q&A (Corona)]. The volume reaction to those 2026 increases was **not negative**: organic sales grew 41.2% in H1 FY26 and 46.9% in Q2, and distributor sell-in and sell-out were described as "well balanced" with "strong sell-through" [Q2 FY26 10-Q, MD&A — Net sales; Q2 FY26 transcript, Q&A (Wozniak)]. The honest limit: with demand running this hot, a price increase absorbed without volume loss tests very little — the 2023 print, taken in a flat-volume year, is the better evidence, and it is now three years old. Two things push the other way. Gross margin has fallen in five consecutive reported halves-and-quarters shown in the pool (40.3% FY23 → 40.2% FY24 → 38.8% Q1'25 → 37.0% H1'26), so whatever price is being taken is not yet holding the factory margin line; and the project business prices through "a lengthy bidding and selection process" against competitors, where the bid, not the list, sets the price [FY24 10-K, Item 1A — ability to bid and win new contracts].

## 4. Economic Control Verdict

**Mixed.**

nVent is strong on the output side and a price-taker on the input side. Downstream it sets list prices and made them stick — **+5.5% group price in 2023 with volume down only 0.4%**, worth 4.0–4.2 points of segment margin — and no customer has taken more than 10% of net sales in any disclosed year, so no buyer dictates terms [FY24 10-K, Item 7 — Net sales components; Item 8, Note 1 — Trade receivables]. Upstream it has **alternatives but not authority**: it buys steel, copper and aluminium on the open market with **no commodity hedging disclosed**, locks only ~3.7% of a year's input bill in price commitments it says are not material, and has absorbed a two-year tariff bill of roughly **$170m** that it can only price back with a one-to-two-quarter lag — recovering roughly **40% in Q1 FY26 and 79% in Q2** [FY24 10-K, Item 7A; Item 1 — Raw materials; Q1 and Q2 FY26 transcripts and 10-Qs]. That is a company that **recovers** its economics rather than one that **controls** them. It is not "Squeezed": a squeezed business does not put through 5.5% price with flat volume, and does not lift adjusted return on sales 110bp to 21.9% in the middle of a tariff shock [Q2 FY26 transcript, prepared remarks (Corona)].

## 5. The Single Biggest Bargaining Risk

The **data-centre customer relationship** — infrastructure went from 45% of 2025 sales to "nearly 60%" in H1 FY26 on management's own account, orders are "large and lumpy", the liquid-cooling specification is set by someone else's roadmap ("we're working with NVIDIA and others on their road maps out through 2030"), and the only customer-concentration disclosure in the pool — "no customer accounted for more than 10% of net sales" — is a **FY2024** fact from before that vertical doubled, with the FY2025 10-K absent, so whether a single hyperscaler has since crossed 10% is untestable from this evidence [2026 William Blair deck, 2026-06-03, slide 7; Q2 FY26 transcript, prepared remarks and Q&A (Wozniak); FY24 10-K, Item 8, Note — Segment reporting].
