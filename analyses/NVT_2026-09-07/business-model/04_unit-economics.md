**Evidence warning before any number is read.** The **FY2025 Form 10-K is absent from the pool**, so there is no audited FY2025 segment or cost-of-sales data available to this run [`analyses/NVT_2026-09-07/business-model/00_data-triage.md`, §3]. Three tiers appear below and are never blended:

- **FY2022–FY2024** — the only **audited** segment cost detail in the pool [FY24 10-K, Note 15, p.70–71].

## 1. Natural Unit

**The natural economic unit for Systems Protection is one megawatt (MW) of data-centre capacity equipped — the unit nVent itself sizes its own opportunity in, at roughly $1 million of nVent content per MW** [2026 William Blair Growth Stock Conference deck, 2026-06-03, slide 11: "~$1M / MW opportunity for nVent", footnoted "Opportunity analysis is based on nVent internal estimates"].

That is the right unit because the segment has been remade by one end market: infrastructure is 1,289.3 of 1,966.9 = 65.5% of Systems Protection sales in H1 FY26, up from 44.8% a year earlier [Q2 FY26 10-Q, Note 2, p.9], and management expects total data-centre sales above $2 billion in 2026 against $1.0 billion in 2025 [Q2 FY26 transcript, 2026-07-31, prepared remarks; William Blair deck, 2026-06-03, slide 11].

**But nVent never discloses megawatts.** It does not report MW served, MW shipped, units produced, tons, or any physical volume — in the 10-K, the 10-Qs, the decks or the calls. An analyst asked directly about "content per megawatt" on the Q2 call and management answered about the product roadmap without giving a number [Q2 FY26 transcript, Q&A, Varun Govindaraj / Bernstein]. The $1M/MW figure is an **opportunity ceiling** (what nVent could sell into a megawatt), not realised revenue per megawatt, and it is a deck estimate, not a filing.

So the table below is built on the two units nVent's own disclosure can actually measure, and each row says which:
- **(A) One dollar of Systems Protection product sold** — the segment's per-dollar economics, which the filings give in full (net sales, cost of goods sold, SG&A, R&D by segment) [Q2 FY26 10-Q, Note 13, p.20; FY24 10-K, Note 15, p.70].
- **(B) One dollar of new annual revenue capacity** — what it costs nVent to build or buy the ability to sell that dollar.

Secondary unit types: for **Electrical Connections** (27.5% of revenue) the unit is a shorter-cycle catalogue product sold through distribution; for the M&A programme the unit is an **acquired business**, priced per dollar of its revenue and EBITDA.

## 3. Value Creation Read

**Creates value — clearly, on the capacity nVent builds itself, and at peak-cycle returns. Unclear on the capacity it buys.** Each dollar of new annual Systems Protection revenue costs roughly $0.10 of capex plus ~$0.21 of working capital and returns ~$0.254 of segment income a year, so built capacity pays for itself in a bit over a year pre-tax; that comfortably clears any plausible cost of capital, and it survives a wide margin of error in the derived inputs. The bought unit is a different business: Maverick Power at $1.75 billion for ~$700 million of revenue is **25 times more expensive per dollar of annual revenue than building**, and at ~11.5x 2026 adjusted EBITDA the purchase price returns about 8.7% pre-tax before depreciation, tax and integration [`nVent-to-Acquire-Maverick-Power-2026.pdf`, 2026-08-24].

**The group-level number that proves the point, named.** Capital IQ's return on capital for nVent is **9.5% for the twelve months to 2026-06-30 — the best in six years — after 6.7% (FY21), 6.2% (FY22), 6.3% (FY23), 6.3% (FY24) and 7.2% (FY25)** [`ciq_facts.json`, `multi_year_trajectory`, sourced to Capital IQ Financials → Ratios; tier-5 vendor, not a filing]. Systems Protection's own pre-tax return on its identifiable assets is roughly **26%** on an annualised H1 FY26 basis (segment income 451.3 × 2 = 902.6 over average identifiable assets of 3,461.7, the mean of 3,342.2 at 2025-12-31 and 3,581.1 at 2026-06-30 — a run-rate flow over an average stock, both bases stated; derived here, inference, not from filings) [Q2 FY26 10-Q, Note 13, p.20 and p.22; Capital IQ `Segments` tab for the 2025-12-31 balance]. The gap between ~26% at the segment and 9.5% for the group is the acquisition bill: $2,676.3m of goodwill and $1,793.3m of net intangibles sit in group capital but not in the segment margin, because reportable segment income is struck **before** intangible amortization of 82.2 in H1 FY26 alone [Q2 FY26 10-Q, Note 7, p.14; Balance Sheet p.4; Note 13, p.19 and p.21].

**Ring-fence: this is a peak-cycle read.** The 22.9% Systems Protection segment margin, the 25.4% incremental margin and the 9.5% group return on capital all sit at the top of every series in the pool. The through-cycle comparison is FY2022: Systems Protection earned a **17.0%** segment margin on a **31.9%** gross margin, and group return on capital was **6.2%** [FY24 10-K, Note 15, p.70 and MD&A, p.25; Capital IQ Ratios via `ciq_facts.json`]. Do not treat ~26% segment returns or a one-year payback as a structural constant.

**The number that disagrees, named (CLAUDE.md §3).** Segment margin rose 1.7 points year on year while **gross margin per unit fell 2.1 points**. Those move in opposite directions, and the reconciliation is pure scale, not better unit economics: Systems Protection SG&A fell from 13.18% to 9.66% of sales and R&D from 1.95% to 1.61% — a 3.9-point improvement in fixed-cost leverage — against the 2.1-point gross-margin loss [Q2 FY26 10-Q, Note 13, p.20; arithmetic performed here]. **Each unit nVent sells is less profitable before overheads than a year ago; the segment margin only rose because the same overhead was spread over 72.5% more revenue.** That is an operating-leverage result and it reverses if volume reverses. This does not overturn the "creates value" verdict — 34.2 cents of gross profit per dollar sold still funds the payback easily — but it does mean the improvement is borrowed from volume, not earned per unit.

**Single most valuable missing disclosure:** **volume — megawatts served, units shipped, or a price-versus-volume split of organic growth.** Without it, nobody can tell how much of the 56.7% organic growth is price (which sticks) and how much is volume (which can vanish with one capital-spending cycle), and nobody can tell whether the ~$1 million per MW opportunity is being captured at 20% or at 60%. Second most valuable: backlog and order intake **by segment**, which would show whether the three committed Minnesota plants have work booked against them.

## 4. Sensitivity

**Volume is the input that would change the read, and it is by a wide margin the most volatile input in this business's own history.** The build-payback conclusion survives a 20% move in every other input: 20% more expensive capacity ($0.10 → $0.12 per $1 of revenue) pushes the all-in payback from ~1.2 to ~1.3 years; a 20% lower contribution margin (25.4% → 20.3%) pushes it to ~1.5 years; a 20% cut in the realised price per unit is arithmetically fatal but has no precedent in the disclosed record — the company has been *raising* prices ("selective increases in selling prices" [Q2 FY26 10-Q, MD&A, p.29]) and expects pricing to offset the roughly $100 million 2026 tariff bill in full [Q2 FY26 transcript, prepared remarks]. Volume is different. Systems Protection organic growth has swung from +6.8% (FY22→FY23 total growth) to +13.5% (FY23→FY24) to +56.7% (H1 FY26) in four reported periods [FY24 10-K, Note 15, p.70; Q2 FY26 10-Q, MD&A, p.29], while gross margin has moved inside a narrower 31.9%–37.3% band over the same span. A 20% volume shortfall would hit twice: it would strip out the fixed-cost leverage that produced the entire 1.7-point margin gain (roughly 3.9 points of SG&A and R&D leverage would run in reverse), and it would strand capacity that is already committed — three Minnesota liquid-cooling plants, the third ("Blaine 2") opening in H1 2027, against an order book management itself calls lumpy and a group backlog of $2.5 billion that was "basically kind of flattish, maybe down a little bit sequentially" in Q2 [Q2 FY26 transcript, prepared remarks and Q&A]. The capacity is being built roughly a year ahead of the demand it serves; that is the timing risk the unit economics do not price.
