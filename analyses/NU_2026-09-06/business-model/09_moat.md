**Regime and basis.** Nu Holdings Ltd. (NYSE: NU, Class A ordinary shares, USD) is a Cayman-incorporated US SEC foreign private issuer; the annual filing is **Form 20-F**, not a 10-K. **IFRS as issued by the IASB**, reporting currency **US dollars**, fiscal year ends 31 December `[FY25 Form 20-F (filed 8 Apr 2026), cover page]`.

**Sector overlay: bank / lender.** Per the triage, the Capital IQ workbook uses a **Bank template**, so there is **no EBITDA, no EBIT and no gross-margin row** anywhere in it. This report therefore tests returns the way a bank must be tested — **return on equity (ROE) against the cost of equity**, not ROIC against WACC — and builds the competitive economics on bank measures (net income margin, return on tangible book, net interest margin, efficiency). Forcing ROIC/WACC onto a deposit-funded lender would be a category error.

**Three bases are kept apart throughout.** (a) **IFRS / audited** — total revenue, expected credit loss, net income, equity, capital ratios. (b) **Managerial / non-IFRS, FX-neutral** — NIM, risk-adjusted NIM, ARPAC, cost to serve, efficiency ratio; company-defined, restated in Q4'25, not equal to the audited figures. (c) **Vendor (§4 tier 5)** — Capital IQ ratio and comps series, labelled at every use. Every number below says which basis it is on.

### The economic moat test

> Return on capital **above** cost of capital: **31.6% ROE (LTM to 30 Jun 2026, computed on average equity)** vs **16.51% cost of equity** (**+1,512 bps**) — cost of equity is the company's own disclosed rate: *"The discount rate used was the cost of equity for business in Brazil"*, 16.51%, with a 3.69% perpetual growth rate, used in the goodwill impairment test performed 30 September 2025 `[FY25 Form 20-F, Consolidated Financial Statements, Note 3(b) — Impairment of goodwill, p.F-38]`. **Scope label, required:** this is a **CGU-level** rate (the investments-activities cash-generating unit), not a group cost of equity, so it is used here as a **labelled comparator**, cross-checked against an independent group estimate below. It is nonetheless the closest disclosed read available, and it is the right *geography* — Brazil is 91.3% of geographically-attributed revenue `[FY25 Form 20-F, Note 34(b), p.F-97]`.

**Independent group cross-check — CAPM, *inference, not from filings*.** Inputs shown: Brazilian nominal risk-free rate **15.00%** (the Selic policy rate as of the annual-report date; average CDI interbank rate was 14.3% in 2025) `[FY25 Form 20-F, Item 3.D and Item 5, macroeconomic indicators table]`; five-year beta **0.94** `[Capital IQ Comps → Operating Statistics, as-of 2026-08-29 — vendor export]`; equity risk premium **4–6 percentage points — judgment, no sourced Brazilian ERP exists in this pool**. That gives a group BRL cost of equity of roughly **18.8%–20.6%, say 19–21%**. Two honest weaknesses: the beta is measured on a USD-listed line against a US index and is being paired with a BRL risk-free rate, which is a basis mismatch; and the ERP is judgment, not evidence. **The conservative default (§4) is therefore to test against the higher independent estimate, not the company's own lower figure.** Against 21%: LTM ROE 31.6% is **+1,063 bps** above; FY2025 ROE 30.3% is **+928 bps** above. The verdict is the same either way — the spread is positive and large.

**How to read those four rows, because they are not in conflict.** The loss-inclusive average is dragged below the cost of equity by FY2021 and FY2022, which were a **scaling business emerging from start-up losses, not a credit cycle** — upstream established this and the qualifier travels `[07_business-quality §1, margin-stability row]`. So the five-year average understates the mature economics. But the profitable-era average overstates the through-cycle level for the opposite reason: **the entire profitable record sits inside a benign Brazilian consumer environment**, with unemployment falling from 6.2% to 5.1% `[FY25 Form 20-F, Item 5, macroeconomic indicators table]`, on a book that is **92% unsecured** (credit card 66%, unsecured lending 26%) `[Q2'26 deck, p.13 — managerial]`. **A true through-cycle ROE cannot be computed from this pool: there is no Brazilian consumer downturn in the profitable record at anything like the current book size.** The latest figure is explicitly a peak — the CFO called the Q2'26 ROE of 33% "a record", risk-adjusted NIM at 12.4% is the highest of six quarters, and the loan-to-deposit ratio was pushed from 50% to 58% in three quarters to get there `[Q2 FY26 earnings call transcript (13 Aug 2026), CFO prepared remarks; Q2'26 deck, pp.15–16]`.

**Cross-checking the management-headline return, as required.** Nu's headline is **ROE 33% in Q2'26**, a single quarter annualised on total equity `[Q2 FY26 transcript, CFO prepared remarks]`. My own computation on the standard base (net income ÷ average equity) gives **FY2025: US$2,868.9m ÷ ((7,647.1 + 11,321.6)/2 = 9,484.3) = 30.25%**, and **LTM Jun-26: US$3,607.1m ÷ ((9,576 + 13,251.7)/2 = 11,413.9) = 31.6%** `[CIQ Financials → Balance Sheet and Income Statement exports — vendor export; arithmetic mine]`. Both tie the vendor's 30.28% and 31.63% to within 0.03pp, so there is no material divergence — but the 33% headline is a **peak-quarter annualised** figure and is not used as the test number. The computed LTM and FY figures are.

**Two mechanical checks the rules require, answered:**
- **Net-cash / gross-capital adjustment: not applicable.** The rule exists to stop a collapsed, cash-netted denominator inflating a return. Here the denominator is **shareholders' equity** (US$13,251.7m at 30 Jun 2026), which is not netted against anything; the vendor's "net cash" of US$9,274.2m is an artefact of a bank's balance sheet holding customer deposits, not a capital-structure fact `[CIQ facts sidecar, net_debt_m −9,274.2 — vendor basis]`. No gross-capital restatement is needed or meaningful.
- **Normalized tax rate anchor for the DCF (`valuation/04_intrinsic-dcf` §1).** The ROE numerator is already after tax, so no NOPAT is built here — but the anchor is stated so the DCF reconciles to it. Reported effective tax rate: **FY2023 33.0% · FY2024 29.4% · FY2025 25.8% · H1'26 11.8%**. The H1'26 rate is heavily distorted and must not be used: against a statutory combined Brazilian rate of **42.5%**, the reported charge was cut by US$280.8m of "effect of different tax rates — subsidiaries and parent company", US$108.8m of interest on capital, a US$28.4m one-off deferred-tax remeasurement from the CSLL rate change (Complementary Law 224/2025), and US$253.6m of foreign tax credits, non-taxable sovereign-bond interest and tax incentives `[Q2 FY26 Interim Report (14 Aug 2026), Note — Income tax reconciliation, items (i)–(iii)]`. **The normalized structural rate to use is ~26%** — the FY2025 audited full-year rate, stripping those one-offs. Direction of error is known and stated: the enacted CSLL step-ups (payment institutions 9% → 12% in 2026–27 → 15% from 2028; SCFIs 17.5% → 20%) push the structural rate **up** from here, so 26% is a floor, not a ceiling `[same note, item (ii)]`.

## 4. Where The Company Sits

1. **Relative to peers:** **Nu sits at the top of its named peers on both margin and capital efficiency.** LTM net income margin 42.73% against Itaú 32.58%, Bradesco 26.07%, Inter 22.92% and a peer median of 27.37%; return on tangible book 29.2% against Itaú 24.3%, BTG 23.7%, Inter 16.7%, Bradesco 15.2%, Santander Brasil 14.7% and Banco do Brasil 9.6% — the highest of the eleven companies on the matched vendor basis `[Capital IQ Comps → Financial Data and Operating Statistics, as-of 2026-08-29 — vendor export, via 08_competitive-map §2]`. Four of the eleven (Caixa, C6, PicPay, Mercado Pago) disclose nothing, so the ranking covers the listed set only.

2. **Absolute (the economic moat test):** **the company earns a return on capital above its cost of capital — 31.6% ROE against a 16.5%–21% cost of equity (a gap of roughly +1,060 to +1,510 bps)** — which is the decisive test of whether the advantage is economic rather than merely structural. Nu passes it on the latest and on the profitable-era average. It does **not** pass it on the loss-inclusive five-year average (12.40%, −411 bps below the company's own 16.51%), and no genuine through-cycle figure exists because the profitable record contains no consumer downturn.

## 5. Moat Verdict

# Narrow moat

**Why not Strong.** The advantage is real, specific and measured — an ~85% lower cost to serve, 14,314 customers per employee against incumbents' 1,234, a 64-versus-43 NPS that delivers 71% of new customers free, and 62% of Brazil's adults already on the platform — and it currently produces a return roughly 1,000–1,500 basis points above the cost of equity. But "Strong" requires that spread to be **sustained through the cycle**, and it is not yet: the profitable record is three and a half years old, every one of those years ran with Brazilian unemployment falling to 5.1%, the book is 92% unsecured, and the five-year loss-inclusive average return of 12.40% sits *below* the company's own disclosed 16.51% Brazilian cost of equity. On top of that, `07_business-quality` scored the industry rate-of-change row **38 (weak, ≤40)**, tripping the fast-changing-industry filter (`CLAUDE.md` §24, Filter 5) — the rails (Pix), the data rules (open finance), the price ceiling on card interest, the capital regime and the virtual-asset regime have each been rewritten inside six years, so **moat durability is explicitly discounted here**. A moat in an industry that reorders this fast decays faster than the same moat in a boring one.

**The strongest moat is the cost advantage (78/100), and the durability test it must pass over the next five years is this:** hold a return on equity above roughly 16.5% *through one full Brazilian consumer credit downturn*, with the 90+ day non-performing loan ratio (6.9% at Q2'26, up 35bp in the quarter) rising materially, while the price of the largest revenue line stays capped by Law 14,690/2023 and open finance keeps lowering the cost for customers to leave. Nothing in this pool tests that, because nothing in this pool contains that downturn. If it clears it, this is a Strong moat; if the spread over cost of equity closes in a normal recession, the cost advantage was a cyclical margin, not a moat.

**Naming the metric that disagrees with the verdict (§3).** The single loudest number arguing for **Strong** rather than Narrow is the peer gap: Nu out-earns every listed named competitor on both measures, and by a wide margin — 42.73% net margin against a 27.37% peer median, 29.2% return on tangible book against 24.3% for the best-performing large incumbent. That is real and it is not dismissed. It does not lift the verdict to Strong for one reason: peer superiority is not the economic test, and the *duration* of Nu's superiority is three and a half years inside one favourable credit environment, against Itaú's twenty-plus-year record of mid-twenties returns. A wide gap measured over a short, benign window is a weaker claim than a narrower gap measured across cycles.

### Moat trajectory — **stable**

Separate from the level. The direction over the last three to five years is **stable**: the *return* spread is widening, but the two lines that measure whether the *advantage itself* is still being extended — the annual share gain and the after-credit-cost spread ratio — have both flattened, and the improvement is being measured inside a benign credit cycle.

**Adjudicating the panel, by name, before naming the direction.** Five of the seven usable rows point up (pre-tax margin, net margin, return-on-capital spread, efficiency ratio, ARPAC/NIM), one points down (the gross-profit ratio, −543bp from its FY2024 peak) and one is not meaningful for a bank (cash conversion). On a straight count that majority would license **widening**. It does not, for three reasons that have to be stated rather than skipped. First, the **down row is the one that measures the moat most directly**: the gross-profit ratio is what Nu keeps after paying for its money and for its bad debts, and it is thinner than it was two years ago even as the top line trebled. Second, the **share row is up in level but down in rate** — a +0.3pp and +0.5pp gain in the last two years against +2.9pp and +2.3pp in the first two, on a base where 62% of Brazilian adults are already customers; a challenger taking share five times more slowly than it was is not extending its advantage, it is consolidating one. Third, the **pricing row is mix, not price** — management's own attribution says so — and the price of the biggest revenue line is set by statute, so this row cannot be read as evidence of growing pricing power. Against that, "eroding" is plainly not supportable either: the return spread over the cost of equity went from +173bp to +1,512bp, the cost ratio keeps improving, and churn halved. **The calibrated word is therefore *stable*: the level of the moat is holding and profitability is rising, but the extension of the advantage has flattened, and none of it has been tested against a downturn.** Neither "widening confirmed" nor "erosion confirmed" may be carried downstream from this panel.

**Which way I would be wrong, and when.** The single next data point is the **Q3 2026 results, due November 2026**: specifically the risk-adjusted net interest margin read beside the 90+ day non-performing loan ratio and the Tier 1 capital ratio in the same quarter. If risk-adjusted NIM falls back toward its 10.4% six-quarter average while 90+ NPL keeps climbing from 6.9% and Tier 1 keeps sliding from 13.4%, the return spread narrows from a peak that was never through-cycle, and the trajectory read moves from **stable to eroding**. If risk-adjusted NIM holds above 12% with 90+ NPL flat or lower, the read moves to **widening** and the level verdict moves toward Strong. A second, slower check lands with the FY2026 20-F (around April 2027), which prints the next annual ABECS card-share increment: another +0.3–0.5pp confirms the share ceiling; a return to +1pp or more reopens the widening case.
