# Value Chain Position — NU

**Regime and basis.** Nu Holdings Ltd. is a Cayman-incorporated foreign private issuer listed on the NYSE; the annual filing is a Form 20-F, not a 10-K. Reporting standard IFRS as issued by the IASB, reporting currency US dollars, fiscal year ends 31 December. Operating currencies are the Brazilian real, Mexican peso and Colombian peso. Tradable line referenced: **NYSE: NU, Class A ordinary shares, USD** [`FY25 Form 20-F, cover page`].

**What this agent is reading.** Upstream `02_business-identity.md` establishes the offering: a branchless digital consumer bank whose customer pays for credit, with 85.2% of FY2025 revenue arriving as interest income and gains net of losses on financial instruments [`FY25 Form 20-F, Item 5 — "Interest Income and Gains net of Losses on Financial Instruments"`]. For a bank the value chain is not made of physical parts. Its main input is **money** (deposits), and its other real inputs are the **card network** it must license to issue cards, the **cloud and network infrastructure** it runs on, and the **rails** the central bank owns. Those are the stages scored below.

**One source is deliberately excluded.** The 15-page deep-dive memo dated 30 Aug 2026 sitting in the pool is a prior engine output, not evidence. It is treated as a §4 tier-9 user note and nothing in this report is taken from it.

## 2. Input Cost Pass-Through

The dominant input cost for this business is the rate it pays for deposits. It is not a raw material, but it behaves exactly like one: an outside benchmark moves, and Nu's cost of goods moves with it. Brazil's policy rate went from 10.50% on 8 May 2024 to 15.00% on 18 June 2025 and was still 15.00% at the date of the annual report — a 450 basis point rise in the price of Nu's main input inside about thirteen months [`FY25 Form 20-F, Item 5 — Brazilian macroeconomic environment`].

| Axis | What it asks | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Contractual pass-through** | Is there an escalator, indexed-pricing, or cost-plus clause that moves price automatically? | **Partial, and it runs the wrong way on the input side.** Nu's *cost* is contractually indexed — deposits are priced as a percentage of the local interbank rate, the Colombian syndicated loan is indexed to IBR, the margin loan to CME Term SOFR, the DFC facility at SOFR + 2.90%. On the *revenue* side there is **no** disclosed escalator: credit card and personal loan rates are set by Nu at its own discretion, and interchange is set by a third party (Mastercard) and capped by the regulator (BCB Res. 246, 0.7% on prepaid). So the input re-prices automatically and the output does not. | `Q2 FY26 earnings call transcript (13 Aug 2026), CFO prepared remarks` (cost of deposits as % of interbank rate); `FY25 Form 20-F, Item 5 — Borrowings and Financing` (IBR, CME Term SOFR, SOFR + 2.90%); `FY25 Form 20-F, Item 4.B — Regulation of Credit Cards and Interchange Fees`; `FY25 Form 20-F, Item 5 — "Fees and Commission Income"` |
| **Realised recovery** | Of a given input-cost increase, how much did the company actually recover — through price, mix, hedging, re-sourcing, or a cost programme? | **Roughly 40% recovered on the deposit-price lever alone; more than fully recovered at the spread level by Q2 FY26; but not recovered inside the FY2025 annual accounts, where gross margin still fell 361bp.** All three numbers are shown below because they answer different questions and a single number here would mislead. | Derived from `FY25 Form 20-F, "Statement of Income Data"` and `FY25 Form 20-F, Item 5 — Interest and other financial expenses`; `Q2 FY26 earnings call transcript (13 Aug 2026)`; `Q3 FY25 earnings call transcript (13 Nov 2025)` |

**The realised-recovery arithmetic, shown.**

*Step 1 — size the gross, pre-mitigation shock, separating rate from volume.* Interest expense on deposits rose US$1,534.3m, or 65.5%, in FY2025, so the FY2024 base was US$2,342.4m (1,534.3 ÷ 0.655) and the FY2025 figure US$3,876.7m. Over the same year deposits grew 45.3%, from US$28.9bn to US$41.9bn [`FY25 Form 20-F, Item 5 — Interest and other financial expenses`]. Had the unit rate not moved at all, the volume growth alone would have carried the expense to US$3,403.5m (2,342.4 × 1.453). The residual — **US$473.2m — is the part caused purely by the higher rate**, and that is 3.00% of FY2025 total revenue of US$15,774.8m, or **300 basis points of revenue** [`FY25 Form 20-F, "Statement of Income Data"`]. *Basis flag (§15): the 45.3% deposit growth is a point-in-time year-end-to-year-end change, while the interest expense is a full-year flow. The split is therefore approximate, not exact; the filing does not disclose average deposit balances.*

*Step 2 — measure the mitigation Nu actually applied.* It cut the price it pays depositors relative to the benchmark: the consolidated cost of deposits was 88% of the interbank rate in Q2 FY26, "3 percentage points lower than a year ago" [`Q2 FY26 earnings call transcript (13 Aug 2026), CFO prepared remarks`], having been 91% a year before that and then 89% and 87% at intermediate points [`Q3 FY25 earnings call transcript (13 Nov 2025), CFO Q&A`; `Q4 FY25 earnings call transcript (25 Feb 2026), CFO prepared remarks`]. Three percentage points of a 15.00% Brazilian benchmark is about 0.45 percentage points of absolute deposit rate; on the US$41.9bn FY2025 deposit balance that is roughly **US$189m a year, or about 40% of the US$473.2m rate shock** (188.6 ÷ 473.2 = 39.9%). *Labelled derivation — inference, not from filings, on the aggregation: the 3pp figure is consolidated across three countries while 15.00% is the Brazilian rate only (Brazil held US$36.4bn of US$45.3bn deposits at Q2 FY26), and the 3pp change is a Q2 FY26 year-on-year measure applied to a FY2025 year-end balance. Read it as order of magnitude, not to the dollar.*

*Step 3 — check where it landed.* Two answers, and they differ by period. **In the FY2025 annual accounts, more than the whole rate shock landed.** Interest and other financial expenses rose from 24.61% of revenue (2,834.9 ÷ 11,517.0) to 29.02% (4,578.7 ÷ 15,774.8), a 441bp drag against a 300bp rate component; gross profit margin fell 361 basis points, from 45.61% (5,252.8 ÷ 11,517.0) to 42.00% (6,625.0 ÷ 15,774.8) [`FY25 Form 20-F, "Statement of Income Data"`]. Note what makes up the difference: of the 441bp, roughly 300bp is the rate and roughly 141bp is Nu deliberately holding more deposits than it lends out — a loan-to-deposit ratio of 35% is a balance-sheet choice, not a cost it failed to recover. **By Q2 FY26 the spread had more than recovered:** net interest margin (the gap between what it earns on loans and what it pays on deposits) reached 22.9% and risk-adjusted net interest margin (that gap after credit losses) 12.4%, up from 9.5% the prior quarter, both records on management's framework [`Q2 FY26 earnings call transcript (13 Aug 2026), CFO prepared remarks`]. *Basis warning: management restated its operating metrics onto a new managerial, non-IFRS framework in Q4 FY25 and back-cast it to Q1 2021, so a NIM figure quoted from a Q3 FY25 call (17.7% falling to 17.3%) is not on the same basis as the 22.9% quoted for Q2 FY26. Do not subtract one from the other. The like-for-like statements used above are the ones management itself made year-on-year (cost of deposits 3pp lower) and quarter-on-quarter within the new framework (9.5% → 12.4%).*

**The mechanisms the filings actually disclose, and how well each worked.**

Nu recovers input cost through four levers, and the filings let three of them be measured. **First, re-pricing its own deposits down** — the 91% → 88% path above, worth roughly 40% of the rate shock, and it is a *price* action with a visible volume cost: management "lowering deposit yields in Mexico and Colombia... with some significant changes implemented only now in early July 2025" [`Q2 FY25 earnings call transcript (14 Aug 2025), CFO prepared remarks`], after which Mexican deposits fell in Q1 FY26 and again in Q2 FY26, which management called a deliberate "deposit optimization strategy" [`Q1 FY26 earnings call transcript (14 May 2026)`; `Q2 FY26 earnings call transcript (13 Aug 2026)`]. **Second, asset mix** — shifting the book toward unsecured lending, which grew 45% year on year to US$10.3bn against secured at 30%, plus what the CFO calls "the deliberate risk expansions we made"; management attributes 178bp of the quarter's risk-adjusted NIM expansion to credit income [`Q2 FY26 earnings call transcript (13 Aug 2026), CFO prepared remarks`]. **Third, the card take rate.** Despite the 0.7% prepaid interchange cap that the filing says "impacted our fee and commission income", credit and prepaid card income rose 24.6% to US$1,720.3m on card purchase volume growth of 17% — implying the money Nu keeps per unit of card volume **rose about 6.5%** (1.246 ÷ 1.17 − 1) [`FY25 Form 20-F, Item 5 — Total revenue and Total fee and commission income`]. *Contradicting figure adjudicated by name (§3): the same MD&A paragraph also states "total purchase volume grew 29%". On that base the take rate would have **fallen 3.4%** (1.246 ÷ 1.29 − 1). The filing does not reconcile the two volume figures; the 17% is the one it attaches directly to the card-income line and is the matched basis, so it is used as primary — but the direction of this one measure is not settled by the pool, and it should not be quoted without the 29% alongside it.* **Fourth, the network cost line held.** Payments and network costs — which include the fee paid to Mastercard — went US$76.2m → US$83.6m → US$107.6m across FY2023–FY2025, which is 0.95% → 0.73% → 0.68% of total revenue [`FY25 Form 20-F, Consolidated Financial Statements, note 6(d) Transactional expenses`; revenue from `"Statement of Income Data"`]. The sole-source supplier that the risk factor says can raise its fees has, on this measure, taken a *smaller* share of Nu's revenue each year. What the filing does not let anyone see: the Mastercard-only portion of that line, because it also carries boleto issuance, Apple Pay fees, partner-bank ATM withdrawals and chargeback restatements.

Distinguishing the two kinds of recovery: the deposit re-pricing and the card take rate are **price pass-through** — the depositor gets less and the merchant-funded fee per unit holds or rises. The mix shift into unsecured lending and the falling network-cost share are **cost absorption and mix** — Nu's own economics improved rather than the customer paying more. That matters for durability, because the mix lever runs out (unsecured lending is already 26% of a book that grew 37%, and its losses arrive later), while a re-priced deposit base tends to stay re-priced.

**What must not be written downstream.** There is **no contractual escalator on Nu's revenue**. That is a fact about contracts. It is *not* a finding that Nu cannot recover input cost — the measured recovery on the deposit lever alone is roughly 40%, and the spread measures had fully recovered by Q2 FY26. Anyone shortening this section must carry the word "contractual", or quote the realised-recovery numbers instead.

## 3. Customer Pricing Power

Nu can raise price without losing volume, and the clearest proof is that it keeps choosing not to. The credit book grew 37% year on year to US$39.4bn while management held its underwriting framework unchanged, and the price it pays for its own funding fell three percentage points of the benchmark over the same year — so both sides of the spread moved in Nu's favour at once [`Q2 FY26 earnings call transcript (13 Aug 2026), CFO prepared remarks`]. Two explicit pricing actions inside the last 24 months are on the record, and both are **cuts**, not rises. On the deposit side, management began "lowering deposit yields in Mexico and Colombia... with some significant changes implemented only now in early July 2025", and the volume reaction was visible and accepted: Mexican deposits fell in Q1 FY26 and again in Q2 FY26, which the CFO described as an "ongoing deposit optimization strategy" that "continues to improve our cost of funding while maintaining ample liquidity with our loan-to-deposit ratio still at just 35%" [`Q2 FY25 earnings call transcript (14 Aug 2025)`; `Q1 FY26 earnings call transcript (14 May 2026)`; `Q2 FY26 earnings call transcript (13 Aug 2026)`]. On the lending side, Nu competes by charging less: the central bank's own December 2025 rate statistics put it among the lowest payroll-loan rates in Brazil, which the filing attributes to cutting out the distributing intermediary rather than to accepting a thinner margin [`FY25 Form 20-F, Item 4.B — payroll loans`]. The CEO states the trade-off directly: on the AI-driven price-optimisation work, "I wouldn't necessarily think in the short term that this will drive an improvement in funding costs because we might decide to reinvest all of those gains back into the customer... We're not in a mode of optimizing for net income" [`Q2 FY26 earnings call transcript (13 Aug 2026), CEO Q&A`]. The honest reading is that pricing power exists on the deposit side (measured: 3pp of benchmark reclaimed, with a tolerated deposit outflow in Mexico) and is **unproven by action** on the lending side, because Nu has not tested how much volume a rate rise would cost it. Two hard limits sit above it regardless: Brazilian law caps total interest and charges on revolving and installment card credit at the original debt amount, and the interchange Nu earns is set by Mastercard and capped by the central bank at 0.7% on prepaid [`FY25 Form 20-F, Item 4.B — Regulatory Overview, Brazil` and `Regulation of Credit Cards and Interchange Fees`].

## 4. Economic Control Verdict

**Mixed.**

Nu controls the input side of its economics almost completely: it manufactures its own funding from 139 million retail depositors, pays 88% of the local interbank rate for it, cut that by three percentage points in a year, lends out only 35 cents of every dollar it holds, and buys none of it through brokers or intermediaries — so no supplier of money has leverage over it, and the US$100m DFC facility, its only named outside credit line, is committed and undrawn [`FY25 Form 20-F, Item 5 — "Low Cost of Funding"` and `Borrowings and Financing`; `Q2 FY26 earnings call transcript (13 Aug 2026)`]. On the output side it does not control the price of a fifth of what it earns and cannot fully control the rest: the fees behind US$1,720.3m of FY2025 card income are "set by Mastercard and paid by the merchants", the prepaid slice of them is capped at 0.7% by BCB Res. 246, and the interest on the card book is capped by statute at the original debt amount [`FY25 Form 20-F, Item 5 — "Fees and Commission Income"`, `Note 6(b)`, `Item 4.B — Regulatory Overview`]. The party actually setting the rules on that side is not a supplier or a customer but the regulator — and it can move fast: a change to FGTS rules "reduced new originations by more than half" in one quarter [`Q4 FY25 earnings call transcript (25 Feb 2026), CFO prepared remarks`]. Strong upstream, constrained downstream, is the definition of Mixed.

## 5. The Single Biggest Bargaining Risk

**Mastercard.** It is the named sole source for authorising every one of Nu's card transactions — the card book is US$26bn of a US$39.4bn portfolio (66%) — it sets the interchange rate that produced US$1,720.3m of FY2025 fee income, it "may increase the fees that they charge", and it can fine, suspend or terminate Nu's registration outright; the December 2024 renewal with volume-linked incentives blunts this today but does not remove it, because nothing in the filing discloses the term or the exit terms of that agreement [`FY25 Form 20-F, Item 3.D risk factors`; `FY25 Form 20-F, Note 6(b)`; `FY25 Form 20-F, Consolidated Financial Statements, payables to credit card network note`; `Q2 FY26 earnings call transcript (13 Aug 2026)`].
