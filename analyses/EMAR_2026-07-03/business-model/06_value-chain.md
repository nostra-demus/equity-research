## 2. Input Cost Pass-Through

Emaar's core input costs are (a) land, (b) construction (contractor labour and materials), and (c) operating costs for the recurring businesses. For land, there is no pass-through question — Emaar assembled its land bank at historical cost and is not buying individual plots in competition with other developers on a project-by-project basis; land cost is essentially fixed at time of acquisition and recognised into COGS as units deliver. For construction, Emaar's structural protection comes from the sequencing of its revenue model: units are pre-sold to buyers before construction is complete (93% of units under development were sold as at 31 Dec 2025), meaning the selling price is locked in at launch and Emaar bears the construction cost risk between launch and delivery. There is no disclosed contractual pass-through clause by which a buyer absorbs Emaar's construction cost increases after the sale contract is signed. However, Emaar typically fixes construction costs through contractor agreements at or shortly after project launch, and uses milestone-based payment schedules to buyers to fund construction progressively, reducing unsecured cash exposure. No explicit indexed-pricing or escalator clauses between Emaar and end-buyers are disclosed in the data pool.

**Supplier / input concentration (quantify).** The Capital IQ supplier data covers recently disclosed supplier relationships but does not quantify any single contractor's share of COGS or total procurement spend. Disclosed named contractors include Ahluwalia Contracts (India) for the India subsidiary and Otis Worldwide (NYSE:OTIS) for elevators/escalators across the UAE portfolio; neither is reported as a sole-source or material single-source dependency in the filings available. Beyond named suppliers, Emaar's ESG disclosure references a "Supplier Quality Assurance" programme and embeds Dubai Green Building Regulations into all main contract works tenders, implying a competitive tender process across multiple contractors rather than a sole-source model. No percentage of COGS sourced from a single contractor or from the top-3 contractors is disclosed in the available data pool — this figure cannot be stated without inventing a number. The nearest available proxy is the homebuilding COGS / housing sales ratio, which was 50.8% in FY2025 (AED 18,966M cost on AED 37,347M housing sales, Capital IQ Annual Industry Specific data, FY2025), compared to 52% gross margin reported by management for UAE development overall (FY2025 Preliminary Annual Report). The recurring businesses (malls, hospitality) carry much lower input-cost intensity: Emaar Malls delivered an 89% gross margin in FY2025, reflecting minimal consumable input costs once the physical asset is built. The principal supply-side input risk is therefore concentrated in the development segment's construction cost line, where Dubai's building boom creates periodic tightness in skilled labour and key materials (steel, concrete, façade systems), but no single contractor or material has been flagged as a sole-source dependency in the available disclosures.

## 3. Customer Pricing Power

In residential development, Emaar has demonstrated sustained ability to sell units at rising nominal values without visible volume deterioration. UAE property sales rose from AED 65.4 bn in FY2024 to AED 71.1 bn in FY2025 (+9%) on unit sales of 13,905 vs 11,293 (+23%), which implies average price per unit moved down modestly in absolute terms due to mix shift (more apartments vs villas) but the gross margin on the UAE development segment remained 50% at the gross profit line, suggesting Emaar was not conceding margin to move volume. Across the 2023-2025 launch cohort, project-level gross margins achieved range from 40% to 61% by project [FY2025 Preliminary Annual Report, Feb-12-2026, Slide — revenue backlog by project table]. In the mall portfolio, base rent escalates annually under lease terms, and tenant turnover-based rent resets the base rent upward when tenant sales exceed the threshold; malls revenue grew from AED 5.10 bn in FY2024 to AED 5.75 bn in FY2025 (+13%) on essentially stable GLA, indicating effective per-sqft rent growth without a meaningful decline in occupancy (Dubai Mall remains >98% occupied) [FY2025 Preliminary Annual Report, Feb-12-2026, Slide 22-24]. In hospitality, RevPAR of AED 632 in FY2025 at 82% UAE occupancy was supported by Dubai's strong inbound tourism, but no specific year-on-year RevPAR change is quantified in the available data to show a rate increase in isolation from occupancy effects. The customer default rate in residential sales is c. 0.5% of sales value [FY2025 Preliminary Annual Report, Slide 16], an extremely low figure that confirms buyers are not walking away even at current price levels, pointing to genuine demand rather than price-induced suppression.

## 4. Economic Control Verdict

**Controls economics** — Emaar sets prices to suppliers (through competitive tender across multiple contractors), commands premium prices from buyers (rising property sales values with stable-to-improving margins, 0.5% buyer default rate), owns a captive land bank that removes spot-land cost exposure, and runs a retail and hospitality portfolio where it is the landlord setting rent terms across a fragmented, replaceable tenant base. The company's gross margin (54.9% LTM to March 2026 [Capital IQ, LTM Mar-31-2026 Income Statement]) is the highest in the listed GCC developer peer group and nearly 20 percentage points above the next-closest comp in the comparable analysis set. The pre-sale model for development locks in revenue before most construction spend is incurred, inverting the normal cost-pass-through problem: Emaar prices first and builds second, rather than building first and pricing at completion.

---

## 5. The Single Biggest Bargaining Risk

Construction contractor pricing and labour availability — if Dubai's development boom intensifies cost inflation, Emaar cannot rewrite already-signed buyer contracts to pass through cost overruns, which would compress margins on units already sold but not yet delivered (AED 134.3 bn backlog, to be recognised over 5-6 years [FY2025 Preliminary Annual Report, Feb-12-2026, Slide 16]).
