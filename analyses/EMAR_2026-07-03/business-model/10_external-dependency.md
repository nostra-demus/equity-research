## 1. Dependency Table

| External Variable | Dependency Level | Why It Matters | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| UAE / Dubai property cycle | **High** | UAE Development is 73% of group revenue and 66% of group EBITDA (FY2025: AED 36.4 bn revenue, AED 16.7 bn EBITDA). Revenue is deferred via IFRS 15 percentage-of-completion accounting, so a Dubai property downturn hits both new sales and backlog recognition rates. The 2020 COVID shock compressed group revenue from AED 27.9 bn (FY2019) to AED 18.4 bn (FY2020) — a 34% drop — driven entirely by development slowdown. Buyer demand is tied to Dubai's attractiveness as a global city for UHNW/HNW purchasers, oil-economy-linked GCC buyers, and expat residents; none of these flows is within management's control. | FY2025 Investor Presentation, slide 13 (Segment Analysis); FY2024 Annual Report (IFRS), Geographic Segments Note 3, p.165; revenue chart slides 7 and 15 |
| Government policy / ruler-directed development | **High** | Dubai's development pipeline is co-ordinated with the Dubai government at a structural level. Major land deals, new master-plan approvals, tourism targets, and infrastructure spend (metro expansions, Al Maktoum Airport expansion, Dubai Square Creek Harbour designation) that create demand for Emaar projects are all government-set. Emaar's largest JVs are with government-related entities (Dubai Holding, Dubai Aviation City Corporation, Mina Rashid Properties). The 2024 UAE corporate tax introduction and the 2025 DMTT (domestic top-up tax to 15%) — both outside management control — reduced reported net profit margins. Any further regulatory change (property transfer duty, foreign ownership restrictions, visa regime shifts) could alter demand immediately. | FY2025 Investor Presentation, slide 12 (DMTT disclosure); Capital IQ Income Statement — effective tax rate moved from 1.5% (FY2023) to 12.3% (FY2024) to 13.0% (FY2025); Key Developments (CIQ export): government-linked JV structures |
| FX (EGP, INR, TRY, PKR vs. AED/USD) | **Mid** | International operations contributed AED 3.4 bn in revenue (7% of group, FY2025 geographic segment data) but hold AED 30.7 bn in international assets. Emaar Misr (Egypt, 73.79%-owned) is the largest international exposure. The EGP depreciated ~10.7% against the AED in FY2025 (AED 0.0746/EGP vs AED 0.0835/EGP at end-FY2024), causing a forex loss at Emaar Misr of AED ~170 mn in FY2025 (versus a gain of AED ~650 mn in FY2024) — a AED 820 mn swing in a single sub-segment. India (INR loans AED 1.7 bn, INR-priced assets), Turkey (TRY), and Pakistan (PKR) add smaller but present currency mismatches. Management does not hedge transaction FX systematically; the exposure is accepted operationally. | FY2025 Investor Presentation, slide 21 (Emaar Misr FX rates and forex loss disclosure); Capital IQ Income Statement (FX gain/loss line); Capital Structure Details — INR-denominated loans AED 1.7 bn, EGP-denominated revolvers AED 603 mn |
| Interest rates | **Low** | Emaar is a net creditor, not a net borrower. At end-FY2025: total debt AED 9.8 bn vs. cash + short-term deposits of AED 71.5 bn (net cash AED 61.7 bn). Interest income was AED 2.5 bn and interest expense was AED 492 mn in FY2025 — a net interest income of AED 2.0 bn. The two outstanding sukuk (USD 500 mn at 3.64% due Sep-2026; USD 500 mn at 3.875% due 2029; USD 499.9 mn at 3.7% due 2031) are fixed-rate. The only floating-rate exposure is a small AED revolving credit facility (EIBOR + 1.00%, AED 3.7 mn drawn) and a Pakistani Rupee facility (KIBOR-linked, AED 430.8 mn). Rising rates hurt Emaar primarily if they slow mortgage availability for UAE buyers — a second-order effect, not a direct P&L hit. Rate cuts would reduce interest income on AED ~52 bn of cash, but this is a small proportion of AED 25.6 bn EBITDA. | FY2025 Investor Presentation, slides 8 and 12 (debt/cash profile); Capital IQ Capital Structure Details (sukuk fixed coupons, floating-rate facilities); Capital IQ Income Statement (interest income AED 2,505 mn vs expense AED 492 mn, FY2025) |
| Commodity prices (steel, cement, aluminium) | **Mid** | Emaar is a developer, not a contractor; it passes construction costs to main contractors via fixed-price contracts for many projects. However, material cost inflation (steel, cement) still flows through because cost-plus contracts or re-priced tenders are common in UAE. Emaar pre-sells ~93% of projects under development by value before completion, so margins are fixed at launch. Cost overruns on construction compress the gross margin achieved. The company disclosed an average gross margin of 46–47% on projects under development as of end-FY2025. A sustained 20% rise in construction costs would erode delivered project margins, but would take several years to fully materialise given the backlog recognition structure. No explicit commodity sensitivity figure is disclosed. | FY2025 Investor Presentation, slide 19 (Projects Under Development, "Gross Margin Achieved" of 46%); Inference — standard developer cost-pass-through mechanism; no direct sensitivity disclosure in available filings |
| Regulation / planning approvals | **Mid** | Emaar's entire master-plan pipeline depends on Dubai Land Department (DLD) and relevant authorities granting timely plot registrations, permit approvals, and height/GFA consents. A planning freeze or density restriction would constrain new supply that feeds future sales. The AED 200 bn new urban district announced June 2026, the Dubai Square Creek Harbour designation (AED 180 bn master project), and Dubai Mall Grand Drive expansion all require continuing DLD alignment. RERA regulations govern escrow requirements (buyers' instalments must be held in ring-fenced project escrow accounts, reducing default risk for buyers but constraining cash availability — AED ~43 bn of Emaar's AED 52.6 bn cash is in project escrow at end-FY2025). Regulatory disruption in Egypt (CASE:EMFD operations) and other international markets adds a smaller layer. | FY2025 Investor Presentation, slide 14 (escrow disclosure: AED ~43 bn of cash under escrow); Key Developments (CIQ): June 2026 AED 200 bn master-plan announcement; FY2025 Investor Presentation, slide 12 (DMTT impact on net profit) |
| Consumer cycle (UHNW / HNW buyer sentiment) | **High** | Dubai luxury and premium residential demand is structurally discretionary. The buyer base is global — Russian, Indian, Chinese, GCC, European UHNW buyers — and highly sensitive to global equity wealth, oil prices (for GCC buyers), and geopolitical safe-haven demand. Emaar's unit sales pricing has risen sharply (property sales value +16% to AED 80.4 bn in FY2025). Customer default rate is disclosed as ~0.5% of sales value (very low), but this reflects a buoyant cycle. In a downturn, default rates rise and new sales freeze. COVID 2020 demonstrated this: FY2020 revenue dropped 34% from FY2019 levels. Management cannot control the demand cycle; it can only manage the pace of launches. | FY2025 Investor Presentation, slides 16–17 (UAE Development highlights: property sales AED 71.1 bn, customer default rate ~0.5%); Revenue time series, slide 7 (FY2020 COVID trough) |
| Industrial / construction cycle | **Low** | Emaar is a developer, not a construction contractor or an industrial company. It does not rely on industrial throughput for its own revenues. Contractor capacity tightness (labour, materials) can delay project delivery schedules, which delays revenue recognition under percentage-of-completion accounting — an indirect rather than direct external variable. Not scored separately from commodity prices; subsumed above. | Inference, not from filings |
| Geopolitics | **Mid** | The Middle East as a region is geopolitically active. The UAE benefits from its neutral positioning but is not immune. Disruption to regional air routes, Gulf tensions (Iran, Houthi), or destabilisation of key buyer markets (Russia/Ukraine impact on Russian buyers 2022; Egyptian political economy) can reduce demand. Emaar has operations in Syria (The Eighth Gate in Damascus — May 2026: transitioning from JV to solo operation as Syria's situation shifts), Egypt, Turkey, Pakistan, India, and Lebanon. The share of revenue from these markets is small (~7% geographic revenue), but asset exposure is AED 30.7 bn internationally. Dubai's hospitality (occupancy 82%, RevPAR AED 632 in FY2025) relies on ongoing regional and global air connectivity. | FY2025 Investor Presentation, slides 20–21 (international market exposure, Emaar Misr); Key Developments (CIQ): May 2026 Syria JV exit; FY2025 Investor Presentation, slide 32 (Hospitality occupancy/RevPAR) |

## 3. Classification

**Partly externally driven** — Emaar has real management levers (land bank sequencing, project launch timing, pricing strategy, recurring asset mix, geographic diversification, pre-sales discipline) but its core business — selling Dubai real estate — is riding a demand wave that is fundamentally set by the Dubai property cycle, global wealth flows into Dubai, and UAE government-directed urban expansion. A sustained reversal in any one of these would hit results materially regardless of management execution quality.

---

## 4. External Dependency Risk Score

**52 / 100** (higher = worse; more external dependence)

**Band: Material external exposure, mixed mitigation (41–60)**

Reasoning:
- The single biggest exposure — Dubai property demand cycle — is genuinely outside management control and accounts for 73% of revenue. This alone would put the score close to 60.
- Three significant mitigants pull the score below 60: (a) the AED 154.8 bn global revenue backlog (AED 134.3 bn UAE) locks in revenues 5–6 years forward, softening near-term cycle sensitivity; (b) the AED 61.7 bn net cash position means the company can survive a significant demand drought without refinancing risk; (c) recurring businesses (Malls, Hospitality, Entertainment) contribute 32% of EBITDA at structurally stable, contractually protected margins — providing a counter-cyclical floor.
- FX exposure at Emaar Misr and international markets is a real but contained risk given international revenues are only 7% of the total.
- Interest rates are not a meaningful direct risk given the net cash structure and predominantly fixed-rate debt.
- Government/regulatory dependency is real but runs in Emaar's favour in the current environment (government-led demand creation, Expo site developments, tourist visa reforms).

---

## 5. The Single Biggest Lever

**A 20% adverse move in Dubai luxury/premium residential demand** — if UAE property buyer volumes dropped 20%, the number of new units sold would fall sharply, Emaar's backlog accretion would slow, and revenue recognition from percentage-of-completion accounting would compress for 2–3 years until the existing backlog (AED 134.3 bn) is worked through. This is exactly the pattern seen in 2020 and is the one variable that directly threatens 73% of group revenue and 66% of EBITDA simultaneously.
